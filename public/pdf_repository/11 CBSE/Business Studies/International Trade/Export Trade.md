# 10.4 EXPORT TRADE 

## Meaning of Export Trade

Export trade refers to selling of goods and services by a firm of home country to a firm of foreign country. For example, sale of sweets by Haldiram to WalMart Store in USA. The seller of such goods and services is referred to as an 'exporter' and the overseas based buyer is referred to as an 'importer'.

## Objectives of Export Trade

The main objectives of export trade are as follows:

1. To sell surplus goods: A country may produce a commodity in such quantities that are more than its needs. Export trade is undertaken to make fruitful use of such surplus goods.
2. To make better use of resources: Export trade widens the market for goods. A country can make better use of its resources by producing goods on a large scale both for domestic use and exports.
3. To earn foreign exchange: A country aims to earn valuable foreign exchange through exports.
4. To increase national income: Export trade helps a country increase its national income. Increase in national income improves the quality of life of the people.
5. To generate employment: The growth of export trade creates employment opportunities for a large number of people.
6. To increase government revenue: Export trade generates revenue for the government in the form of custom duty and excise duty. The government can spend more on public welfare activities.
7. To create international cooperation: Export trade improves mutual understanding and cooperation between nations.

## Procedure of Export Trade

Exporting goods to foreign countries is quite different from producing and marketing goods in the domestic market. A number of procedural formalities are to be complied with before goods are actually shipped to foreign destinations. The various steps involved in exporting goods are as follows:

1. Receipt of Enquiry and Sending Quotations: First of all, the prospective buyer sends an enquiry to different exporters to seek information about availability of goods, price, quality and terms and conditions for export of goods. The enquiry can also be conducted through an advertisement.
In response to the enquiry, the exporter sends a reply in the form of a quotation known as 'Proforma Invoice'. The proforma invoice contains detailed information regarding price, quality, grade, size, weight and all other relevant conditions regarding sale.
It must be noted that exporter should furnish all possible details about the product in order to avoid any misunderstanding or complaint by the importer.

2. Receipt of Order or Indent: If the prospective buyer is satisfied with the quotations sent through proforma invoice, then he places an order for the goods to be despatched. This order is known as 'Indent'. Indent contains a description of the goods ordered, prices to be paid, delivery terms, packing and marking details and delivery instructions.
3. Assessing Importer's Credit-worthiness and securing a Guarantee for Payments: After receiving the order, the exporter makes necessary enquiry about the creditworthiness of the importer in order to secure the payment. To minimise the risk of non-payment, exporters generally demand a 'Letter of Credit' from the importer. A letter of credit is a guarantee issued by the importer's bank that it will honour payment up to a certain amount of export bill to the bank of the exporter.
Letter of credit is considered as the most appropriate method to secure payment in international transactions.
4. Obtaining Export License: When the exporter is assured about the Payment, he initiates the steps to fulfill export formalities. In India, the exporter is required to obtain 'Export License' before exporting the goods.
The pre-requisites for getting an export license are as follows:
(i) Opening a Bank Account: The exporter has to open a bank account in any bank authorised by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) to deal in foreign exchange.
(ii) Obtaining Import Export Code (IEC) number: The exporter needs IEC number to fill it in various export/import documents. To get the IEC number, exporter has to apply to Regional Import Export Licensing Authority in the prescribed form, which should be supported by the following documents:
(a) Full Profile of the exporter.
(b) Bank Receipt for payment of the fee.
(c) Certificate from the Banker of the applicant firm on the prescribed form.
(d) Two copies of photographs attested by the banker to the applicant.
(e) Declaration by the exporters that they are not associated with a caution listed firm. (Caution list contains the list of goods on which there is restriction on exports).
(f) Detail of non-resident interest, if there is any such interest in the exporter's firm.
(iii) Obtaining RCMC Certificate from Export Promotion Council: It is necessary for the exporter to become a member of an appropriate Export Promotion Council (like Engineering Export Promotion Council (EEPC) and Apparel Export Promotion Council (AEPC)) and obtain a 'Registration cum Membership Certificate (RCMC)' for availing benefits available to export firms from the Government.
(iv) Registering with Export Credit and Guarantee Corporation (ECGC): Registration with ECGC is necessary to protect overseas payments from political and commercial risks. Such registration also helps the exporter in getting financial assistance from commercial banks and other financial institutions.

5. Obtaining Pre-shipment Finance: After receiving the letter of credit, the exporter approaches his banker for pre-shipment finance. Pre-shipment finance is the finance that the exporter needs for procuring raw materials and other components, processing and packing of goods and transportation of goods to the port of shipment.
6. Production or Procurement of Goods: After getting the pre-shipment finance, the exporter starts manufacturing the goods. In case the exporter is not a manufacturer, then he procures the goods from the market. The goods so produced or procured should confirm to the specifications given in the export order.
7. Pre-shipment Inspection: In order to ensure that only good quality products are exported, the Indian Government has passed Export Quality Control and Inspection Act, 1963.

- The basic aim is to make compulsory inspection of certain category of products. If goods fall under this category, then exporter applies to Export Inspection Agency (EIA).
- If the goods confirm to the prescribed specifications, an 'Inspection Certificate' is issued. This certificate is to be submitted along with other export documents at the time of exports.
Inspection is not compulsory for: (i) Star Trading Houses; (ii) Trading Houses; (iii) Export Houses; (iv) Industrial units setup in Export Processing Zones (EPZs) or Special Economic Zones (SEZs); (v) 100\% Export Oriented Units (EOUs).
For your reference: Products having ISI Certification mark or Agmark are not required to be inspected by any agency.

8. Excise Clearance: According to Central Excise Tariff Act, exporter is required to pay excise duty on certain excisable goods and to obtain 'Excise Clearance Certificate' for the same. For the certificate, he has to apply to the concerned Excise Commissioner with an invoice. When the commissioner is satisfied, he issues the excise clearance.
However, in many cases, exported goods are exempted from the excise duty. If the duty is already paid, then it is refunded back to the exporter in order to make export products more competitive. Such refund of duty is known as 'Duty Drawback'. For the refund, the exporter has to apply to the Commissioner of Customs or Central Excise Incharge.
For your knowledge: The duty drawback scheme is administered by Directorate of Drawback under the Ministry of Finance and is responsible to fix duty drawback rates for different products.
9. Obtaining Certificate of Origin: Some importing countries provide tariff concessions or other exemptions to the goods coming from a particular country. In order to obtain such benefits, the importer may ask the exporter to send a 'Certificate of Origin'. This certificate testifies the origin of goods exported. It can be obtained from the Trade Consulate located in the exporter's country.
10. Reservation of Shipping Space: The exporting firm applies to the shipping company for reservation of shipping space.

- He has to provide complete details regarding type of goods, expected date of shipment and port of destination.

- On accepting the application, the shipping company issues a 'Shipping Order'.
- Shipping order is an instruction to the captain of the ship to receive the specified goods on board after clearance from the customs.

11. Packing and Forwarding: The goods are then properly packed and marked with necessary details, like name and address of importer, gross and net weight, port of shipment and destination, etc.

- Then exporter makes necessary arrangement to transport goods to the port by road or rail. In case goods are loaded into the railway wagon, a 'Railway Receipt' is issued, which serves as a title to the goods.
- This receipt is endorsed in favour of the agent to enable him to take the delivery of goods at the port of shipment.

12. Insurance of Goods: In order to safeguard loss of goods on account of perils of the sea, the goods are insured with an insurance company and insurance policy is collected by the exporter.
13. Customs Clearance: Goods can be loaded on the ship only when they get custom clearance. To obtain the custom clearance, the exporter prepares the 'Shipping Bill'. Shipping bill contains details regarding goods to be exported, name of the vessel, port where goods are to be discharged, destination country, exporter's name and address, etc.
Five copies of the shipping bill along with the following documents are submitted to the Customs Appraiser at the Customs House:
(a) Export Contract or Export Order
(b) Letter of Credit
(c) Commercial Invoice
(d) Certificate of Origin
(e) Certificate of Inspection (if needed)
(f) Marine Insurance Policy

After submitting these documents, the superintendent is approached to obtain 'Carting Order'. Carter order instructs the staff at the port's gate to permit cargo's entry inside the dock. After obtaining the carting order, the cargo is physically moved into the port area and stored in the appropriate shed.
Exporter can hire a 'Clearing and Forwarding (C\&F) agent' if he does not have time for performing all these formalities.
14. Obtaining Mate's Receipt: After getting the custom clearance, the goods are loaded on ship for which the captain of the ship or his mate (i.e., his assistant) issues a receipt to the port superintendent, known as 'Mate's Receipt'.

- It contains details regarding name of the vessel, berth, date of shipment, description of packages, marks and numbers, etc.
- The port superintendent hands over the mate's receipt to the C\&F agent or exporter after receiving the port dues.

15. Payment of Freight and Issuance of Bill of Lading: After getting the mate's receipt, the exporter or C\&F agent surrenders the mate's receipt to the shipping company for computation of freight.

- After shipping company receives the freight, it issues a 'Bill of Lading', which serves as an evidence that the shipping company has accepted to carry the goods to a particular destination.
- In case, goods are being sent by air, this document is referred to as airway bill. If the freight has to be paid by the importer, then the bill of lading is marked as 'Freight Forward'.

16. Preparation of Invoice: After sending the goods, the exporter prepares an invoice for the goods exported. The invoice will contain details about the quantity of goods sent and the amount to be paid by the importer. The C\&F agent gets it duly attested by the customs.
17. Securing Payment: After the shipment, the exporter informs the importer and sends following documents so that importer can claim title of goods: (a) Certified copy of Invoice; (b) Bill of Lading; (c) Packing List; (d) Insurance Policy; (e) Certificate of Origin; (f) Letter of Credit.
These documents are sent through the exporter's banker with the instruction that the documents be delivered to importer only when he accepts the 'Bill of Exchange', which is also sent along with the above mentioned documents.
Bill of Exchange is an order to the importer to pay a certain amount of money to, or to the order of, a certain person or to the bearer of the instrument.
Bill of exchange can be of two types:
(i) Document against sight (Sight draft): In this case, documents are handed over only against payment, i.e. importer releases the payment when relevant documents are delivered.
(ii) Document against acceptance (Usance draft): In this case, documents are delivered to the importer when he accepts the bill of exchange and agrees to make payment on maturity.
The exporter's bank receives the payment through the importer's bank either immediately (in case of sight draft) or on maturity (in case of usance draft). This amount is credited to the exporter's account.
In case of usance draft, the exporter can also get immediate payment from his bank after submitting documents and signing a 'Letter of Indemnity' (letter states that exporter undertakes to indemnify the bank if payment is not received from the importer, along with accrued interest).
After receiving the payment for exports, the exporter needs to get a 'Bank Certificate of Payment', which says that necessary documents relating to the export have been presented to the importer for payment and the payment has been received in accordance with the exchange control regulations.


## Documents used in Export Transactions

The documents required in exports transactions can be broadly classified into three categories:

1. Documents related to Goods
2. Documents related to Shipment
3. Documents related to Payment

### Documents related to Goods

The following documents related to goods are needed in export of goods:

1. Export Invoice: It is the bill issued by the exporter to provide information such as quantity of goods sent, total value of goods, number of packages, marks on packing, port of destination, etc.
2. Packing List: It is a statement, which indicates the number of cases or packs and the details of the goods contained in these packs. It also gives details of the nature of goods which are being exported and the form in which these are being sent.
3. Certificate of Origin: This certificate specifies the country in which the goods are being produced. It is needed by the importer as some importing countries provide tariff concessions or other exemptions on the basis of this certificate. This certificate is also required when there is a ban on imports of certain goods from select countries.
4. Certificate of Inspection: In order to ensure that only good quality products are exported, the government has made it compulsory) for certain products be inspected by some authorised agency. Export Inspection Council of India (EICI) is one such agency, which carries out the inspection and issues a 'Certificate of Inspection' if goods confirm to the prescribed specifications.

### Documents related to Shipment

1. Mate's Receipt: Mate's receipt is the receipt issued by the commanding officer of the ship to the exporter after the cargo is loaded on the ship. It contains details regarding name of the vessel, berth, date of shipment, description of packages, marks and numbers, etc. Mate's receipt is an important receipt as shipping company issues the bill of lading only after getting this receipt.

2. Shipping Bill: A shipping bill is the main document on the basis of which customs office grants permission for the export. It contains details regarding goods to be exported, name of the vessel, port where goods are to be discharged, destination country, exporter's name and address, etc.
3. Shipping Order: Shipping order is an instruction to the captain of the ship to receive the specified goods on board after clearance from the customs.

- It is an inventory control document that identifies the confirmed space booking, the goods to be shipped from the warehouse and the place to which the goods must be shipped.
- A shipping order generally contains the order number and date, names and addresses of the shipper, customs broker or forwarder, shipping and receipt dates, a customer purchase order number, vessel and voyage number, sailing time, delivery date and location, customs closing date and number and type of packages.
- The shipping order accompanies the shipment, so that the recipient can verify and sign that the items listed were received.

4. Bill of Lading: It is a document prepared by the shipping company to acknowledge the receipt of goods on board the ship and gives an undertaking to carry them to the port of destination. It is also a document of title to the goods and is freely transferable by the endorsement and delivery.
5. Airway Bill: Like a bill of lading, an airway bill is a document, which is prepared by the airline company to acknowledge the receipt of goods on board its aircraft and gives an undertaking to carry them to the port of destination. It is also a document of title to the goods and is freely transferable by the endorsement and delivery.
Both, bill of lading and airway bill serve the same purpose. The only difference is that the bill of lading is issued by the shipping company (when goods are loaded on the ship), while the airway bill is issued by airline company (when goods are loaded on the aircraft).
6. Marine Insurance Policy: It is a certificate of insurance contract whereby the insurance company agrees to indemnify the insured against any loss caused due to perils of the sea in consideration of a payment called premium
7. Cart Ticket: Cart ticket is prepared by the exporter, which provides details regarding export cargo, like shipper's name, number of packages, shipping bill number, etc.
Cart ticket is also known as a 'Cart Chit', 'Vehicle Pass' or 'Gate Pass'.
8. Consular Invoice: It is a legal document prepared in the prescribed format and is signed or certified by the counsel of the importing country located in the country of export.

- It certifies the shipment of goods and shows information such as the consignor, consignee and value of the shipment.
- The consular invoice is required by some countries to facilitate customs and collection of taxes.

### Documents related to Payment 

1. Letter of Credit: Letter of credit is a guarantee issued by the importer's bank that it will honour payment up to a certain amount of export bills to the bank of the exporter. As stated before, it is the most appropriate and secure method of payment to settle international transactions.
2. Bill of Exchange: It is a written instrument whereby the person issuing the instrument directs the other party to pay a specified amount to a certain person or the bearer of the instrument. In an export-import transaction, it is drawn by the exporter on the importer. Importer gets the title to export consignment only when he accepts the order contained in the bill of exchange.
3. Bank Certificate of Payment: It certifies that the necessary documents (including bill of exchange) relating to the particular export consignment have been presented to the importer for payment and the payment has been received in accordance with the exchange control regulations.
