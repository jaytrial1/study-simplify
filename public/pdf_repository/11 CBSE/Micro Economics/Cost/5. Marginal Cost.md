# Marginal Cost

Marginal cost refers to the addition to total cost when one more unit of output is produced. For example, if TC of producing 2 units is ₹ 200 and TC of producing 3 units is ₹ 240, then $\mathrm{MC}=240-200=₹ 40$.

$$
\mathrm{MC}_{\mathrm{n}}=\mathrm{TC}_{\mathrm{n}}-\mathrm{TC}_{\mathrm{n}-1}
$$

Where:
$\mathrm{n}=$ Number of units produced
$\mathrm{MC}_{\mathrm{n}}=$ Marginal cost of the $\mathrm{n}^{\text {th }}$ unit
$\mathrm{TC}_{\mathrm{n}}=$ Total cost of n units
$\mathrm{TC}_{\mathrm{n}-1}=$ Total cost of $(\mathrm{n}-1)$ units.

## One More Way to Calculate MC

We know, MC is the change in TC when one more unit of output is produced. However, when the change in units produced is more than one, then MC can also be calculated as:

$$
\mathrm{MC}=\frac{\text { Change in Total Cost }}{\text { Change in units of Output }}=\frac{\Delta \mathrm{TC}}{\Delta \mathrm{Q}}
$$

If TC of producing 2 units is ₹ 200 and TC of producing 5 units is ₹ 350, then MC will be:

$$
\mathrm{MC}=\frac{\mathrm{TC} \text { of } 5 \text { units }-\mathrm{TC} \text { of } 2 \text { units }}{5 \text { units }-2 \text { units }}=\frac{350-200}{5-2}=₹ 50
$$

## MC is not affected by Fixed Costs

We know, MC is addition to TC when one more unit of output is produced. We also know, TC $=$ TFC + TVC. As TFC does not change with change in output, MC is independent of TFC and is affected only by change in TVC.
This can be explained with the help of a simple mathematical derivation:
We know:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{MC}_{\mathrm{n}} & =\mathrm{TC}_{\mathrm{n}}-\mathrm{TC}_{\mathrm{n}-1} \\
\mathrm{TC} & =\mathrm{TFC}+\mathrm{TVC}
\end{aligned}
$$

Putting the value of (2) in (1), we get

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{MC}_{\mathrm{n}} & =\left(\mathrm{TFC}_{\mathrm{n}}+\mathrm{TVC}_{\mathrm{n}}\right)-\left(\mathrm{TFC}_{\mathrm{n}-1}+\mathrm{TVC}_{\mathrm{n}-1}\right) \\
& =\mathrm{TFC}_{\mathrm{n}}+\mathrm{TVC}_{\mathrm{n}}-\mathrm{TFC}_{\mathrm{n}-1}-\mathrm{TVC}_{\mathrm{n}-1}=\mathrm{TFC}_{\mathrm{n}}-\mathrm{TFC}_{\mathrm{n}-1}+\mathrm{TVC}_{\mathrm{n}}-\mathrm{TVC}_{\mathrm{n}-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

Now, TFC is same at all levels of output, so $\mathrm{TFC}_{\mathrm{n}}=\mathrm{TFC}_{\mathrm{n}-1}$
It means, $\mathrm{TFC}_{\mathrm{n}}-\mathrm{TFC}_{\mathrm{n}-1}=0$
So, $\mathrm{MC}_{\mathrm{n}}=\mathrm{TVC}_{\mathrm{n}}-\mathrm{TVC}_{\mathrm{n}-1}$

As seen in Table 6.7, MC can be calculated from both TC and TVC. MC curve in Fig. 6.8 is obtained by plotting the points shown in Table 6.7. MC is a U-shaped curve due to Law of Variable Proportions (i.e. due to operations of returns to a factor input). Initially, units of the variable factor are employed with the fixed factor, which yield increasing returns and reduces MC. It pushes the MC curve down. Further addition of variable factors leads to diminishing returns to creep in, thereby increasing MC after reaching to its minimum level. As a result, MC curve falls to its minimum level and then rises. Therefore, the short-run marginal cost curve is U-shaped.

#### Overall Impression:
The graph illustrates the typical relationship between output and marginal cost. Marginal cost initially decreases due to increasing efficiency or economies of scale. However, as output continues to increase, diseconomies of scale or other factors lead to rising marginal costs. The minimum point on the curve represents the level of output where marginal cost is minimized.
