# Per Unit Costs

The per unit costs explain the relationship between cost and output in a more realistic manner. From total fixed cost (TFC), total variable cost (TVC) and total cost (TC), we can obtain per unit costs. The 3 kinds of 'per unit costs' are:

1.  Average Fixed Cost (AFC)
2.  Average Variable Cost (AVC)
3.  Average Total Cost (ATC) or Average Cost (AC)

## Average Fixed Cost (AFC)

Average fixed cost refers to the per unit fixed cost of production. It is calculated by dividing TFC by total output.

$$
\mathrm{AFC}=\frac{\mathrm{TFC}}{\mathrm{Q}}
$$

Where:
* AFC = Average fixed cost
* TFC = Total fixed cost
* \( Q= \) Quantity of output

AFC falls with increase in output as TFC remains same at all levels of output.

### Table 6.4: Average Fixed Cost

| Output (in units) | Total Fixed Cost or TFC (₹) | Average Fixed Cost or AFC (₹) <br> (TFC ÷ Output = AFC) |
| :-----------------: | :---------------------------: | :-------------------------------------------------------: |
|         0         |               12              |                       12 ÷ 0 = ∞                        |
|         1         |               12              |                       12 ÷ 1 = 12                       |
|         2         |               12              |                       12 ÷ 2 = 6                        |
|         3         |               12              |                       12 ÷ 3 = 4                        |
|         4         |               12              |                       12 ÷ 4 = 3                        |
|         5         |               12              |                      12 ÷ 5 = 2.40                      |

As seen in Table 6.4, AFC falls with rise in output because constant TFC is divided by increasing output. AFC curve in Fig. 6.4 is obtained by plotting the points shown in Table 6.4. AFC diminishes by the same proportion as that of the proportion of increase of the number of units and the product will always be same and equal to TFC. AFC keeps on falling as the level of output increases, but can never become zero. As a result, AFC curve is a 'Rectangular Hyperbola'. A rectangular hyperbola is a curve in which any rectangle formed under the curve has the same area. It means, area under AFC curve remains same at different points.

### AFC Does Not Touch Any of the Axes

As AFC is a rectangular hyperbola, it approaches both the axes. It gets nearer and nearer to the axes, but never touches them.

*   AFC can never touch the X-axis as TFC can never be zero.
*   AFC curve can never touch the Y-axis because at zero level of output, TFC is a positive value and any positive value divided by zero will be an infinite value.

# Average Variable Cost (AVC)

Average variable cost refers to the per-unit variable cost of production. It is calculated by dividing TVC by total output.

$$
A V C = \frac{T V C}{Q}
$$

(Where: $A V C=$ Average Variable cost; TVC = Total Variable cost; $Q=$ Quantity of output)

AVC initially falls with an increase in output. Once the output rises till the optimum level, AVC starts rising. It can be better understood with the help of Table 6.5 and Fig. 6.5.

# Average Total Cost (ATC) or Average Cost (AC)

Average cost refers to the per unit total cost of production. It is calculated by dividing TC by total output.

$$
\mathrm{AC}=\mathrm{TC} \div \mathrm{Q}
$$

(Where: $A C=$ Average cost; $T C=$ Total cost; $Q=$ Quantity of output)
Average cost is also defined as the sum of average fixed cost (AFC) and average variable cost (AVC), i.e. $A C=A F C+A V C$
Like AVC, average cost also initially falls with increase in output. Once the output rises till optimum level, AC starts rising. It can be better understood with the help of Table 6.6 and Fig. 6.6.

## Table 6.6: Average Cost

| Output <br> (in Units) | AFC <br> (₹) | AVC <br> (₹) | $A C(₹)$ <br> $A F C+A V C=A C$ |
| :---------------------: | :------------: | :------------: | :------------------------------: |
|            0            |    $\infty$    |       -        |                -               |
|            1            |       12       |       6        |             $12+6=18$            |
|            2            |       6        |       5        |             $6+5=11$             |
|            3            |       4        |       5        |             $4+5=9$              |
|            4            |       3        |       6        |             $3+6=9$              |
|            5            |      2.40      |       7        |           $2.40+7=9.40$          |

As seen in Table 6.6, AC is calculated by adding AFC and AVC. As seen in Fig. 6.6, AC curve a U-shaped curve. It means AC initially falls ($1^{\text {st }}$ phase), and after reaching its minimum point ($2^{\text {nd }}$ phase), it starts rising ($3^{\text {rd }}$ phase).
Let us understand the three phases of $A C$:
$1^{\text {st }}$ Phase: When both AFC and AVC fall till the level of 2 units of output, AC also falls i.e. till point A.
$2^{\text {nd }}$ Phase: From 2 units to 3 units, AFC continues to fall, but AVC remains constant. So, AC falls (due to falling AFC) till it reaches its minimum point ' B '. From 3 units to 4 units, fall in AFC (by ₹ 1) is equal to rise in AVC (by ₹ 1). So, AC remains constant.

## Important Observations: AC, AVC and AFC

1.  AC curve will always lie above the AVC curve (See Fig. 6.7) because AC, at all levels of output includes both AVC and AFC.
2.  AVC reaches its minimum point (point ' B ') at a level of output lower than that of AC (point ' A ') because when AVC is at its minimum point, $A C$ is still falling because of falling AFC.
