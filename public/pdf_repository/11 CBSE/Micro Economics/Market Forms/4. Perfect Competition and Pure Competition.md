# Perfect Competition

Perfect Competition is used in a wider sense compared to Pure Competition. Competition is considered 'Pure Competition' when these 3 fundamental conditions are met:

1.  Very Large number of buyers and sellers;
2.  Homogeneous product;
3.  Freedom of entry and exit.

Perfect competition is a broader concept. For a market to be perfectly competitive, in addition to the three fundamental conditions, these four additional conditions must be satisfied:

1.  Perfect Knowledge among buyers and sellers;
2.  Perfect mobility of factors of production;
3.  Absence of transportation costs;
4.  Absence of selling costs.

## Firm is a Price-taker

Price taker means that an individual firm has no option but to sell at a price determined by the industry. Under perfect competition, an individual firm cannot influence the price on its own as its share in total market supply is negligible.

## Firm Vs Industry

'Firm' is a single unit that produces or provides goods and services. On the other hand, 'Industry' is an aggregate of all the firms producing the same commodity. For example, firms manufacturing chocolates will be collectively called the chocolate industry. So, a firm is just a part of an industry.

**Interpretation**

The graphs highlight the difference in market power:

*   **Price Maker:** The industry demand and supply forces interact to determine the market price. An individual firm's output decisions affect the overall market supply and, therefore, the price.

*   **Price Taker:** The firm is small relative to the overall market. It cannot influence the market price. Instead, it takes the market price (determined by the industry's demand and supply) as given. This means the firm can sell any quantity at the prevailing market price, so its average revenue (AR) and marginal revenue (MR) are constant and equal to that price.

In summary, the image effectively illustrates how a firm's pricing power differs based on its competitive environment (price maker vs. price taker).

Therefore, a firm plays no role in price determination. It can affect neither the supply nor the demand in the market. So, 'Firm is a price-taker and Industry is the Price-maker'. Each firm has to accept the price as determined by market forces of demand and supply. Price is determined at the point where the market demand curve intersects the market supply curve.

In Fig 10.1, the market demand curve DD and market supply curve SS intersect at point E, at which OP price is determined. The price of OP is adopted by the price-taker firm, and the firm is free to sell any quantity ($O Q, O Q_{1}$ or any other quantity) at this price. This makes the AR curve perfectly elastic and thus parallel to the X-axis. According to the AR and MR relationship, when AR is constant, $\mathrm{MR}=\mathrm{AR}$. So, the AR curve is also the MR curve of the firm.

**For your Reference:** In the long run, every perfectly competitive firm aims to produce that level of output at which there is a minimum average cost.
