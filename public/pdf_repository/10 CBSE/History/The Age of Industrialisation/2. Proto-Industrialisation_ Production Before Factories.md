# Production Before Factories

Industrialisation is often solely associated with the growth of factory industries, leading to the assumption that industrial production and workers are synonymous with factories. However, a significant phase of industrial production existed even before factories became common in England and Europe. This large-scale production was for an international market but was not based on factories. Historians refer to this phase as *proto-industrialisation*, where *proto-* indicates the first or early form of something.

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, merchants from European towns began to move into the countryside. They supplied money to peasants and artisans, persuading them to produce goods for an international market. The growing demand for goods, driven by the expansion of world trade and the acquisition of colonies, could not be met by urban production. This was due to the powerful urban crafts and trade guilds. These guilds were associations of producers that:
*   Trained craftspeople.
*   Maintained control over production.
*   Regulated competition and prices.
*   Restricted the entry of new people into the trade.
Rulers often granted these guilds monopoly rights to produce and trade specific products, making it difficult for new merchants to establish businesses in towns. Consequently, merchants sought out producers in the countryside.

In the countryside, poor peasants and artisans eagerly agreed to work for merchants. At this time, open fields were disappearing, and common lands were being enclosed, forcing cottagers and poor peasants to seek alternative income sources. Many had tiny plots of land insufficient for their entire household's labour. Working for merchants allowed them to remain in the countryside, continue cultivating their small plots, and supplement their shrinking agricultural income. It also ensured fuller use of their family labour resources.

This proto-industrial system fostered a close relationship between towns and the countryside: merchants were based in towns, but most of the work was carried out in rural areas. For example, a merchant clothier in England would:
*   Purchase wool from a *wool stapler* (a person who sorts wool according to its fibre).
*   Carry it to spinners.
*   Take the spun yarn to weavers, then to *fullers* (a person who 'fulls' – gathers – cloth by pleating), and finally to dyers.
*   The finishing of the cloth was often done in London before the export merchant sold it in the international market, leading to London becoming known as a finishing centre.

This system was a vast network of commercial exchanges, controlled by merchants, with goods produced by a large number of producers working within their family farms, not in factories. Each merchant often employed 20 to 25 workers at each stage of production, meaning a single clothier could control hundreds of workers.
