# Write in brief

1.  Explain the following:
    a)  Women workers in Britain attacked the Spinning Jenny.
    b)  In the seventeenth century merchants from towns in Europe began employing peasants and artisans within the villages.
    c)  The port of Surat declined by the end of the eighteenth century.
    d)  The East India Company appointed gomasthas to supervise weavers in India.

> **Answer:**
> a)  **Women workers in Britain attacked the Spinning Jenny** because this new machine, designed by James Hargreaves, significantly sped up the spinning process and drastically reduced the demand for manual labour. Many women relied on hand spinning for their livelihoods, and the introduction of the Spinning Jenny directly threatened their jobs and income, leading to widespread fear of unemployment and hostility towards the machines.
> b)  **In the seventeenth century, merchants from towns in Europe began employing peasants and artisans within the villages** because urban craft and trade guilds in towns were powerful and restricted production. These guilds maintained monopolies, controlled prices, and limited the entry of new merchants. To meet the growing demand for goods from expanding world trade and colonies, merchants turned to the countryside, where poor peasants and artisans, facing shrinking land resources, eagerly accepted advances to produce goods, allowing them to supplement their income and utilize family labour.
> c)  **The port of Surat declined by the end of the eighteenth century** primarily due to the increasing power and monopoly rights asserted by European trading companies, particularly the East India Company. As European companies gained control over trade, they shifted focus to new ports like Bombay and Calcutta, leading to a dramatic fall in exports from Surat, the drying up of credit that financed local trade, and the bankruptcy of local bankers.
> d)  **The East India Company appointed gomasthas to supervise weavers in India** as part of their strategy to establish direct control over the weavers, eliminate competition, control costs, and ensure regular supplies of cotton and silk goods. The gomasthas were paid servants tasked with supervising weavers, collecting supplies, examining cloth quality, and preventing weavers from dealing with other buyers, often through a system of loans that tied weavers to the Company.

2.  Write True or False against each statement:
    a)  At the end of the nineteenth century, 80 per cent of the total workforce in Europe was employed in the technologically advanced industrial sector.
    b)  The international market for fine textiles was dominated by India till the eighteenth century.
    c)  The American Civil War resulted in the reduction of cotton exports from India.
    d)  The introduction of the fly shuttle enabled handloom workers to improve their productivity.

> **Answer:**
> a)  **False.** At the end of the nineteenth century, less than 20 per cent of the total workforce was employed in technologically advanced industrial sectors. Traditional industries and domestic units still predominated.
> b)  **True.** Before the age of machine industries, silk and cotton goods from India, particularly the finer varieties, dominated the international textile market.
> c)  **False.** The American Civil War *increased* cotton exports from India. When cotton supplies from the US were cut off due to the war, Britain turned to India for raw cotton, causing its exports to surge and prices to shoot up.
> d)  **True.** The fly shuttle was a key innovation that increased productivity per worker, sped up production, and reduced labour demand, allowing weavers to operate larger looms and weave wider pieces of cloth.

3.  Explain what is meant by proto-industrialisation.

> **Answer:**
> *Proto-industrialisation* refers to the phase of industrial production that existed in Europe and other parts of the world *before* the establishment of large-scale factory industries. In this system, merchants from towns would move to the countryside, supplying money and raw materials to peasants and artisans. These rural workers, often working from their homes or small workshops using traditional methods, would produce goods for an international market. This system allowed for large-scale production without centralized factories, utilizing abundant rural labour and supplementing the income of peasant households.

# Discuss

1.  Why did some industrialists in nineteenth-century Europe prefer hand labour over machines?

> **Answer:**
> Some industrialists in nineteenth-century Europe preferred hand labour over machines for several reasons:
> *   **Abundance of Labour:** There was no shortage of human labour in Victorian Britain, with poor peasants and vagrants migrating to cities in large numbers in search of jobs. This abundance kept wages low, making human labour a cheap alternative to expensive machinery.
> *   **High Cost of Machines:** New technology was expensive to purchase, set up, and maintain. Industrialists were cautious about making large capital investments in machines.
> *   **Breakdowns and Repairs:** Machines often broke down, and their repairs were costly. Their effectiveness was also sometimes overstated by inventors and manufacturers.
> *   **Seasonal Demand:** Many industries experienced seasonal fluctuations in demand (e.g., gas works, breweries, bookbinders). For such industries, it was more economical to employ workers for the season rather than investing in machinery that would sit idle for part of the year.
> *   **Specific Product Requirements:** A range of products, especially those with intricate designs, specific shapes, or fine finishes (like 500 varieties of hammers or luxury goods for the upper classes), could only be produced with skilled hand labour. Machines were typically geared towards uniform, standardised goods for a mass market.
> *   **Social Preference:** The upper classes in Victorian Britain preferred handmade products, which symbolised refinement and class due to their better finish, individual production, and careful design. Machine-made goods were often seen as inferior and primarily for export to the colonies.

2.  How did the East India Company procure regular supplies of cotton and silk textiles from Indian weavers?

> **Answer:**
> To procure regular supplies of cotton and silk textiles from Indian weavers, the East India Company, after establishing political power, implemented a system of management and control designed to eliminate competition, control costs, and ensure consistent supply:
> *   **Elimination of Existing Traders:** The Company sought to bypass existing Indian traders and brokers who had traditionally linked weavers to the market. This allowed them to establish more direct control.
> *   **Appointment of Gomasthas:** The Company appointed paid servants called *gomasthas* to directly supervise weavers. These gomasthas were responsible for collecting supplies, examining the quality of cloth, and ensuring timely delivery.
> *   **System of Advances (Loans):** Weavers were given loans (advances) to purchase raw materials for their production. The crucial condition was that those who took these loans were obligated to hand over the cloth they produced exclusively to the gomastha. They were strictly forbidden from selling their produce to any other trader. This tied the weavers to the Company and ensured a monopoly supply.
> *   **Coercion and Punishment:** The gomasthas, being outsiders with no social ties to the villages, often acted arrogantly. They marched into villages with sepoys (Indian soldiers) and peons, punishing weavers for delays in supply, often through beating and flogging. This coercive approach ensured compliance and regular supply, despite low prices offered by the Company.
> *   **Loss of Bargaining Power:** Weavers lost their ability to bargain for better prices or sell to alternative buyers, as the loans and the gomastha system effectively eliminated their economic freedom.

3.  Imagine that you have been asked to write an article for an encyclopaedia on Britain and the history of cotton. Write your piece using information from the entire chapter.

> **Answer:**
> **Britain and the History of Cotton: From Proto-Industrialisation to Global Dominance**
>
> The history of cotton in Britain is inextricably linked to the broader narrative of the Industrial Revolution, marking a profound transformation from dispersed cottage industries to centralized factory production, and ultimately, global economic dominance.
>
> Before the advent of factories, Britain's cotton industry was characterized by *proto-industrialisation*. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, merchants from towns would supply raw cotton to rural peasant and artisan households. Families, including women and children, would engage in processes like spinning and weaving from their homes, supplementing their agricultural incomes. This decentralized production, while large-scale for an international market, was labour-intensive and lacked centralized supervision.
>
> The true revolution in cotton began in the late eighteenth century with a series of crucial inventions. Innovations in processes like carding, twisting, and spinning dramatically increased efficiency and output. Richard Arkwright's creation of the *cotton mill* was a watershed moment. For the first time, expensive new machines could be housed, operated, and maintained under one roof. This centralization allowed for meticulous supervision of production, stringent quality control, and systematic regulation of labour, which were impossible in the scattered domestic system. Cotton became the first symbol of the new industrial era, with raw cotton imports soaring from 2.5 million pounds in 1760 to 22 million pounds by 1787.
>
> Despite the dazzling promise of factory production, the pace of industrial change was not uniform. While cotton was the leading sector in the first phase of industrialisation (up to the 1840s), traditional industries persisted. Even by the late nineteenth century, less than 20% of the workforce was in technologically advanced sectors. New technology, such as James Watt's improved steam engine, was expensive, prone to breakdowns, and slow to be adopted, especially in industries where hand labour remained cheaper and more flexible, or where demand was seasonal. The *Spinning Jenny*, while revolutionary, faced significant opposition from workers, particularly women, who feared job displacement.
>
> The growth of the British cotton industry had profound global implications. As British cotton mills expanded, they exerted pressure on the government to impose import duties, protecting their domestic market from foreign competition, notably from fine Indian textiles. Simultaneously, they pushed for the East India Company to sell British manufactures in Indian markets. This led to a dramatic surge in British cotton goods exports to India, effectively shrinking India's local market and devastating its traditional handloom industry.
>
> The First World War, ironically, provided a temporary boost to Indian mills as British mills shifted to war production, but post-war, Manchester's dominance waned. The British economy struggled to modernise and compete with emerging industrial powers. This allowed Indian industrialists to gradually consolidate their position, eventually substituting foreign manufactures and capturing the home market.
>
> In conclusion, Britain's history with cotton is a testament to the transformative power of industrialisation. It showcases the shift from proto-industrial models to factory systems, the complex interplay between technological innovation and labour dynamics, and the far-reaching global economic and social consequences of industrial expansion and colonial trade policies.

4.  Why did industrial production in India increase during the First World War?

> **Answer:**
> Industrial production in India increased significantly during the First World War due to a confluence of factors that created an unprecedented demand for Indian manufactured goods:
> *   **Decline in British Imports:** With British mills and factories preoccupied with war production to meet the needs of the army, Manchester imports into India declined dramatically. This created a huge vacuum in the Indian market that local industries could fill.
> *   **Vast Home Market:** The reduction in foreign competition suddenly opened up a vast home market for Indian mills. Indian industrialists, who had previously avoided competing directly with Manchester goods, now had the opportunity to supply domestic demand.
> *   **War-Time Demands:** As the war prolonged, Indian factories were directly called upon by the British government to supply a wide array of war-related needs for the army. This included:
>     *   Jute bags (for supplies)
>     *   Cloth for army uniforms
>     *   Tents
>     *   Leather boots
>     *   Horse and mule saddles
>     *   Numerous other items.
> *   **Expansion of Production:** To meet these immense demands, new factories were set up, and existing ones were made to run multiple shifts, often with workers putting in longer hours. This led to a significant boom in industrial output across various sectors in India during the war years.
