This section delves into the challenging conditions faced by workers in Victorian Britain, including job scarcity, seasonal employment, and the impact of technological advancements on their livelihoods.

# Hand Labour and Steam Power

In Victorian Britain, there was no shortage of human labour. Poor peasants and vagrants migrated to cities in large numbers, seeking employment. The abundance of labour kept wages low, meaning industrialists did not face problems of labour shortage or high wage costs. This reduced their incentive to introduce machines that would replace human labour and require large capital investment.

Furthermore, demand for labour in many industries was seasonal. For instance:
*   Gas works and breweries were busiest during colder months, requiring more workers to meet peak demand.
*   Bookbinders and printers needed extra hands before December to cater to Christmas demand.
*   At the waterfront, winter was the time for ship repairs and maintenance.

In industries where production fluctuated seasonally, industrialists typically preferred hand labour, employing workers only for the required season.

A range of products could only be produced with hand labour. Machines were designed for uniform, standardised goods for a mass market. However, there was often a market demand for goods with intricate designs and specific shapes. For example, in mid-nineteenth-century Britain, 500 varieties of hammers and 45 kinds of axes were produced, requiring human skill rather than mechanical technology.

In Victorian Britain, the upper classes – aristocrats and the bourgeoisie – preferred handmade products. These items symbolised refinement and class, being better finished, individually produced, and carefully designed. Machine-made goods were often destined for export to the colonies.

In countries with labour shortages, industrialists were eager to use mechanical power to minimise the need for human labour, as was the case in nineteenth-century America. Britain, however, had no difficulty in hiring human hands.

### Source A: Will Thorne's Account

Will Thorne, a job-seeker, describes his journey to London in search of seasonal work in 1881, highlighting the arduous nature of the search for employment and the reliance on social networks.

> 'I had always wanted to go to London, and my desire ... was stimulated by letters from an old workmate ... who was now working at the Old Kent Road Gas Works ... I finally decided to go ... in November, 1881. With two friends I started out to walk the journey, filled with the hope that we would be able to obtain employment, when we get there, with the kind assistance of my friend ... we had little money when we started, not enough to pay for our food and lodgings each night until we arrived in London. Some days we walked as much as twenty miles, and other days less. Our money was gone at the end of the third day ... For two nights we slept out - once under a haystack, and once in an old farm shed ... On arrival in London we tried to find ... my friend ... but ... were unsuccessful. Our money was gone, so there was nothing for us to do but to walk around until late at night, and then try to find some place to sleep. We found an old building and slept in it that night. The next day, Sunday, late in the afternoon, we got to the Old Kent Gas Works, and applied for work. To my great surprise, the man we had been looking for was working at the time. He spoke to the foreman and I was given a job.'
> Quoted in Raphael Samuel, 'Comers and Goers', in H.J. Dyos and Michael Wolff, eds, The Victorian City: Images and Realities, 1973.

### Activity: Merchant's Letter

Imagine that you are a merchant writing back to a salesman who has been trying to persuade you to buy a new machine. Explain in your letter what you have heard and why you do not wish to invest in the new technology.

> **Answer:**
> Dear Salesman,
>
> Thank you for your persistent efforts in trying to convince me to invest in your new machinery. I have carefully considered your proposals and listened to what others in the industry are saying.
>
> While the promise of increased output is tempting, I have heard that these new machines are incredibly expensive to purchase and set up. Furthermore, I've been informed that they are prone to frequent breakdowns, and the cost of repairs is substantial. There are also concerns that they are not as effective as their inventors claim, and the quality of the output might not always meet the precise demands of the market for intricate, custom designs.
>
> In our current market, we have an abundant supply of human labour, and wages are relatively low. Employing skilled hand labourers allows us to produce a variety of goods, including those with fine designs that machines cannot replicate. Our current system also offers flexibility, especially with seasonal demand. Introducing large, capital-intensive machinery would mean a significant financial risk and might not be suitable for the diverse, often bespoke, nature of the goods our discerning customers prefer.
>
> For these reasons, I currently do not wish to invest in your new technology. I believe our existing methods, relying on skilled hand labour, remain more cost-effective and adaptable for our business.
>
> Sincerely,
> A Prudent Merchant

## Life of the Workers

The abundance of labour profoundly impacted workers' lives. News of potential jobs drew hundreds from the countryside to the cities. Obtaining a job often depended on existing networks of friendship and kin relations; having a relative or friend in a factory increased one's chances. However, many job seekers lacked such connections and had to wait for weeks, spending nights under bridges, in night shelters, or in *Night Refuges* set up by private individuals, or *Casual Wards* maintained by Poor Law authorities.

The seasonality of work meant prolonged periods of unemployment for many. After the busy season, the poor were back on the streets. Some returned to the countryside when rural labour demand increased in winter, but most sought odd jobs, which were difficult to find until the mid-nineteenth century.

Wages saw a slight increase in the early nineteenth century, but average figures masked significant variations between trades and fluctuations year-to-year. For example, during the prolonged Napoleonic War, prices rose sharply, causing the real value of workers' earnings to fall significantly as their wages could buy fewer goods. Moreover, income depended not just on the wage rate but crucially on the *period of employment* – the number of days worked determined average daily income. Even at the best of times until the mid-nineteenth century, about 10% of the urban population was extremely poor. During economic slumps, such as the 1830s, the proportion of unemployed people soared to between 35% and 75% in different regions.

The fear of unemployment made workers hostile to new technology. When the *Spinning Jenny* was introduced in the woollen industry, women who relied on hand spinning attacked the machines. This conflict over the jenny continued for a long time. The *Spinning Jenny*, devised by James Hargreaves in 1764, speeded up the spinning process and reduced labour demand by allowing a worker to set multiple spindles in motion with one wheel, spinning several threads simultaneously.

### Source B: Magistrate's Report on Worker Opposition

A magistrate reported in 1790 about an incident where he was called to protect a manufacturer's property from attacking workers:

> 'From the depredations of a lawless Banditti of colliers and their wives, for the wives had lost their work to spinning engines ... they advanced at first with much insolence, avowing their intention of cutting to pieces the machine lately introduced in the woollen manufacture; which they suppose, if generally adopted, will lessen the demand for manual labour. The women became clamorous. The men were more open to conviction and after some expostulation were induced to desist from their purpose and return peaceably home.'
> J.L. Hammond and B. Hammond, The Skilled Labourer 1760-1832, quoted in Maxine Berg, The Age of Manufactures.

### Discuss: Opposition to the Spinning Jenny

Look at Figs. 3, 7 and 11, then reread Source B. Explain why many workers were opposed to the use of the Spinning Jenny.

> **Answer:**
> Many workers were opposed to the use of the Spinning Jenny primarily because it threatened their livelihoods and skill-based employment.
> *   **Reduced Labour Demand:** As shown in Fig. 11 and explained in the definition, the Spinning Jenny allowed one worker to operate multiple spindles simultaneously, drastically increasing yarn production per person. This directly reduced the need for many hand spinners, especially women (as mentioned in Source B).
> *   **Fear of Unemployment:** Workers, particularly those who relied on manual spinning (like the women in Source B), feared losing their jobs to machines. With an already abundant labour supply and seasonal work, any technology that further reduced job opportunities was seen as a direct threat to their survival.
> *   **Impact on Wages and Income:** With fewer jobs available and increased competition, wages would inevitably fall, further worsening the already precarious economic situation of workers.
> *   **Loss of Traditional Skill:** The Jenny devalued the traditional skills of hand spinners, shifting the emphasis from human dexterity to machine operation.
> *   **Magistrate's Report (Source B):** The report clearly states that the workers, particularly the women, were attacking the machines because they "had lost their work to spinning engines" and believed the machines "will lessen the demand for manual labour." This directly confirms their economic motivation for opposition.
>
> In essence, the Spinning Jenny, despite its efficiency, was perceived as a job-destroying innovation that would exacerbate poverty and unemployment among a vulnerable working class.

After the 1840s, intensified building activity in cities created more employment opportunities. Roads were widened, new railway stations appeared, railway lines were extended, tunnels were dug, drainage and sewers were laid, and rivers were embanked. The number of workers in the transport industry doubled in the 1840s and again in the subsequent 30 years.
