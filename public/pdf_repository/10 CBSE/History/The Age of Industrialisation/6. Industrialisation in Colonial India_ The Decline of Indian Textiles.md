This section examines how the East India Company's growing political power in India led to the decline of traditional Indian textile trade networks and the imposition of new control systems over weavers.

# Industrialisation in the Colonies

We now shift our focus to India to understand how a colony industrialises, looking at both factory industries and the non-mechanised sector, primarily focusing on textiles.

## The Age of Indian Textiles

Before the age of machine industries, Indian silk and cotton goods dominated the international textile market. While coarser cottons were produced globally, the finer varieties often came from India.
*   Armenian and Persian merchants transported goods from Punjab to Afghanistan, eastern Persia, and Central Asia. Bales of fine textiles were carried by camel via the north-west frontier, through mountain passes and across deserts.
*   A vibrant sea trade operated through major pre-colonial ports:
    *   **Surat** on the Gujarat coast connected India to the Gulf and Red Sea Ports.
    *   **Masulipatam** on the Coromandel coast and **Hoogly** in Bengal had trade links with Southeast Asian ports.

A diverse network of Indian merchants and bankers was involved in this export trade, financing production, carrying goods, and supplying exporters. Supply merchants connected port towns to inland regions, providing advances to weavers, procuring woven cloth from weaving villages, and transporting supplies to the ports. At the ports, big shippers and export merchants employed brokers to negotiate prices and purchase goods from inland supply merchants.

By the 1750s, this Indian merchant-controlled network began to break down. European companies gradually gained power, first securing concessions from local courts, then monopoly rights to trade. This led to the decline of old ports like Surat and Hoogly, through which local merchants had operated. Exports from these ports fell dramatically, credit that had financed earlier trade dried up, and local bankers slowly went bankrupt. For instance, the gross value of trade through Surat, which was Rs 16 million in the late seventeenth century, slumped to Rs 3 million by the 1740s.

While Surat and Hoogly declined, Bombay and Calcutta grew. This shift indicated the rise of colonial power. Trade through these new ports came under the control of European companies and was carried in European ships. Many old trading houses collapsed, and those that survived had to operate within the new network shaped by European trading companies.

## What Happened to Weavers?

The consolidation of East India Company power after the 1760s did not immediately cause a decline in textile exports from India. British cotton industries had not yet expanded, and Indian fine textiles were in high demand in Europe. The Company was thus keen on expanding textile exports from India.

Before establishing political power in Bengal and Carnatic in the 1760s and 1770s, the East India Company struggled to ensure a regular supply of goods. French, Dutch, Portuguese, and local traders competed in the market, allowing weavers and supply merchants to bargain for the best prices. Company officials frequently complained to London about supply difficulties and high prices.

However, once the East India Company gained political power, it asserted a monopoly right to trade. It developed a system of management and control to eliminate competition, control costs, and ensure regular supplies of cotton and silk goods through several steps:
1.  **Elimination of Existing Traders:** The Company sought to eliminate existing traders and brokers, establishing more direct control over weavers. It appointed a paid servant called the *gomastha* to supervise weavers, collect supplies, and examine cloth quality.
2.  **System of Advances:** To prevent Company weavers from dealing with other buyers, the Company introduced a system of advances. Weavers were given loans to purchase raw materials, but in return, they had to hand over their finished cloth to the gomastha and could not sell it to any other trader.

As loans flowed in and demand for fine textiles grew, weavers eagerly took advances, hoping to earn more. Many weavers had small plots of land that they cultivated alongside weaving to meet family needs. Now, they had to lease out their land and dedicate all their time to weaving. Weaving, in fact, required the labour of the entire family, with women and children engaged in different stages of the process.

Soon, however, clashes between weavers and gomasthas became common in many weaving villages. Unlike earlier supply merchants who often lived in the villages and had close relationships with weavers, looking after their needs, the new gomasthas were outsiders with no long-term social links. They acted arrogantly, marched into villages with *sepoys* (Indian soldiers in British service) and peons, and punished weavers for supply delays, often beating and flogging them. Weavers lost their ability to bargain for prices and sell to different buyers; the price received from the Company was miserably low, and the accepted loans tied them to the Company.

In many parts of Carnatic and Bengal, weavers deserted their villages and migrated, setting up looms in other villages where they had family connections. Elsewhere, weavers, along with village traders, revolted against the Company and its officials. Over time, many weavers began refusing loans, closing their workshops, and resorting to agricultural labour.

By the turn of the nineteenth century, cotton weavers faced a new set of problems.

## Manchester Comes to India

In 1772, Henry Patullo, a Company official, confidently stated that demand for Indian textiles could never decline due to their unmatched quality. Yet, by the early nineteenth century, Indian textile exports began a long decline. In 1811-12, piece-goods accounted for 33% of India's exports; by 1850-51, this figure dropped to a mere 3%.

Why did this happen, and what were its implications?
As cotton industries developed in England, industrial groups pressured the British government to impose import duties on cotton textiles. This ensured that Manchester goods could sell in Britain without competition from outside. Simultaneously, industrialists persuaded the East India Company to sell British manufactures in Indian markets. Exports of British cotton goods to India increased dramatically in the early nineteenth century. At the end of the eighteenth century, there had been virtually no import of cotton piece-goods into India. However, by 1850, cotton piece-goods constituted over 31% of the value of Indian imports, and by the 1870s, this figure exceeded 50%.

Indian cotton weavers thus faced two simultaneous problems:
1.  Their export market collapsed.
2.  Their local market shrank, flooded with cheap Manchester imports.
Produced by machines at lower costs, imported cotton goods were so inexpensive that Indian weavers struggled to compete. By the 1850s, reports from most weaving regions of India narrated stories of decline and desolation.

### Source C: Commissioner of Patna's Report

The Commissioner of Patna wrote:
> 'It appears that twenty years ago, a brisk trade was carried on in the manufacture of cloth at Jahanabad, and Behar, which has in the former place entirely ceased, while in the latter the amount of manufacture is very limited, in consequence of the cheap and durable goods from Manchester with which the Native manufactures are unable to compete.'
> Quoted in J. Krishnamurty, 'Deindustrialisation in Gangetic Bihar during the nineteenth century', The Indian Economic and Social History Review, 1985.

### Source D: Census Report on Koshtis

Reporting on the Koshtis, a community of weavers, the Census Report of Central Provinces stated:
> 'The Koshtis, like the weavers of the finer kinds of cloth in other parts of India, have fallen upon evil times. They are unable to compete with the showy goods which Manchester sends in such profusion, and they have of late years emigrated in great numbers, chiefly to Berar, where as day labourers they are able to obtain wages ...'
> Census Report of Central Provinces, 1872, quoted in Sumit Guha, 'The handloom industry in Central India, 1825-1950', The Indian Economic and Social History Review.

By the 1860s, weavers faced a new problem: insufficient supply of good quality raw cotton. When the American Civil War broke out and cotton supplies from the US were cut off, Britain turned to India. As raw cotton exports from India increased, the price of raw cotton shot up. Weavers in India were starved of supplies and forced to buy raw cotton at exorbitant prices, making weaving unprofitable.
