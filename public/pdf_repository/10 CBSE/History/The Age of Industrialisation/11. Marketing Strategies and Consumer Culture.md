This section explores how manufacturers created markets for their new products during the industrial age, focusing on the role of advertisements, labels, and the use of cultural and nationalist messages.

# Market for Goods

We have seen how British manufacturers attempted to capture the Indian market, and how Indian weavers, craftsmen, traders, and industrialists resisted colonial controls, demanded tariff protection, created their own spaces, and tried to expand the market for their produce.

However, when new products are manufactured, consumers need to be persuaded to buy them. They need to feel a desire or need for the product. How was this achieved?

One primary way new consumers are created is through advertisements. Advertisements make products appear desirable and necessary, shaping people's minds and creating new needs. While advertisements surround us today in various media, they have played a crucial role in expanding markets and shaping consumer culture since the very beginning of the industrial age.

When Manchester industrialists began selling cloth in India, they placed labels on cloth bundles. These labels served several purposes:
*   To make the place of manufacture and the company name familiar to the buyer.
*   To serve as a mark of quality. Buyers were expected to feel confident about purchasing cloth with "MADE IN MANCHESTER" boldly written on the label.

Labels did not just carry words; they often featured beautiful illustrations. These old labels offer insights into the manufacturers' mindset, their calculations, and their strategies for appealing to people.

Images of Indian gods and goddesses frequently appeared on these labels. This was intended to:
*   Give divine approval to the goods being sold, as if associating with gods blessed the product.
*   Make foreign-made products appear familiar to Indian people, for instance, by featuring images of Krishna or Saraswati.

By the late nineteenth century, manufacturers also printed calendars to popularise their products. Unlike newspapers and magazines, calendars were accessible even to those who could not read. They were hung in tea shops, poor people's homes, offices, and middle-class apartments. This ensured that those who hung the calendars would see the advertisements day after day, throughout the year. These calendars, too, often used figures of gods to sell new products.

Like images of gods, figures of important personages, such as emperors and nawabs, adorned advertisements and calendars. The implicit message often was: if you respect the royal figure, then respect this product. If a product was used by kings or produced under royal command, its quality was presented as unquestionable.

When Indian manufacturers advertised, the nationalist message was clear and loud: "If you care for the nation, then buy products that Indians produce." Advertisements thus became a powerful vehicle for the nationalist message of *Swadeshi*.
