# The Coming Up of the Factory

The earliest factories in England appeared around the 1730s, but their numbers multiplied significantly only in the late eighteenth century. Cotton became the first symbol of this new era. Its production boomed in the late nineteenth century. In 1760, Britain imported 2.5 million pounds of raw cotton; by 1787, this soared to 22 million pounds. This dramatic increase was driven by several changes in the production process.

A series of inventions in the eighteenth century greatly increased the efficiency of each step of cotton production, including *carding* (the process of preparing fibres like cotton or wool prior to spinning), twisting, spinning, and rolling. These innovations enhanced output per worker, enabling more production and the creation of stronger threads and yarn.

Richard Arkwright is credited with creating the cotton mill. Previously, cloth production was dispersed across the countryside, carried out within village households. With Arkwright's innovation, costly new machines could be purchased, set up, and maintained in a central mill. Bringing all production processes under one roof and management allowed for:
*   More careful supervision over the production process.
*   Better quality control.
*   More effective regulation of labour.
These aspects were difficult to manage when production was scattered in the countryside.

By the early nineteenth century, factories became an integral part of the English landscape. Their imposing structures and the seemingly magical power of new technology dazzled contemporaries, who focused their attention on the mills, often overlooking the smaller workshops and bylanes where production still continued.
