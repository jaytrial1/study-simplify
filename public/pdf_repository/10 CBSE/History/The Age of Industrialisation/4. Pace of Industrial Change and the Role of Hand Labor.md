# The Pace of Industrial Change

How rapid was the process of industrialisation, and did it exclusively mean the growth of factory industries?

1.  **Dominant Industries:** The most dynamic industries in Britain were cotton and metals. Cotton led the first phase of industrialisation until the 1840s. Subsequently, the iron and steel industry took over, driven by the expansion of railways from the 1840s in England and the 1860s in the colonies. By 1873, Britain's iron and steel exports reached about £77 million, double the value of its cotton exports.

2.  **Persistence of Traditional Industries:** New industries did not easily displace traditional ones. Even by the end of the nineteenth century, less than 20% of the total workforce was employed in technologically advanced industrial sectors. While textiles were dynamic, a large portion of output still came from domestic units, not factories.

3.  **Innovations in Traditional Sectors:** The pace of change in 'traditional' industries was not set by steam-powered cotton or metal industries, but they were not stagnant either. Seemingly ordinary and small innovations drove growth in non-mechanised sectors such as food processing, building, pottery, glass work, tanning, furniture making, and implement production.

4.  **Slow Spread of New Technology:** Technological changes occurred slowly and did not spread dramatically across the industrial landscape. New technology was expensive, and merchants and industrialists were cautious about adopting it. Machines often broke down, repairs were costly, and their effectiveness was not always as claimed by inventors.

Consider the *steam engine*. James Watt improved the steam engine produced by Newcomen and patented his new engine in 1781. His industrialist friend Mathew Boulton manufactured the new model, but it found few buyers for years. By the beginning of the nineteenth century, there were only 321 steam engines in all of England. Of these, 80 were in cotton industries, 9 in wool industries, and the rest in mining, canal works, and iron works. Steam engines were not used in other industries until much later in the century. This illustrates that even the most powerful new technology, capable of significantly enhancing labour productivity, was slow to be accepted by industrialists.

Historians increasingly recognise that the typical worker in the mid-nineteenth century was not a machine operator but a traditional craftsperson and labourer.

### Activity: Comparing Industrial Images

Look at Figs. 4 and 5. Can you see any difference in the way the two images show industrialisation? Explain your view briefly.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Fig. 4 (A Lancashire cotton mill, 1925):** This image focuses on a single, massive factory building, glowing with electricity in the twilight. It portrays industrialisation as a dominant, imposing, and almost magical force, emphasizing the scale and modern technology of a specific industrial unit. The smoke from the chimney is present but the overall impression is one of efficiency and power.
> *   **Fig. 5 (Industrial Manchester, 1857):** This image provides a panoramic view of a landscape dominated by numerous chimneys billowing smoke. It depicts industrialisation as a widespread, pervasive transformation of the entire environment, with many factories contributing to a hazy, polluted atmosphere. It emphasizes the sheer quantity of industrial activity and its collective impact on the landscape, rather than the grandeur of a single mill.
