This section details the origins and characteristics of the labour force in Indian factories, highlighting migration patterns and the role of the jobber in recruitment.

# Where Did the Workers Come From?

Factories required workers, and with the expansion of factories, this demand grew significantly. In 1901, Indian factories employed 584,000 workers; by 1946, this number exceeded 2,436,000. Where did these workers originate?

In most industrial regions, workers came from surrounding districts. Peasants and artisans who could not find work in their villages migrated to industrial centres in search of employment. For example:
*   Over 50% of workers in the Bombay cotton industries in 1911 came from the neighbouring district of Ratnagiri.
*   The mills of Kanpur drew most of their textile workers from villages within the Kanpur district itself.

Millworkers often moved between their villages and the city, returning to their rural homes during harvests and festivals.

Over time, as news of employment spread, workers travelled great distances in hope of securing mill jobs. For instance, many from the United Provinces migrated to work in the textile mills of Bombay and the jute mills of Calcutta.

However, getting jobs was always difficult, even as mills multiplied and demand for workers increased, because the number of job seekers consistently exceeded available positions. Entry into mills was also restricted. Industrialists typically employed a *jobber* to recruit new workers. The jobber was often an old and trusted worker from the factory. His role involved:
*   Bringing people from his village.
*   Ensuring them jobs.
*   Helping them settle in the city.
*   Providing financial assistance during crises.

The jobber thus became a person of authority and power, often demanding money and gifts for his favour and exerting control over the lives of workers.

While the number of factory workers grew over time, they constituted only a small proportion of the total industrial workforce.

### Source E: Vasant Parkar on Jobbers and Rural Ties

Vasant Parkar, a former millworker in Bombay, recalled:
> 'The workers would pay the jobbers money to get their sons work in the mill ... The mill worker was closely associated with his village, physically and emotionally. He would go home to cut the harvest and for sowing. The Konkani would go home to cut the paddy and the Ghati, the sugarcane. It was an accepted practice for which the mills granted leave.'
> Meena Menon and Neera Adarkar, One Hundred Years: One Hundred Voices, 2004.

### Source F: Bhai Bhosle on Working Conditions

Bhai Bhosle, a trade unionist from Bombay, recollected his childhood in the 1930s and 1940s:
> 'In those days, the shift was 10 hours - from 5 pm to 3 am - terrible working hours. My father worked for 35 years; he got the asthma like disease and could not work any more...Then my father went back to village.'
> Meena Menon and Neera Adarkar, One Hundred Years: One Hundred Voices.

European Managing Agencies, which dominated industrial production in India, focused on specific products. They established tea and coffee plantations, acquiring land cheaply from the colonial government, and invested in mining, indigo, and jute. Most of these products were primarily for export and not for sale within India.

When Indian businessmen began establishing industries in the late nineteenth century, they avoided direct competition with Manchester goods in the Indian market. Since yarn was not a significant British import into India, early Indian cotton mills produced coarse cotton yarn (thread) rather than fabric. Imported yarn was typically of superior quality. The yarn produced in Indian spinning mills was either used by handloom weavers in India or exported to China.
