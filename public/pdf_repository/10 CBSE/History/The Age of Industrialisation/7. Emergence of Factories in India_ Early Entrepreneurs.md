This section details the establishment of the first factories in India, identifying the key Indian entrepreneurs and the sources of their capital, often linked to international trade.

# Factories Come Up

The first cotton mill in Bombay was established in 1854 and began production two years later. By 1862, four mills were operating with 94,000 spindles and 2,150 looms. Around the same time, jute mills emerged in Bengal, with the first set up in 1855 and another in 1862. In north India, the Elgin Mill started in Kanpur in the 1860s, followed a year later by the first cotton mill of Ahmedabad. By 1874, Madras saw its first spinning and weaving mill begin production.

This raises key questions: Who set up these industries? Where did the capital come from? And who came to work in these mills?

## The Early Entrepreneurs

Industries were established in different regions by various individuals and groups. The history of many business groups traces back to trade with China. From the late eighteenth century, the British in India exported opium to China and imported tea from China to England. Many Indians became junior partners in this trade, providing finance, procuring supplies, and shipping consignments. Having accumulated wealth through trade, some of these businessmen envisioned developing industrial enterprises in India.

*   In **Bengal**, Dwarkanath Tagore made his fortune in the China trade before investing in industry, setting up six joint-stock companies in the 1830s and 1840s. Although Tagore's enterprises faced setbacks during the business crises of the 1840s, many other China traders became successful industrialists later in the nineteenth century.
*   In **Bombay**, Parsis like Dinshaw Petit and Jamsetjee Nusserwanjee Tata, who built vast industrial empires in India, accumulated their initial wealth partly from exports to China and partly from raw cotton shipments to England.
*   Seth Hukumchand, a Marwari businessman who established the first Indian jute mill in Calcutta in 1917, also traded with China. The father and grandfather of the famous industrialist G.D. Birla similarly engaged in this trade.

Capital was also accumulated through other trade networks. Some merchants from Madras traded with Burma, while others had links with the Middle East and East Africa. Other commercial groups operated solely within India, transporting goods, banking money, transferring funds between cities, and financing traders. When industrial investment opportunities arose, many of them established factories.

As colonial control over Indian trade tightened, the scope for Indian merchants became increasingly limited. They were barred from trading with Europe in manufactured goods and primarily had to export raw materials and food grains – such as raw cotton, opium, wheat, and indigo – required by the British. They were also gradually edged out of the shipping business.

Until the First World War, European Managing Agencies controlled a large sector of Indian industries. Three of the biggest were Bird Heiglers & Co., Andrew Yule, and Jardine Skinner & Co. These agencies mobilised capital, set up joint-stock companies, and managed them. In most cases, Indian financiers provided the capital, while the European Agencies made all investment and business decisions. European merchant-industrialists maintained their own chambers of commerce, which Indian businessmen were not permitted to join.
