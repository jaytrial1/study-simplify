This section explores how small-scale production, particularly handloom weaving, managed to survive and even expand despite the growth of factory industries, adapting through technological innovations and catering to niche markets.

# Small-scale Industries Predominate

While factory industries grew steadily after the war, large industries constituted only a small segment of the economy. In 1911, about 67% of large industries were concentrated in Bengal and Bombay. Across the rest of the country, small-scale production continued to dominate. Only a small proportion of the total industrial labour force worked in registered factories: 5% in 1911 and 10% in 1931. The majority worked in small workshops and household units, often located in alleys and bylanes, hidden from public view.

In fact, in some instances, handicrafts production actually expanded in the twentieth century, even for the handloom sector. While cheap machine-made thread decimated the spinning industry in the nineteenth century, weavers survived, despite facing numerous problems. Handloom cloth production expanded steadily in the twentieth century, almost trebling between 1900 and 1940.

How did this happen?
This resilience was partly due to technological changes. Handicrafts people adopted new technology if it helped them improve production without excessively increasing costs. By the second decade of the twentieth century, weavers began using looms fitted with a *fly shuttle*. This mechanical device, moved by ropes and pulleys, places horizontal threads (weft) into vertical threads (warp), enabling weavers to operate larger looms and weave wider pieces of cloth, thereby increasing productivity per worker, speeding up production, and reducing labour demand. By 1941, over 35% of handlooms in India were equipped with fly shuttles; in regions like Travancore, Madras, Mysore, Cochin, and Bengal, the proportion was 70% to 80%. Several other small innovations also helped weavers improve productivity and compete with the mill sector.

Certain groups of weavers were better positioned than others to survive competition from mill industries. Weavers produced both coarse and finer varieties of cloth.
*   **Coarse cloth:** Bought by the poor, its demand fluctuated violently. During bad harvests and famines, when rural poor had little to eat and no cash income, they could not afford cloth.
*   **Finer varieties:** Bought by the well-to-do, its demand was more stable. The rich could afford these even during times of scarcity for the poor. Famines did not affect the sale of specialised items like Banarasi or Baluchari saris.

Moreover, mills could not easily imitate specialised weaves. Saris with woven borders, or the famous lungis and handkerchiefs of Madras, could not be easily displaced by mill production.

Weavers and other craftspeople who continued to expand production through the twentieth century did not necessarily prosper. They lived hard lives and worked long hours. Very often, the entire household – including all women and children – had to work at various stages of the production process. However, they were not merely remnants of past times in the age of factories; their life and labour were integral to the process of industrialisation.
