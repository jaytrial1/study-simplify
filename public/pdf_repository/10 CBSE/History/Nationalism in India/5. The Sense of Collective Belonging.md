This section explores the various cultural processes that contributed to the emergence of a collective sense of belonging and national identity in India. It examines the role of history, fiction, folklore, songs, popular prints, and symbols, particularly the visual representation of Bharat Mata and the reinterpretation of India's past, in fostering nationalism. It also acknowledges the challenges arising from the Hindu-centric nature of some of these unifying efforts.

# The Sense of Collective Belonging

Nationalism thrives when people begin to believe they are all part of the same nation, when they discover a unity that binds them together. But how did this sense of nationhood become a reality in the minds of the people? How did individuals from diverse communities, regions, or language groups develop a shared sense of collective belonging?

This feeling of collective belonging emerged partly through the shared experience of united struggles against colonial rule. However, a variety of cultural processes also played a crucial role in capturing people's imagination and fostering nationalism. History and fiction, folklore and songs, popular prints and symbols—all contributed significantly to the making of nationalism.

The identity of a nation is most often symbolized in a figure or image, which helps people identify with the nation. In the twentieth century, with the burgeoning of nationalism, the identity of India became visually associated with the image of **Bharat Mata** (Mother India). This image was first created by **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay**. In the 1870s, he composed '**Vande Mataram**' as a hymn to the motherland. It was later included in his novel *Anandamath* and widely sung during the Swadeshi movement in Bengal. Inspired by the Swadeshi movement, **Abanindranath Tagore** painted his famous image of Bharat Mata in 1905 (see Fig. 12). In this painting, Bharat Mata is depicted as an ascetic figure: calm, composed, divine, and spiritual. In subsequent years, the image of Bharat Mata evolved into many different forms, circulating in popular prints and painted by various artists (see Fig. 14a). Devotion to this mother figure came to be regarded as evidence of one's nationalism.

Ideas of nationalism also developed through a movement to revive Indian **folklore**. In late-nineteenth-century India, nationalists began recording folk tales sung by bards and toured villages to gather folk songs and legends. They believed these tales provided a true picture of traditional culture that had been corrupted and damaged by outside forces. It was considered essential to preserve this folk tradition to discover one's national identity and restore a sense of pride in one's past. In Bengal, **Rabindranath Tagore** himself began collecting ballads, nursery rhymes, and myths, leading the movement for folk revival. In Madras, **Natesa Sastri** published a massive four-volume collection of Tamil folk tales, *The Folklore of Southern India*. He asserted that folklore was national literature; it was 'the most trustworthy manifestation of people's real thoughts and characteristics'.

As the national movement progressed, nationalist leaders became increasingly aware of the power of such icons and symbols in unifying people and inspiring a feeling of nationalism. During the Swadeshi movement in Bengal, a **tricolour flag** (red, green, and yellow) was designed. It featured eight lotuses, representing the eight provinces of British India, and a crescent moon, symbolizing Hindus and Muslims. By 1921, Gandhiji had designed the **Swaraj flag**. It was also a tricolour (red, green, and white) and had a spinning wheel (charkha) in the center, representing the Gandhian ideal of self-help. Carrying this flag, holding it aloft during marches, became a powerful symbol of defiance.

Another means of fostering nationalism was through the **reinterpretation of history**. By the end of the nineteenth century, many Indians began to feel that to instill a sense of pride in the nation, Indian history needed to be viewed differently. The British often portrayed Indians as backward and primitive, incapable of self-governance. In response, Indians started delving into the past to uncover India's great achievements. They wrote about the glorious developments in ancient times when art and architecture, science and mathematics, religion and culture, law and philosophy, crafts and trade had flourished. This glorious period, in their view, was followed by a history of decline, culminating in India's colonization. These nationalist histories urged readers to take pride in India's past achievements and to struggle to change the miserable conditions of life under British rule.

*The image is a classic allegorical representation of **Bharat Mata** (Mother India), a powerful icon that emerged during the Indian nationalist movement. The central figure is a serene woman, depicted as a goddess, embodying the Indian nation. She holds a flag featuring the tricolor (saffron, white, and green) with a wheel, which is the national flag of India, directly linking the divine mother figure to the modern political entity. The image draws heavily on Hindu iconography, with her holding a **trident (trishul)**, a **lion** at her feet, and leaning on an **elephant**, all symbols of power and authority. In the background, figures resembling other Hindu goddesses like Saraswati (playing a veena) and Lakshmi (seated on a lotus) further emphasize India's rich spiritual, intellectual, and material heritage. By portraying the nation as a divine mother figure, the image aimed to inspire patriotism, foster emotional attachment, and encourage collective action among diverse populations during the freedom struggle.*

Fig. 14a - Bharat Mata. This figure of Bharat Mata is a contrast to the one painted by Abanindranath Tagore. Here she is shown with a trishul, standing beside a lion and an elephant - both symbols of power and authority.

However, these efforts to unify people were not without problems. When the glorified past was predominantly Hindu, and the celebrated images were drawn from Hindu iconography, people from other communities often felt excluded.
