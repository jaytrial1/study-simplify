This section examines the profound impact of the First World War on India's economic and political landscape, setting the stage for the emergence of Mahatma Gandhi as a national leader. It details his philosophy of Satyagraha, the controversial Rowlatt Act, the tragic Jallianwalla Bagh incident, and the pivotal Khilafat issue, all of which culminated in the launch of the Non-Cooperation Movement.

# The First World War, Khilafat and Non-Cooperation

In the years following 1919, the national movement in India began to expand into new regions, integrate new social groups, and develop novel methods of struggle. Understanding these developments requires an examination of their underlying causes and implications.

The First World War brought about a new economic and political situation in India. It led to a massive increase in **defence expenditure**, financed through war loans and escalating taxes. Customs duties were raised, and **income tax** was introduced for the first time. Throughout the war years, prices doubled between 1913 and 1918, causing extreme hardship for the common people. Villages were compelled to supply soldiers, and **forced recruitment** in rural areas generated widespread anger.

**New words**
*   **Forced recruitment:** A process by which the colonial state compelled people to join the army.

Furthermore, in 1918-19 and 1920-21, widespread crop failures in many parts of India resulted in acute food shortages. This crisis was exacerbated by an influenza epidemic. According to the census of 1921, an estimated 12 to 13 million people perished due to famines and the epidemic. People had hoped that their hardships would cease after the war, but this expectation remained unfulfilled.

At this critical juncture, a new leader emerged, proposing a distinct mode of struggle.

## The Idea of Satyagraha

Mahatma Gandhi returned to India in January 1915. He had previously been in South Africa, where he had successfully challenged the racist regime using a novel method of mass agitation, which he termed **satyagraha**.

The core idea of **satyagraha** emphasized the power of truth and the necessity of seeking truth. It posited that if the cause was just, and the struggle was against injustice, then physical force was not required to combat the oppressor. A satyagrahi, without resorting to vengeance or aggression, could triumph through **nonviolence**. This was achieved by appealing to the oppressor's conscience, persuading them to acknowledge the truth rather than coercing them through violence. Mahatma Gandhi firmly believed that through this struggle, truth was ultimately destined to prevail. He was convinced that this *dharma* of non-violence had the potential to unite all Indians.

Upon his return to India, Mahatma Gandhi successfully organized several satyagraha movements:
*   **1917 (Champaran, Bihar):** To inspire peasants to fight against the oppressive plantation system.
*   **1917 (Kheda, Gujarat):** To support peasants affected by crop failure and plague, who were unable to pay revenue and demanded its relaxation.
*   **1918 (Ahmedabad):** To organize a satyagraha movement among cotton mill workers.

## The Rowlatt Act

Encouraged by these successes, Gandhiji decided in 1919 to launch a nationwide satyagraha against the proposed **Rowlatt Act (1919)**. This Act had been hastily passed through the Imperial Legislative Council despite unified opposition from Indian members. It granted the government immense powers to repress political activities and permitted the detention of political prisoners without trial for two years. Mahatma Gandhi advocated for non-violent civil disobedience against such unjust laws, initiating it with a *hartal* (strike) on April 6th.

Rallies were organized in various cities, railway workers went on strike, and shops closed down. Alarmed by the popular uprising and fearing disruptions to vital communication lines like railways and telegraphs, the British administration decided to suppress the nationalists. Local leaders were arrested in Amritsar, and Mahatma Gandhi was prohibited from entering Delhi. On April 10th, the police in Amritsar fired upon a peaceful procession, leading to widespread attacks on banks, post offices, and railway stations. **Martial law** was imposed, and General Dyer assumed command.

### Mahatma Gandhi on Satyagraha

> 'It is said of "passive resistance" that it is the weapon of the weak, but the power which is the subject of this article can be used only by the strong. This power is not passive resistance; indeed it calls for intense activity. The movement in South Africa was not passive but active ...
> ' Satyagraha is not physical force. A satyagrahi does not inflict pain on the adversary; he does not seek his destruction ... In the use of satyagraha, there is no ill-will whatever.
> ' Satyagraha is pure soul-force. Truth is the very substance of the soul. That is why this force is called satyagraha. The soul is informed with knowledge. In it burns the flame of love. ... Nonviolence is the supreme dharma ...
> 'It is certain that India cannot rival Britain or Europe in force of arms. The British worship the war-god and they can all of them become, as they are becoming, bearers of arms. The hundreds of millions in India can never carry arms. They have made the religion of non-violence their own ...'

### Activity

Read the text carefully. What did Mahatma Gandhi mean when he said satyagraha is active resistance?

> **Answer:** Mahatma Gandhi meant that satyagraha is not a passive or weak form of resistance, but rather a highly active and courageous one. It requires immense inner strength, conviction, and constant effort. Unlike physical force, which aims to inflict pain or destroy the opponent, satyagraha actively seeks to persuade the oppressor by appealing to their conscience through truth and non-violence. It is a struggle that demands intense moral and spiritual engagement, not mere inaction.

On April 13th, the infamous **Jallianwalla Bagh incident** occurred. A large crowd had gathered in the enclosed ground of Jallianwalla Bagh; some came to protest new repressive government measures, while others were attending the annual Baisakhi fair. Many villagers, being from outside the city, were unaware of the imposed martial law. General Dyer entered the area, blocked the exit points, and opened fire on the unsuspecting crowd, killing hundreds. His stated objective was to "produce a moral effect," to instill a feeling of terror and awe in the minds of satyagrahis.

As news of the Jallianwalla Bagh massacre spread, crowds took to the streets in many north Indian towns. There were strikes, clashes with the police, and attacks on government buildings. The government responded with brutal repression, aiming to humiliate and terrorize people: satyagrahis were forced to rub their noses on the ground, crawl on the streets, and perform *salaam* (salute) to all sahibs; people were flogged, and villages (such as those around Gujranwala in Punjab, now in Pakistan) were bombed. Witnessing the escalating violence, Mahatma Gandhi called off the movement.

While the Rowlatt satyagraha had been a widespread movement, it was primarily limited to cities and towns. Mahatma Gandhi now recognized the need to launch a more broad-based movement across India. He was convinced that such a movement could not be organized without fostering closer unity between Hindus and Muslims. He believed that taking up the **Khilafat issue** was one way to achieve this.

The First World War had concluded with the defeat of Ottoman Turkey, and rumors circulated that a harsh peace treaty would be imposed on the Ottoman emperor, who was the spiritual head of the Islamic world (the **Khalifa**). To defend the Khalifa's temporal powers, a **Khilafat Committee** was formed in Bombay in March 1919. A new generation of Muslim leaders, including the brothers Muhammad Ali and Shaukat Ali, began discussing with Mahatma Gandhi the possibility of united mass action on this issue. Gandhiji viewed this as an invaluable opportunity to bring Muslims under the umbrella of a unified national movement. At the Calcutta session of the Congress in September 1920, he successfully persuaded other leaders of the necessity to launch a **Non-Cooperation Movement** in support of both the Khilafat cause and **swaraj** (self-rule).

## Why Non-cooperation?

In his renowned book *Hind Swaraj* (1909), Mahatma Gandhi asserted that British rule in India was established and sustained only with the cooperation of Indians. He declared that if Indians refused to cooperate, British rule in India would collapse within a year, and *swaraj* would be achieved.

Gandhiji proposed that the Non-Cooperation Movement should unfold in stages. It would commence with the surrender of titles awarded by the government and a **boycott** of civil services, the army, police, courts, legislative councils, schools, and foreign goods. Subsequently, if the government resorted to repression, a full civil disobedience campaign would be initiated. Throughout the summer of 1920, Mahatma Gandhi and Shaukat Ali toured extensively, mobilizing popular support for the movement.

**New words**
*   **Boycott:** The refusal to deal and associate with people, or participate in activities, or buy and use things; usually a form of protest.

However, many within the Congress harbored concerns about these proposals. They were reluctant to boycott the council elections scheduled for November 1920 and feared that the movement might lead to widespread violence. Between September and December, an intense struggle ensued within the Congress, making a compromise between supporters and opponents of the movement seem unlikely. Ultimately, at the Congress session in Nagpur in December 1920, a compromise was reached, and the Non-Cooperation programme was officially adopted.
