This section details the reasons for the withdrawal of the Non-Cooperation Movement and the subsequent shift in Indian politics towards the Civil Disobedience Movement. It covers the internal debates within the Congress, the impact of the worldwide economic depression, the Simon Commission, the declaration of Purna Swaraj, and the iconic Salt March. It also examines how various social groups participated and their distinct interpretations of *Swaraj*, alongside the limitations of the movement, particularly regarding Dalits and Muslim communities.

# Towards Civil Disobedience

In February 1922, Mahatma Gandhi made the decision to withdraw the Non-Cooperation Movement. He felt that the movement was becoming violent in many places, and that *satyagrahis* needed proper training before they could be ready for mass struggles. Within the Congress, some leaders were by now weary of mass struggles and desired to participate in elections to the provincial councils that had been established by the Government of India Act of 1919. They believed it was crucial to oppose British policies from within the councils, advocate for reforms, and expose the undemocratic nature of these councils. **C. R. Das** and **Motilal Nehru** formed the **Swaraj Party** within the Congress to argue for a return to council politics. However, younger leaders like **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Subhas Chandra Bose** pressed for more radical mass agitation and complete independence.

In this atmosphere of internal debate and dissension, two significant factors shaped Indian politics towards the late 1920s. The first was the impact of the worldwide **economic depression**. Agricultural prices began to decline from 1926 and collapsed after 1930. As the demand for agricultural goods fell and exports dwindled, peasants found it increasingly difficult to sell their harvests and pay their revenue. By 1930, the countryside was in turmoil.

Against this backdrop, the new Tory government in Britain constituted a **Statutory Commission** under Sir John Simon. This commission, set up in response to the nationalist movement, was tasked with examining the functioning of the constitutional system in India and proposing changes. The fundamental problem was that the commission did not include a single Indian member; all its members were British.

When the **Simon Commission** arrived in India in 1928, it was met with widespread protests and the slogan '**Go back Simon**'. All political parties, including the Congress and the Muslim League, participated in these demonstrations. In an attempt to appease them, the viceroy, **Lord Irwin**, announced in October 1929 a vague offer of 'dominion status' for India in an unspecified future, along with a **Round Table Conference** to discuss a future constitution. This offer failed to satisfy the Congress leaders. The radicals within the Congress, led by Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose, became more assertive. The liberals and moderates, who had advocated for a constitutional system within the framework of British dominion, gradually lost their influence.

Lala Lajpat Rai was assaulted by the British police during a peaceful demonstration against the Simon Commission. He succumbed to injuries that were inflicted on him during the demonstration.

In December 1929, under the presidency of Jawaharlal Nehru, the **Lahore Congress** formalized the demand for '**Purna Swaraj**' or **full independence** for India. It was declared that January 26, 1930, would be celebrated as **Independence Day**, when people were to take a pledge to strive for complete independence. However, these celebrations attracted very little attention. Consequently, Mahatma Gandhi had to devise a way to connect this abstract idea of freedom to more tangible issues of everyday life.

## The Salt March and the Civil Disobedience Movement

Mahatma Gandhi identified salt as a powerful symbol capable of uniting the nation. On January 31, 1930, he sent a letter to Viceroy Irwin outlining eleven demands. Some of these were of general interest, while others were specific demands of various classes, ranging from industrialists to peasants. The aim was to make the demands broad-ranging so that all segments of Indian society could identify with them and be brought together in a united campaign. The most impactful demand was the abolition of the **salt tax**. Salt was consumed by both the rich and the poor and was an essential food item. The tax on salt and the government's monopoly over its production, Mahatma Gandhi declared, exposed the most oppressive aspect of British rule.

Mahatma Gandhi's letter was, in essence, an ultimatum. It stated that if the demands were not met by March 11, the Congress would launch a civil disobedience campaign. Irwin was unwilling to negotiate. Thus, Mahatma Gandhi embarked on his famous **Salt March**, accompanied by 78 of his trusted volunteers. The march covered over 240 miles, from Gandhiji's ashram in Sabarmati to the Gujarati coastal town of **Dandi**. The volunteers walked for 24 days, covering approximately 10 miles daily. Thousands gathered to hear Mahatma Gandhi wherever he stopped, and he explained his vision of *swaraj* while urging them to peacefully defy the British. On April 6, he reached Dandi and ceremonially violated the law by manufacturing salt from boiling sea water.

This act marked the beginning of the **Civil Disobedience Movement**. How was this movement different from the Non-Cooperation Movement? People were now asked not only to refuse cooperation with the British, as they had done in 1921-22, but also to *break colonial laws*. Thousands across the country violated the salt law, manufactured salt, and demonstrated in front of government salt factories. As the movement spread, foreign cloth was boycotted, and liquor shops were picketed. Peasants refused to pay revenue and *chaukidari* taxes, village officials resigned, and in many places, forest dwellers violated forest laws by entering Reserved Forests to collect wood and graze cattle.

In this situation, Mahatma Gandhi once again decided to call off the movement and entered into a pact with Irwin on March 5, 1931. By this **Gandhi-Irwin Pact**, Gandhiji agreed to participate in a **Round Table Conference** (the Congress had boycotted the first conference) in London, and the government consented to release political prisoners. In December 1931, Gandhiji traveled to London for the conference, but negotiations broke down, and he returned disappointed. Back in India, he discovered that the government had initiated a new cycle of repression. Ghaffar Khan and Jawaharlal Nehru were both in jail, the Congress had been declared illegal, and a series of measures had been imposed to prevent meetings, demonstrations, and boycotts. With great apprehension, Mahatma Gandhi relaunched the Civil Disobedience Movement. The movement continued for over a year, but by 1934, it had lost its momentum.

## How Participants Saw the Movement

Let us now examine the different social groups that participated in the Civil Disobedience Movement, their motivations, ideals, and how they interpreted *swaraj*.

In the countryside, **rich peasant communities**—such as the Patidars of Gujarat and the Jats of Uttar Pradesh—were actively involved in the movement. As producers of commercial crops, they were severely affected by the trade depression and falling prices. With their cash income disappearing, they found it impossible to meet the government's revenue demands. The government's refusal to reduce the revenue demand led to widespread resentment. These rich peasants became enthusiastic supporters of the Civil Disobedience Movement, organizing their communities and, at times, compelling reluctant members to participate in boycott programmes. For them, the struggle for *swaraj* was fundamentally a fight against high revenues. However, they were deeply disappointed when the movement was called off in 1931 without any revision of revenue rates. Consequently, when the movement was restarted in 1932, many of them refused to participate.

The **poorer peasantry** were not solely interested in the reduction of revenue demands. Many were small tenants cultivating land rented from landlords. As the Depression persisted and cash incomes dwindled, these small tenants struggled to pay their rent. They sought the remission of unpaid rent to the landlord. They joined a variety of radical movements, often led by Socialists and Communists. Apprehensive of raising issues that might displease rich peasants and landlords, the Congress was reluctant to support 'no rent' campaigns in most places. Thus, the relationship between the poor peasants and the Congress remained tenuous.

### Box 1

### 'To the altar of this revolution we have brought our youth as incense'

Many nationalists believed that the struggle against the British could not be won through non-violence. In 1928, the **Hindustan Socialist Republican Army (HSRA)** was founded at a meeting in Ferozeshah Kotla ground in Delhi. Among its leaders were **Bhagat Singh, Jatin Das**, and **Ajoy Ghosh**. In a series of dramatic actions across India, the HSRA targeted symbols of British power. In April 1929, Bhagat Singh and Batukeswar Dutta threw a bomb in the Legislative Assembly. In the same year, there was an attempt to blow up the train carrying Lord Irwin. Bhagat Singh was 23 when he was tried and executed by the colonial government. During his trial, Bhagat Singh stated that he did not wish to glorify 'the cult of the bomb and pistol' but sought a revolution in society:
'Revolution is the inalienable right of mankind. Freedom is the imprescriptible birthright of all. The labourer is the real sustainer of society ... To the altar of this revolution we have brought our youth as incense, for no sacrifice is too great for so magnificent a cause. We are content. We await the advent of revolution. Inquilab Zindabad!'

What about the **business classes**? How did they relate to the Civil Disobedience Movement? During the First World War, Indian merchants and industrialists had accumulated huge profits and gained significant power. Keen on expanding their businesses, they now reacted against colonial policies that restricted business activities. They sought protection against the import of foreign goods and a rupee-sterling foreign exchange ratio that would discourage imports. To organize business interests, they formed the **Indian Industrial and Commercial Congress** in 1920 and the **Federation of the Indian Chamber of Commerce and Industries (FICCI)** in 1927. Led by prominent industrialists like **Purshottamdas Thakurdas** and **G. D. Birla**, industrialists attacked colonial control over the Indian economy and supported the Civil Disobedience Movement when it was initially launched. They provided financial assistance and refused to buy or sell imported goods. Most businessmen viewed *swaraj* as a time when colonial restrictions on business would cease, and trade and industry would flourish without constraints. However, after the failure of the Round Table Conference, business groups were no longer uniformly enthusiastic. They were apprehensive about the spread of militant activities, worried about prolonged business disruptions, and concerned about the growing influence of socialism among the younger members of the Congress.

The **industrial working classes** did not participate in the Civil Disobedience Movement in large numbers, except in the Nagpur region. As industrialists grew closer to the Congress, workers remained largely aloof. Despite this, some workers did participate in the Civil Disobedience Movement, selectively adopting certain ideas from the Gandhian programme, such as the boycott of foreign goods, as part of their own movements against low wages and poor working conditions. There were strikes by railway workers in 1930 and dockworkers in 1932. In 1930, thousands of workers in Chotanagpur tin mines wore Gandhi caps and participated in protest rallies and boycott campaigns. However, the Congress was reluctant to include workers' demands as part of its struggle programme, fearing that this would alienate industrialists and divide the anti-imperial forces.

Another important feature of the Civil Disobedience Movement was the **large-scale participation of women**. During Gandhiji's Salt March, thousands of women emerged from their homes to listen to him. They participated in protest marches, manufactured salt, and picketed foreign cloth and liquor shops. Many were imprisoned. In urban areas, these women came from high-caste families; in rural areas, they belonged to rich peasant households. Moved by Gandhiji's call, they began to perceive service to the nation as a sacred duty of women. Yet, this increased public role did not necessarily translate into a radical change in the visualization of women's position. Gandhiji was convinced that it was women's duty to care for the home and hearth, and be good mothers and wives. For a long time, the Congress was hesitant to allow women to hold positions of authority within the organization, primarily favoring their symbolic presence.

## The Limits of Civil Disobedience

Not all social groups were equally moved by the abstract concept of *swaraj*. One such group was the nation's '**untouchables**', who from around the 1930s began to refer to themselves as **dalit** or oppressed. For a long time, the Congress had largely ignored the dalits, fearing that it would offend the *sanatanis* (conservative high-caste Hindus). However, Mahatma Gandhi declared that *swaraj* would not be achieved for a hundred years if untouchability was not eradicated. He referred to the 'untouchables' as **harijan**, meaning 'children of God', and organized *satyagraha* movements to secure their entry into temples and access to public wells, tanks, roads, and schools. He himself cleaned toilets to dignify the work of the *bhangi* (sweepers) and persuaded upper castes to change their hearts and abandon the 'sin of untouchability'.

### Discuss

Why did various classes and groups of Indians participate in the Civil Disobedience Movement?

> **Answer:** Various classes and groups of Indians participated in the Civil Disobedience Movement for a multitude of reasons, primarily driven by their specific grievances against British colonial rule and their interpretations of *Swaraj*:
>
> 1.  **Rich Peasants (e.g., Patidars of Gujarat, Jats of UP):**
>     *   **Motivation:** They were commercial crop producers severely affected by the global economic depression and falling prices, making it impossible to pay the high government revenue demands.
>     *   **Interpretation of Swaraj:** For them, *Swaraj* meant a struggle against high revenues and the expectation of revenue reduction.
>     *   **Participation:** They enthusiastically supported the movement, organized their communities, and participated in boycott programs.
>
> 2.  **Poorer Peasantry:**
>     *   **Motivation:** Besides high revenue demands, they were small tenants struggling to pay rent to landlords due to dwindling cash incomes during the Depression.
>     *   **Interpretation of Swaraj:** They wanted unpaid rent to landlords to be remitted and sought broader land reforms.
>     *   **Participation:** They joined radical movements, often led by Socialists and Communists, as the Congress was reluctant to support 'no rent' campaigns.
>
> 3.  **Business Classes (Merchants and Industrialists):**
>     *   **Motivation:** They had made significant profits during WWI and wanted to expand their businesses but were hindered by colonial policies that restricted trade, such as uncontrolled imports and unfavorable exchange rates.
>     *   **Interpretation of Swaraj:** They envisioned *Swaraj* as a time when colonial restrictions on business would be lifted, allowing trade and industry to flourish without constraints.
>     *   **Participation:** They formed organizations like FICCI, attacked colonial economic control, provided financial assistance to the movement, and boycotted imported goods.
>
> 4.  **Industrial Working Classes:**
>     *   **Motivation:** They sought to improve their low wages and poor working conditions.
>     *   **Interpretation of Swaraj:** They selectively adopted Gandhian ideas like the boycott of foreign goods as part of their own struggles.
>     *   **Participation:** Their participation was limited, but some engaged in strikes (railway workers, dockworkers) and protest rallies, often wearing Gandhi caps. The Congress, however, did not fully incorporate their specific demands.
>
> 5.  **Women (from high-caste urban families and rich peasant rural households):**
>     *   **Motivation:** Moved by Gandhiji's call, they saw participation as a sacred duty to the nation.
>     *   **Interpretation of Swaraj:** For many, it was an opportunity to step out of their homes and participate in public life for the first time, contributing to a national cause.
>     *   **Participation:** They participated in protest marches, manufactured salt, picketed foreign cloth and liquor shops, and went to jail in large numbers.
>
> 6.  **Dalits (Depressed Classes):**
>     *   **Motivation:** They sought political empowerment to resolve their social disabilities and end untouchability.
>     *   **Interpretation of Swaraj:** They desired reserved seats in educational institutions and separate electorates for legislative councils, believing political representation was key to their upliftment.
>     *   **Participation:** Their participation was limited, especially where their organizations were strong, as they often clashed with the Congress over the means to achieve their goals.
>
> In essence, while the overarching goal was *Swaraj*, each group tailored this concept to their immediate needs and grievances, leading to diverse forms and levels of participation in the Civil Disobedience Movement.

However, many dalit leaders sought a different political solution to their community's problems. They began organizing themselves, demanding reserved seats in educational institutions and a **separate electorate** that would choose dalit members for legislative councils. They believed that political empowerment would resolve their social disabilities. Consequently, dalit participation in the Civil Disobedience Movement was limited, particularly in the Maharashtra and Nagpur regions where their organizations were quite strong.

**Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**, who organized the dalits into the **Depressed Classes Association** in 1930, clashed with Mahatma Gandhi at the second Round Table Conference by demanding separate electorates for dalits. When the British government conceded Ambedkar's demand, Gandhiji began a fast unto death. He believed that separate electorates for dalits would impede their integration into society. Ambedkar ultimately accepted Gandhiji's position, resulting in the **Poona Pact of September 1932**. This pact granted the Depressed Classes (later known as the Scheduled Castes) reserved seats in provincial and central legislative councils, but they were to be voted in by the general electorate. The dalit movement, however, continued to be apprehensive of the Congress-led national movement.

Some of the Muslim political organizations in India also responded lukewarmly to the Civil Disobedience Movement. After the decline of the Non-Cooperation-Khilafat Movement, a large segment of Muslims felt alienated from the Congress. From the mid-1920s, the Congress became more visibly associated with openly Hindu religious nationalist groups like the **Hindu Mahasabha**. As relations between Hindus and Muslims deteriorated, each community organized religious processions with militant fervor, provoking Hindu-Muslim communal clashes and riots in various cities. Every riot further deepened the distance between the two communities.

The Congress and the Muslim League made efforts to renegotiate an alliance, and in 1927, it seemed that such unity could be forged. The key differences revolved around the question of representation in the future elected assemblies. **Muhammad Ali Jinnah**, one of the leaders of the Muslim League, was willing to abandon the demand for separate electorates if Muslims were guaranteed reserved seats in the Central Assembly and representation proportional to their population in Muslim-dominated provinces (Bengal and Punjab). Negotiations over representation continued, but all hope of resolving the issue at the **All Parties Conference in 1928** vanished when **M.R. Jayakar** of the Hindu Mahasabha strongly opposed efforts at compromise.

### Source D

In 1930, Sir Muhammad Iqbal, as president of the Muslim League, reiterated the importance of separate electorates for the Muslims as an important safeguard for their minority political interests. His statement is supposed to have provided the intellectual justification for the Pakistan demand that came up in subsequent years. This is what he said:
'I have no hesitation in declaring that if the principle that the Indian Muslim is entitled to full and free development on the lines of his own culture and tradition in his own Indian home-lands is recognised as the basis of a permanent communal settlement, he will be ready to stake his all for the freedom of India. The principle that each group is entitled to free development on its own lines is not inspired by any feeling of narrow communalism ... A community which is inspired by feelings of ill-will towards other communities is low and ignoble. I entertain the highest respect for the customs, laws, religions and social institutions of other communities. Nay, it is my duty according to the teachings of the Quran, even to defend their places of worship, if need be. Yet I love the communal group which is the source of life and behaviour and which has formed me what I am by giving me its religion, its literature, its thought, its culture and thereby its whole past as a living operative factor in my present consciousness ...
'Communalism in its higher aspect, then, is indispensable to the formation of a harmonious whole in a country like India. The units of Indian society are not territorial as in European countries ... The principle of European democracy cannot be applied to India without recognising the fact of communal groups. The Muslim demand for the creation of a Muslim India within India is, therefore, perfectly justified...
'The Hindu thinks that separate electorates are contrary to the spirit of true nationalism, because he understands the word "nation" to mean a kind of universal amalgamation in which no communal entity ought to retain its private individuality. Such a state of things, however, does not exist. India is a land of racial and religious variety. Add to this the general economic inferiority of the Muslims, their enormous debt, especially in the Punjab, and their insufficient majorities in some of the provinces, as at present constituted and you will begin to see clearly the meaning of our anxiety to retain separate electorates.'

### Discuss

Read the Source D carefully. Do you agree with Iqbal's idea of communalism? Can you define communalism in a different way?

> **Answer:**
> **Do you agree with Iqbal's idea of communalism?**
> Muhammad Iqbal's idea of communalism, as expressed in Source D, is a complex one. He argues that communalism, in its "higher aspect," is "indispensable to the formation of a harmonious whole in a country like India" because Indian society is composed of distinct "communal groups" rather than being purely territorial like European countries. He asserts that each group has a right to "full and free development on the lines of its own culture and tradition" and that recognizing this is essential for a permanent communal settlement. He explicitly states that his view is not inspired by "ill-will towards other communities" and emphasizes respect for other faiths.
>
> While Iqbal presents this as a way to ensure the cultural and political distinctiveness of Muslims within India, it is difficult to fully agree with his definition of communalism as "indispensable" for a harmonious whole in the context of modern nation-building. His argument, though framed as a safeguard for minority rights and cultural preservation, inherently emphasizes differences and distinct political interests based on religious identity. This approach, historically, often leads to divisions rather than true harmony, as it can foster suspicion and competition between groups rather than a shared national identity transcending religious lines. The idea that "European democracy cannot be applied to India without recognising the fact of communal groups" ultimately paved the way for arguments that religious groups could not coexist within a single political framework without specific, often separatist, provisions.
>
> **Can you define communalism in a different way?**
> Yes, communalism can be defined in a more critical and widely understood way as:
>
> **Communalism (Alternative Definition):** *A political ideology that promotes the idea that society is divided into distinct, antagonistic religious communities, whose interests are inherently different and often opposed. It asserts that loyalty to one's religious group should take precedence over loyalty to the nation or other social identities. In its extreme forms, communalism can lead to prejudice, discrimination, and violence against members of other religious groups, hindering national unity and secular governance.*
>
> This definition highlights the divisive and often conflict-ridden nature of communalism, contrasting with Iqbal's more benign portrayal of it as a necessary recognition of group identity for harmonious coexistence. While recognizing cultural diversity is important, communalism, in its practical manifestation, often politicizes religious identity to the extent of creating social and political fragmentation.
