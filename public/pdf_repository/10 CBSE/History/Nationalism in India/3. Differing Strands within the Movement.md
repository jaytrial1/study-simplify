This section details the diverse participation in the Non-Cooperation-Khilafat Movement, illustrating how various social groups interpreted the concept of *Swaraj* according to their specific aspirations and grievances. It covers the movement's unfolding in urban centers, its spread to rural areas among peasants and tribals, and its impact on plantation workers, highlighting the varied understanding of freedom.

# Differing Strands within the Movement

The Non-Cooperation-Khilafat Movement commenced in January 1921. Various social groups participated, each driven by their own specific aspirations. While all responded to the call for **Swaraj**, the term held different meanings for different people.

## The Movement in the Towns

The movement began with the participation of the middle class in the cities. Thousands of students abandoned government-controlled schools and colleges, headmasters and teachers resigned, and lawyers relinquished their legal practices. Council elections were largely boycotted in most provinces, with the notable exception of Madras. In Madras, the **Justice Party**, representing non-Brahmans, believed that entering the council offered a means to gain power, which was typically monopolized by Brahmans.

The economic impact of non-cooperation was even more dramatic. Foreign goods were boycotted, liquor shops were **picketed**, and foreign cloth was burned in massive bonfires. The import of foreign cloth was halved between 1921 and 1922, its value plummeting from Rs 102 crore to Rs 57 crore. In many areas, merchants and traders refused to deal in foreign goods or finance foreign trade. As the boycott movement gained traction, people began discarding imported clothes and exclusively wearing Indian ones, leading to a surge in the production of Indian textile mills and handlooms.

**New words**
*   **Picket:** A form of demonstration or protest by which people block the entrance to a shop, factory or office.

However, the movement in the cities gradually lost momentum for several reasons. **Khadi** cloth was often more expensive than mass-produced mill cloth, making it unaffordable for the poor. Consequently, they could not sustain the boycott of mill cloth for extended periods. Similarly, the boycott of British institutions posed a challenge. For the movement to succeed, alternative Indian institutions needed to be established to replace the British ones, but these were slow to materialize. As a result, students and teachers gradually returned to government schools, and lawyers resumed their work in government courts.

## Rebellion in the Countryside

From the cities, the Non-Cooperation Movement extended its reach into the countryside, incorporating the struggles of peasants and tribals that had been developing in various parts of India in the years following the war.

In **Awadh**, peasants were led by **Baba Ramchandra**, a *sanyasi* who had previously worked as an indentured labourer in Fiji. The movement in Awadh was primarily directed against the **talukdars** and landlords who imposed exorbitantly high rents and various other cesses on the peasants. Peasants were often forced to contribute **begar** (unpaid labor) on landlords' farms. As tenants, they lacked security of tenure, being regularly evicted to prevent them from acquiring any rights over the leased land. The peasant movement demanded:
*   Reduction of revenue.
*   Abolition of begar.
*   Social boycott of oppressive landlords.

In many places, *nai-dhobi bandhs* (barber-washermen shutdowns) were organized by panchayats to deprive landlords of even basic services. In June 1920, Jawaharlal Nehru began visiting villages in Awadh, engaging with villagers and trying to understand their grievances. By October, the **Oudh Kisan Sabha** was established, headed by Jawaharlal Nehru, Baba Ramchandra, and others. Within a month, over 300 branches had been set up in the surrounding villages. Consequently, when the Non-Cooperation Movement began the following year, the Congress sought to integrate the Awadh peasant struggle into the broader national movement.

**New words**
*   **Begar:** Labour that villagers were forced to contribute without any payment.

However, the peasant movement developed in ways that the Congress leadership found unsettling. As the movement spread in 1921, the houses of talukdars and merchants were attacked, bazaars were looted, and grain hoards were seized. In many areas, local leaders informed peasants that Gandhiji had declared that no taxes were to be paid and that land would be redistributed among the poor. The name of the Mahatma was thus invoked to legitimize all actions and aspirations.

> **Answer:** If I were a peasant in Uttar Pradesh in 1920, I would have responded to Gandhiji's call for Swaraj with immense hope and enthusiasm, and I would have joined the movement.
>
> **Reasons for my response:**
> 1.  **Relief from Exploitation:** My primary concern would be the oppressive demands of landlords and talukdars, who charge exorbitant rents and force us into *begar* (unpaid labor). Gandhiji's call for Swaraj, interpreted locally as an end to taxes and redistribution of land, would resonate deeply with my daily struggles against exploitation.
> 2.  **Abolition of Begar:** The forced labor I have to provide without payment is a heavy burden. The demand for the abolition of *begar* directly addresses my immediate hardship and offers the promise of dignity and fair compensation for my labor.
> 3.  **Security of Tenure:** As a tenant, I constantly face eviction, preventing me from developing any rights over the land I cultivate. The idea of *Swaraj* would bring the hope of land reform and secure tenure, allowing me to invest my efforts without fear of displacement.
> 4.  **Collective Strength:** Before Gandhiji's call, our struggles were often isolated and easily suppressed. The Non-Cooperation Movement, being a broad-based, nationwide movement, would offer a sense of collective strength and solidarity, making me feel that our grievances are part of a larger, powerful struggle that has a real chance of success.
> 5.  **Moral Authority of Gandhi:** The stories of Mahatma Gandhi's successes in Champaran and Kheda, and his emphasis on non-violence and truth, would inspire trust and belief that his leadership could indeed bring about a better future for people like me. His name would sanction our actions, giving us moral legitimacy.

### Source B

On 6 January 1921, the police in United Provinces fired at peasants near Rae Bareli. Jawaharlal Nehru wanted to go to the place of firing, but was stopped by the police. Agitated and angry, Nehru addressed the peasants who gathered around him. This is how he later described the meeting:
'They behaved as brave men, calm and unruffled in the face of danger. I do not know how they felt but I know what my feelings were. For a moment my blood was up, non-violence was almost forgotten - but for a moment only. The thought of the great leader, who by God's goodness has been sent to lead us to victory, came to me, and I saw the kisans seated and standing near me, less excited, more peaceful than I was - and the moment of weakness passed, I spoke to them in all humility on non-violence - I needed the lesson more than they - and they heeded me and peacefully dispersed.'
Quoted in Sarvapalli Gopal, Jawaharlal Nehru: A Biography, Vol. I.

In 1928, Vallabhbhai Patel led the peasant movement in Bardoli, a taluka in Gujarat, against the enhancement of land revenue. Known as the **Bardoli Satyagraha**, this movement achieved success under Vallabhbhai Patel's capable leadership. The struggle garnered widespread publicity and generated immense sympathy across many parts of India.

Tribal peasants interpreted the message of Mahatma Gandhi and the idea of *swaraj* in yet another distinct way. In the **Gudem Hills of Andhra Pradesh**, for instance, a militant guerrilla movement emerged in the early 1920s—a form of struggle that the Congress could not endorse. Here, as in other forest regions, the colonial government had closed large forest areas, prohibiting people from entering the forests to graze their cattle or collect fuelwood and fruits. This measure infuriated the hill people, as it not only affected their livelihoods but also denied their traditional rights. When the government began forcing them to contribute *begar* for road building, the hill people revolted.

The person who emerged to lead them was an intriguing figure: **Alluri Sitaram Raju**. Raju claimed to possess a variety of special powers, including the ability to make accurate astrological predictions, heal people, and even survive bullet shots. Captivated by Raju, the rebels proclaimed him an incarnation of God. Raju spoke of Mahatma Gandhi's greatness, claimed inspiration from the Non-Cooperation Movement, and encouraged people to wear *khadi* and abstain from drinking. However, he simultaneously asserted that India could only be liberated through the use of force, not non-violence. The Gudem rebels attacked police stations, attempted to kill British officials, and waged guerrilla warfare to achieve *swaraj*. Raju was captured and executed in 1924, eventually becoming a folk hero.

## Swaraj in the Plantations

Workers also developed their own understanding of Mahatma Gandhi and the concept of *swaraj*. For **plantation workers in Assam**, freedom meant the right to move freely in and out of the confined space in which they were enclosed, and it signified retaining a connection with their native village. Under the **Inland Emigration Act of 1859**, plantation workers were not permitted to leave the tea gardens without permission, and such permission was rarely granted.

Upon hearing of the Non-Cooperation Movement, thousands of workers defied the authorities, abandoned the plantations, and headed for their homes. They believed that **Gandhi Raj** was imminent and that everyone would be granted land in their own villages. However, they never reached their destinations. Stranded en route by a railway and steamer strike, they were apprehended by the police and brutally beaten.

> **Similar Example from the National Movement in Indo-China (Chapter 2):**

A more direct parallel to "captured and put to death" might be the numerous Vietnamese nationalists and revolutionaries who were guillotined or executed by the French colonial regime, such as **Nguyen Thai Hoc**, a leader of the Vietnamese Nationalist Party (VNQDD), who was executed in 1930 after the failure of the Yen Bai mutiny. The visions of these movements were not rigidly defined by the Congress programme. Participants interpreted the term *swaraj* in their own unique ways, often imagining it as a time when all their suffering and troubles would cease. Nevertheless, when tribals chanted Gandhiji's name and raised slogans demanding 'Swatantra Bharat' (Free India), they were emotionally connecting to a wider, all-India agitation. When they acted in the name of Mahatma Gandhi or linked their movements to that of the Congress, they were identifying with a struggle that transcended the boundaries of their immediate locality.
