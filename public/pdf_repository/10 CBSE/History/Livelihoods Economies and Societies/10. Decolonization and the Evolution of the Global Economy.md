This section explores the challenges faced by newly independent nations after decolonization, their demand for a New International Economic Order (NIEO), and the eventual shift in the global financial system with the end of Bretton Woods, leading to the rise of multinational corporations and a new phase of globalization.

# Decolonization and the Evolution of the Global Economy

## The Challenges of Decolonization

When the Second World War ended, large parts of the world were still under European colonial rule. Over the next two decades most colonies in Asia and Africa emerged as free, independent nations. They were, however, overburdened by poverty and a lack of resources, and their economies and societies were handicapped by long periods of colonial rule.

The IMF and the World Bank were designed to meet the financial needs of the industrial countries. They were not equipped to cope with the challenge of poverty and lack of development in the former colonies. But as Europe and Japan rapidly rebuilt their economies, they grew less dependent on the IMF and the World Bank. Thus from the late 1950s the Bretton Woods institutions began to shift their attention more towards developing countries.

As colonies, many of the less developed regions of the world had been part of Western empires. Now, ironically, as newly independent countries facing urgent pressures to lift their populations out of poverty, they came under the guidance of international agencies dominated by the former colonial powers. Even after many years of decolonisation, the former colonial powers still controlled vital resources such as minerals and land in many of their former colonies.

Large corporations of other powerful countries, for example the US, also often managed to secure rights to exploit developing countries' natural resources very cheaply.

At the same time, most developing countries did not benefit from the fast growth the Western economies experienced in the 1950s and 1960s. Therefore they organised themselves as a group - the *Group of 77* (or *G-77*) - to demand a *new international economic order (NIEO)*. By the NIEO they meant a system that would give them real control over their natural resources, more development assistance, fairer prices for raw materials, and better access for their manufactured goods in developed countries' markets.

### Key Concepts & Examples

**What are MNCs?**

*Multinational corporations (MNCs)* are large companies that operate in several countries at the same time. The first MNCs were established in the 1920s. Many more came up in the 1950s and 1960s as US businesses expanded worldwide and Western Europe and Japan also recovered to become powerful industrial economies. The worldwide spread of MNCs was a notable feature of the 1950s and 1960s. This was partly because high *import tariffs* imposed by different governments forced MNCs to locate their manufacturing operations and become 'domestic producers' in as many countries as possible.

**New words**

*   **Tariff:** Tax imposed on a country's imports from the rest of the world. Tariffs are levied at the point of entry, i.e., at the border or the airport.

## The End of Bretton Woods and the Rise of Globalization

Despite years of stable and rapid growth, not all was well in this post-war world. From the 1960s the rising costs of its overseas involvements weakened the US's finances and competitive strength. The US dollar now no longer commanded confidence as the world's principal currency. It could not maintain its value in relation to gold. This eventually led to the collapse of the system of *fixed exchange rates* and the introduction of a system of *floating exchange rates*.

From the mid-1970s the international financial system also changed in important ways. Earlier, developing countries could turn to international institutions for loans and development assistance. But now they were forced to borrow from Western commercial banks and private lending institutions. This led to periodic *debt crises* in the developing world, and lower incomes and increased poverty, especially in Africa and Latin America.

The industrial world was also hit by unemployment that began rising from the mid-1970s and remained high until the early 1990s. From the late 1970s MNCs also began to shift production operations to low-wage Asian countries.

China had been cut off from the post-war world economy since its revolution in 1949. But new economic policies in China and the collapse of the Soviet Union and Soviet-style communism in Eastern Europe brought many countries back into the fold of the world economy.

Wages were relatively low in countries like China. Thus they became attractive destinations for investment by foreign MNCs competing to capture world markets. Have you noticed that most of the TVs, mobile phones, and toys we see in the shops seem to be made in China? This is because of the low-cost structure of the Chinese economy, most importantly its low wages.

The relocation of industry to low-wage countries stimulated world trade and capital flows. In the last two decades the world's economic geography has been transformed as countries such as India, China and Brazil have undergone rapid economic transformation.

**New words**

*   **Exchange rates:** They link national currencies for purposes of international trade. There are broadly two kinds of exchange rates: fixed exchange rate and floating exchange rate.
*   **Fixed exchange rates:** When exchange rates are fixed and governments intervene to prevent movements in them.
*   **Flexible or floating exchange rates:** These rates fluctuate depending on demand and supply of currencies in foreign exchange markets, in principle without interference by governments.

1.  Give two examples of different types of global exchanges which took place before the seventeenth century, choosing one example from Asia and one from the Americas.

2.  Explain how the global transfer of disease in the pre-modern world helped in the colonisation of the Americas.
    > **Answer:** The global transfer of disease, particularly smallpox, played a crucial role in the colonization of the Americas. Due to millions of years of isolation, the indigenous inhabitants of America had no natural immunity to the diseases carried by European conquerors. When diseases like smallpox were introduced, they spread rapidly and decimated entire communities, often ahead of the Europeans themselves. This widespread death and weakening of native populations significantly reduced their ability to resist the European invaders, effectively paving the way for easier conquest and colonization, as the most powerful weapon of the Spanish conquerors was not military but biological.

3.  Write a note to explain the effects of the following:
    a) The British government's decision to abolish the Corn Laws.
    > **Answer:** The abolition of the *Corn Laws* led to a significant influx of cheaper imported food into Britain. This resulted in British agriculture becoming uncompetitive, leading to vast areas of land being left uncultivated and thousands of agricultural workers losing their jobs. Many of these displaced workers migrated to cities or overseas in search of employment. Conversely, cheaper food prices led to increased consumption in Britain, and the demand for food from abroad stimulated agricultural expansion and infrastructure development (like railways and ports) in other parts of the world, such as America and Australia.

    b) The coming of rinderpest to Africa.
    > **Answer:** The arrival of *Rinderpest* (cattle plague) in Africa in the late 1880s had a devastating impact. It killed about 90% of the cattle, which were central to African livelihoods, sustained by land and livestock. This loss destroyed the traditional economic base of many African communities, forcing them into the labor market. European colonial governments, planters, and mine owners capitalized on this crisis by monopolizing the remaining scarce cattle resources, thereby strengthening their power and coercing Africans into working for wages on plantations and in mines, further facilitating colonial conquest and control.

    c) The death of men of working-age in Europe because of the World War.
    > **Answer:** The death and injury of millions of men of working-age during the First World War significantly reduced the able-bodied workforce in Europe. This led to a decline in household incomes after the war due to fewer earners in families. It also necessitated a restructuring of industries to produce war-related goods and a reorganization of entire societies, as women stepped into jobs traditionally held by men, profoundly changing labor demographics and social roles. The massive human cost also contributed to widespread anxiety and uncertainty about work in the post-war period.

    d) The Great Depression on the Indian economy.
    > **Answer:** The *Great Depression* severely affected the Indian economy, demonstrating its integration into the global system. India's exports and imports halved between 1928 and 1934, and agricultural prices, like wheat, plunged by 50%. This disproportionately hit peasants and farmers, who faced declining incomes but no reduction in colonial government revenue demands, leading to deep indebtedness. Jute producers in Bengal, for instance, saw prices crash. Peasants exhausted savings, mortgaged lands, and sold precious metals, turning India into a gold exporter which aided Britain's recovery but did little for rural India, sparking widespread unrest and contributing to the launch of Mahatma Gandhi's Civil Disobedience Movement.

    e) The decision of MNCs to relocate production to Asian countries.
    > **Answer:** The decision of *MNCs* to relocate production operations to low-wage Asian countries from the late 1970s had several significant effects. It stimulated world trade and capital flows, transforming the global economic geography. Countries like China, India, and Brazil experienced rapid economic transformation due to foreign investment and increased manufacturing. This shift allowed MNCs to lower production costs and capture world markets more effectively, leading to a surge in the availability of cheaper manufactured goods globally, while also contributing to economic growth and development in the recipient Asian nations.

4.  Give two examples from history to show the impact of technology on food availability.
    > **Answer:**
    > 1.  **Refrigerated Ships:** The development of *refrigerated ships* in the 1870s revolutionized the meat trade. Before this, live animals were shipped from America to Europe, often dying or losing weight during the long voyage. Refrigerated ships allowed meat to be slaughtered at the source (e.g., America, Australia, New Zealand) and transported frozen, significantly reducing shipping costs and meat prices in Europe. This made meat accessible to the European poor, diversifying their diet beyond just bread and potatoes.
    > 2.  **Railways and Steamships:** The expansion of *railways* and the use of *steamships* in the nineteenth century drastically improved the transport of food grains. Railways linked agricultural regions to ports, and larger, faster steamships moved food more cheaply and quickly across oceans. This enabled food to be sourced from thousands of miles away (e.g., American wheat to Britain), leading to a global agricultural economy and increasing food availability in urban centers.

5.  What is meant by the Bretton Woods Agreement?
    > **Answer:** The *Bretton Woods Agreement* refers to the framework for the post-war international economic system established at the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference in July 1944 at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, USA. Its main aim was to ensure economic stability and full employment in the industrial world, learning from the inter-war period's instability. This agreement led to the creation of the *International Monetary Fund (IMF)* to manage external surpluses and deficits, and the *International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (World Bank)* to finance post-war reconstruction. The system was based on *fixed exchange rates*, where national currencies were pegged to the US dollar, which in turn was anchored to gold.

7.  Explain the three types of movements or flows within international economic exchange. Find one example of each type of flow which involved India and Indians, and write a short account of it.
    > **Answer:** The three types of movements or 'flows' within international economic exchange, as identified by economists, are:
    > 1.  **Flow of Trade:** This refers to the exchange of goods and commodities between countries.
    > 2.  **Flow of Labour:** This involves the migration of people from one country to another in search of employment or better opportunities.
    > 3.  **Movement of Capital:** This signifies the flow of investments, loans, and other financial assets across national borders, for short-term or long-term purposes.
    >
    > **Examples involving India and Indians:**
    > *   **Flow of Trade (Opium):** During the 19th century, India became a crucial part of a triangular trade network involving Britain and China. Britain cultivated opium in India and exported it rapidly to China, especially from the 1820s. The money earned from these opium sales was then used by Britain to finance its tea and other imports from China. This flow of opium from India to China was a massive trade flow that significantly impacted India's economy under colonial rule.
    > *   **Flow of Labour (Indentured Labourers):** From the mid-19th century, hundreds of thousands of Indian laborers migrated as *indentured workers* to various British colonies, including the Caribbean islands (like Trinidad, Guyana, Surinam), Mauritius, Fiji, Ceylon, and Malaya. They were recruited to work on plantations, in mines, and on railway construction projects, often due to declining cottage industries, rising land rents, and indebtedness in their home regions of eastern Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and Tamil Nadu. This represented a significant flow of Indian labor across oceans.
    > *   **Movement of Capital (Indian Bankers and Traders):** Indian bankers and traders, such as the *Shikaripuri Shroffs* and *Nattukottai Chettiars*, played a vital role in the movement of capital. They financed export agriculture in Central and Southeast Asia, using their own funds or loans from European banks. They developed sophisticated systems to transfer money over large distances and even indigenous forms of corporate organization. This demonstrates Indian capital flowing to support economic activities in other regions, often following European colonial expansion.

8.  Explain the causes of the Great Depression.
    > **Answer:** The *Great Depression*, which began around 1929, was caused by a combination of several interconnected factors:
    > 1.  **Agricultural Overproduction:** Following World War I, wheat production in countries like Canada, America, and Australia expanded dramatically to meet wartime demand. After the war, when European production revived, this led to a massive glut in wheat output. As agricultural prices slumped due to oversupply, farmers tried to produce even more to maintain their overall income, further worsening the glut and pushing prices down, leading to widespread rural indebtedness and farm produce rotting due to lack of buyers.
    > 2.  **Withdrawal of US Loans:** In the mid-1920s, many countries, particularly in Europe, financed their investments and economic recovery through substantial loans from the US. However, at the first sign of economic trouble, US overseas lenders panicked and drastically cut back their loans. This sudden withdrawal of crucial capital led to an acute financial crisis in dependent countries, causing major bank failures and currency collapses (e.g., the British pound sterling).
    > 3.  **US Domestic Economic Problems:** The US itself experienced severe internal problems. With falling prices and the prospect of a depression, US banks slashed domestic lending and recalled existing loans. This led to widespread failures of farms and businesses, as well as households being ruined and unable to repay debts. Consequently, thousands of US banks went bankrupt and closed, and companies collapsed, leading to soaring unemployment and a sharp decline in consumption, as the consumer boom of the 1920s vanished.
    > 4.  **Protectionist Policies:** The US attempt to protect its domestic economy during the depression by doubling import duties further exacerbated the crisis globally. This protectionist measure severely reduced world trade, deepening the economic downturn in other countries.

9.  Explain what is referred to as the G-77 countries. In what ways can G-77 be seen as a reaction to the activities of the Bretton Woods twins?
    > **Answer:** The *G-77* (Group of 77) refers to a group of developing countries that organized themselves to demand a *New International Economic Order (NIEO)*. These countries, mostly newly independent nations from Asia and Africa after World War II, faced immense challenges like poverty and lack of resources due to long periods of colonial rule.
    >
    > The G-77 can be seen as a reaction to the activities of the *Bretton Woods twins* (the IMF and the World Bank) in several ways:
    > 1.  **Designed for Industrialized Nations:** The Bretton Woods institutions were primarily designed to meet the financial needs and promote stability in the industrialized Western countries. They were not initially equipped or focused on addressing the unique challenges of poverty and underdevelopment faced by the newly independent former colonies.
    > 2.  **Domination by Former Colonial Powers:** Despite decolonization, decision-making in the IMF and World Bank remained controlled by the Western industrial powers, including the former colonial powers. This meant that developing countries, seeking guidance and assistance, often found themselves under the influence of the very nations that had previously exploited their resources.
    > 3.  **Lack of Control over Resources:** Even after independence, former colonial powers and large corporations from powerful countries often retained control over vital resources in developing nations. The NIEO demanded by the G-77 explicitly sought *real control over their natural resources*, which was a direct response to this ongoing economic dominance.
    > 4.  **Unfair Trade and Aid:** The G-77 also demanded more development assistance, fairer prices for their raw materials, and better access for their manufactured goods in developed countries' markets. This reflected their dissatisfaction with the existing global economic system, which they felt did not adequately benefit them and perpetuated economic imbalances that the Bretton Woods institutions, in their initial operation, did not effectively address for developing nations.
