This section explores how artists in the 18th and 19th centuries personified nations as female figures, using allegories and symbols to give abstract ideas like Liberty and the Republic a concrete and relatable form.

# Visualising the Nation

While it is easy enough to represent a ruler through a portrait or a statue, how does one go about giving a face to a nation? Artists in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries found a way out by personifying a nation. In other words they represented a country as if it were a person. Nations were then portrayed as female figures. The female form that was chosen to personify the nation did not stand for any particular woman in real life; rather it sought to give the abstract idea of the nation a concrete form. That is, the female figure became an *allegory* of the nation. An **allegory** is when an abstract idea (for instance, greed, envy, freedom, liberty) is expressed through a person or a thing. An allegorical story has two meanings, one literal and one symbolic.

You will recall that during the French Revolution artists used the female allegory to portray ideas such as Liberty, Justice and the Republic. These ideals were represented through specific objects or symbols. As you would remember, the attributes of Liberty are the red cap, or the broken chain, while Justice is generally a blindfolded woman carrying a pair of weighing scales.

Similar female allegories were invented by artists in the nineteenth century to represent the nation. In France she was christened Marianne, a popular Christian name, which underlined the idea of a people's nation. Her characteristics were drawn from those of Liberty and the Republic - the red cap, the tricolour, the cockade. Statues of Marianne were erected in public squares to remind the public of the national symbol of unity and to persuade them to identify with it. Marianne images were marked on coins and stamps.

## Meanings of the symbols

| Attribute | Significance |
| :-- | :-- |
| Broken chains | Being freed |
| Breastplate with eagle | Symbol of the German empire - strength |
| Crown of oak leaves | Heroism |
| Sword | Readiness to fight |
| Olive branch around the sword | Willingness to make peace |
| Black, red and gold tricolour | Flag of the liberal-nationalists in 1848, banned by the Dukes of the German states |
| Rays of the rising sun | Beginning of a new era |

### Activity

With the help of the chart in Box 3, identify the attributes of Veit's Germania and interpret the symbolic meaning of the painting. In an earlier allegorical rendering of 1836, Veit had portrayed the Kaiser's crown at the place where he has now located the broken chain. Explain the significance of this change.
> **Answer:**
> **Attributes of Veit's Germania (Fig. 17) and their symbolic meaning:**
> *   **Black, red, and gold flag:** Represents the flag of the liberal-nationalists in 1848, symbolizing their hopes for a unified, democratic Germany.
> *   **Crown of oak leaves:** Symbolizes heroism, a key virtue in German culture.
> *   **Breastplate with eagle:** Symbol of the German empire, representing strength and national power.
> *   **Sword:** Signifies readiness to fight and defend the nation.
> *   **Olive branch around the sword:** Indicates a willingness to make peace, suggesting that strength is for defense and peace.
> *   **Broken chains (at her feet):** Symbolizes being freed from oppression or political fragmentation.
> *   **Radiant background (rays of the rising sun):** Represents the beginning of a new era, hope, and enlightenment.
>
> **Significance of the change from Kaiser's crown (1836) to broken chain (1848):**
> *   In 1836, the **Kaiser's crown** would have symbolized the aspiration for a unified German Empire under a strong monarch, or perhaps a lament for the *absence* of such a unifying figure. It might have represented the old, monarchical order or the desire for a strong, centralized imperial power.
> *   By 1848, the **broken chain** signifies a shift in focus. It explicitly represents the **achievement of freedom and liberation** from the autocratic rule, political fragmentation, and oppressive systems that had characterized Germany. This change emphasizes that the new vision for the nation is not just about a powerful ruler, but about the *liberty* and *self-determination* of the people. It reflects the liberal-nationalist ideals of the 1848 revolutions, which sought constitutional government and individual rights, rather than just a change of monarch.

> In Fig. 18, titled "The fallen Germania," I see a female figure, Germania, in a state of despair and defeat. Her head is bowed, her eyes are closed, and she appears to be weeping or in deep sorrow. Her crown of oak leaves is fallen or askew, and she is surrounded by symbols of loss, including a broken sword and possibly a skull. The overall mood is one of profound sadness and failure.
>
> Julius Hübner, through this allegorical vision, is likely referring to the **failure of the liberal revolutions of 1848** in the German states. Specifically, it points to the disbandment of the Frankfurt Parliament in 1849 and the rejection of the crown by King Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia. This event marked the crushing of liberal hopes for a unified, democratic Germany. The "fallen Germania" symbolizes the dashed aspirations for national unity, freedom, and constitutional government, and the return of conservative, autocratic rule.

### Activity

Look once more at Fig. 10. Imagine you were a citizen of Frankfurt in March 1848 and were present during the proceedings of the parliament. How would you (a) as a man seated in the hall of deputies, and (b) as a woman observing from the galleries, relate to the banner of Germania hanging from the ceiling?
> **Answer:**
> *   **(a) As a man seated in the hall of deputies:** I would feel an immense sense of pride and hope. The banner of Germania, with her symbols of strength, unity, and the black, red, and gold flag, would represent the dawn of a new era for Germany. It would embody the aspirations for a unified, constitutional nation-state, free from the old autocratic order. I would see it as a powerful affirmation of our collective will to forge a new German identity based on liberal principles, a testament to the efforts of the people to claim their destiny. It would inspire me with the gravity and importance of the task we, the elected representatives, were undertaking.
> *   **(b) As a woman observing from the galleries:** I would feel a mix of hope and profound frustration. The Germania banner would certainly stir national pride and the dream of a united Germany, a nation personified by a strong female figure, representing liberty. However, looking down at the assembly, where women like myself were denied the right to vote or even participate as deputies, it would highlight the bitter irony. The very symbol of the nation is female, yet the women of the nation are excluded from its political formation. The banner would be a constant reminder of the unfulfilled promise of true equality and the limitations of the liberal movement, even as I shared the patriotic sentiments for a unified Germany.
