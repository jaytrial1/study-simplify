# Division of Sectors as Organised and Unorganised

Let's examine another way of classifying economic activities: based on employment conditions and whether rules and regulations are followed.

## Kanta

Kanta works in an office from 9:30 a.m. to 5:30 p.m. She receives a regular monthly salary, provident fund, medical, and other allowances according to government rules. Sundays are paid holidays. She received an appointment letter detailing her terms and conditions.

## Kamal

Kamal, Kanta's neighbour, is a daily wage labourer in a grocery shop. He works from 7:30 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. and only gets daily wages, no other allowances. He isn't paid for days he doesn't work and has no paid leave or holidays. He received no formal employment letter and can be asked to leave anytime.

These examples show clear differences in working conditions.

Kanta works in the **organised sector**. This sector includes enterprises or workplaces with regular employment terms and assured work. They are registered with the government and follow laws like the Factories Act, Minimum Wages Act, Payment of Gratuity Act, and Shops and Establishments Act. It's called organised due to its formal processes. Even self-employed individuals in this sector must register and follow rules.

Workers in the organised sector have **security of employment**. They work fixed hours and get overtime pay for extra work. They receive benefits like paid leave, holiday pay, provident fund, gratuity, and medical benefits. Employers must ensure facilities like drinking water and a safe environment. They also receive pensions upon retirement.

Kamal works in the **unorganised sector**. This sector consists of small, scattered units largely outside government control. Rules and regulations exist but are not followed. Jobs are often low-paid and irregular. There are no provisions for overtime, paid leave, holidays, or sick leave. Employment is not secure; workers can be dismissed without reason. During slack seasons, some workers may be laid off. Conditions often depend on the employer's discretion. This sector includes many self-employed people doing small jobs like street vending or repair work. Farmers hiring casual labourers also fall partly under this.

1. Look at the following examples. Which of these are unorganised sector activities?
> **Answer:**
> (i) A teacher taking classes in a school: Organised (if in a registered school with regular terms)
> (ii) A headload worker carrying a bag of cement on his back in a market: Unorganised
> (iii) A farmer irrigating her field: Unorganised (typically self-employed or hiring casual labour)
> (iv) A doctor in a hospital treating a patient: Organised (if employed by a registered hospital)
> (v) A daily wage labourer working under a contractor: Unorganised
> (vi) A factory worker going to work in a big factory: Organised (if factory is registered)
> (vii) A handloom weaver working in her house: Unorganised

## How to Protect Workers in the Unorganised Sector?

Jobs in the organised sector are highly desired, but employment opportunities there are growing slowly. Many organised sector enterprises operate like unorganised ones to evade taxes and avoid labor laws, forcing many workers into low-paying, exploited unorganised jobs with irregular earnings, no security, and no benefits.

Since the 1990s, many organised sector workers have lost jobs and are forced into unorganised work with low earnings. Thus, protection and support are needed for unorganised sector workers.

Who are the vulnerable people needing protection?
*   **Rural areas:** Mostly landless agricultural labourers, small and marginal farmers, sharecroppers, and artisans (weavers, blacksmiths, etc.). About 80% of rural households are small/marginal farmers. They need support like timely seeds, inputs, credit, storage, and marketing.
*   **Urban areas:** Mainly workers in small-scale industry, casual workers in construction, trade, transport, street vendors, headload workers, garment makers, rag pickers. Small-scale industries need government support for raw material and marketing. Casual workers in both areas need protection.

Majority of workers from Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and backward communities are in the unorganised sector. Besides low pay and irregularity, they face social discrimination. Protecting and supporting unorganised workers is crucial for both economic and social development.

When factories close, former regular workers often end up selling goods, pushing carts, or doing other odd jobs in the unorganised sector.
