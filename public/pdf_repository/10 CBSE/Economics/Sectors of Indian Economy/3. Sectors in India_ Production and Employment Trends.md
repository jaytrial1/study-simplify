## Rising Importance of the Tertiary Sector in Production

Between 1977-78 and 2017-18, production increased in all sectors, but most significantly in the tertiary sector. By 2017-18, the tertiary sector became the largest producing sector in India, surpassing the primary sector.

Reasons for the rising importance of the tertiary sector:
1.  **Basic Services:** Developing countries require essential services like hospitals, educational institutions, post services, police, courts, administration, defence, transport, banks, and insurance. The government is responsible for providing these.
2.  **Development of Primary and Secondary Sectors:** Growth in agriculture and industry leads to increased demand for support services like transport, trade, and storage.
3.  **Rising Income Levels:** As incomes increase, people demand more services such as eating out, tourism, shopping, private healthcare, private education, and professional training. This is particularly evident in cities.
4.  **New Services:** Recent decades have seen the growth of services based on information and communication technology (ICT), which have become essential and are expanding rapidly.

However, not all parts of the service sector are growing equally. It includes highly skilled workers in limited services and a vast number of low-paid workers (small shopkeepers, repair persons, transport workers) who barely earn a living due to lack of alternative opportunities. Only a segment of the service sector is growing significantly.

Why didn't employment shift significantly out of the primary sector? Because not enough jobs were created in the secondary and tertiary sectors. Industrial production increased over nine times, but industrial employment rose only about three times. Similarly, service sector production rose 14 times, but employment rose only about five times.

Consequently, more than half of India's workers are still in the primary sector (mainly agriculture), producing only about one-sixth of the GVA. The secondary and tertiary sectors produce the remaining GVA but employ less than half the people. This suggests that workers in agriculture might be producing less than their potential.

This means there are more people in agriculture than needed. Removing some wouldn't affect production. Workers in the agricultural sector are **underemployed**.

For example, a small farmer, Laxmi, with a two-hectare rain-dependent plot, has all five family members working on it because they have no other work. Everyone is working, but their labor effort is divided, and no one is fully employed. This is **underemployment**, where people appear to be working but work less than their potential. This is also called **disguised unemployment** because it's hidden, unlike someone visibly unemployed.

If a landlord hires one or two family members, the family earns extra income. Since five people weren't needed on the small plot, two moving out doesn't affect their farm's production. If those two move to a factory, family earnings increase, and farm production remains the same.

Millions of farmers like Laxmi exist in India. Removing many people from agriculture and providing work elsewhere wouldn't harm agricultural production, and the total family income would rise.

Underemployment also exists in other sectors. In urban service sectors, thousands of casual workers (painters, plumbers, repair persons) search for daily work but don't find it every day. Others sell goods on the street, working all day for very little income, because they lack better opportunities.
