This section explains how the total production of goods and services in an economy is measured, introducing the concepts of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and Gross Value Added (GVA).

# Comparing the Three Sectors

The various production activities in the primary, secondary, and tertiary sectors produce a very large number of goods and services. Also, the three sectors have a large number of people working in them. The next step is to see how much goods and services are produced and how many people work in each sector. In an economy, one or more sectors might be dominant in terms of total production and employment.

## How do we count the various goods and services and know the total production in each sector?

Adding up the actual numbers of thousands of different goods and services (cars, computers, nails, furniture) doesn't make sense. To solve this, economists use the **values** of goods and services instead of quantities. For example, 10,000 kgs of wheat at Rs 20/kg has a value of Rs 2,00,000.

A precaution is needed: only **final goods and services** should be counted. Final goods reach the consumers. Intermediate goods are used up in producing final goods. The value of final goods already includes the value of all intermediate goods used. Counting intermediate goods separately would mean counting the value of the same thing multiple times (e.g., wheat, then flour, then biscuits).

The value of final goods and services produced in each sector during a particular year gives the total production for that sector. The sum of production in the three sectors is the **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** of a country. GDP is the value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a particular year. It indicates the size of the economy.

In India, the central government ministry estimates GDP by collecting information on the volume and prices of goods and services from government departments across states and union territories.

Recently, the Indian government started using **Gross Value Added (GVA)** instead of GDP to measure the contribution of sectors, aligning with global practices. GVA measures the contribution of sectors after adjusting for taxes and subsidies.

## Historical Change in Sectors

Historically, in many now developed countries, the primary sector was the most important at early stages of development. As farming methods improved, agriculture prospered, allowing more people to take up other activities like crafts and trade. Buying and selling increased. Transport, administration, and army roles also existed. However, most goods were natural products from the primary sector, and most people were employed there.

Over a long time (more than a hundred years), new manufacturing methods led to factories expanding. People who previously worked on farms moved to factory jobs in large numbers. The secondary sector gradually became the most important in total production and employment, marking a shift in the importance of sectors.

In the past 100 years, developed countries have seen a further shift from the secondary to the tertiary sector. The service sector has become the most important in total production and employs most working people. This is the typical pattern in developed countries.
