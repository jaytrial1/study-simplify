This section introduces the concept of classifying economic activities into groups or sectors to better understand an economy. It details the primary, secondary, and tertiary classifications.

# Sectors of Economic Activities

Economic activities involve producing goods and services. To understand these activities, we group them based on certain criteria, creating sectors.

We start by examining different kinds of economic activities.

Many activities involve directly using natural resources. For instance, growing cotton depends heavily on natural factors like rainfall, sunshine, and climate. The resulting product, cotton, is natural. Similarly, dairy farming relies on animal biological processes and fodder availability, yielding milk, a natural product. Minerals and ores are also natural products.

When a good is produced by exploiting natural resources, it belongs to the **primary sector**. It's called primary because it forms the basis for all subsequent products. Since most natural products come from agriculture, dairy, fishing, and forestry, this sector is also known as the **agriculture and related sector**.

The **secondary sector** covers activities where natural products are transformed into other forms through manufacturing. This is the step after primary activities. The product isn't natural but is made through some manufacturing process, which can occur in a factory, workshop, or at home. Examples include spinning yarn and weaving cloth from cotton fibre, making sugar or gur from sugarcane, or making bricks from earth to build houses. As this sector became associated with industries, it's also called the **industrial sector**.

Following the primary and secondary sectors, there is a third category, the **tertiary sector**, which is distinct from the first two. These activities support the development of the primary and secondary sectors. They don't produce goods themselves but provide aid or support for the production process. For example, goods produced in the primary or secondary sectors need transportation (trucks, trains) and sale (wholesale, retail). Storage in godowns may also be necessary. Communication (telephone, letters) or borrowing money from banks (banking) are also needed to help production and trade. Transport, storage, communication, banking, and trade are examples of tertiary activities. Because these activities generate services rather than goods, the tertiary sector is also called the **service sector**.

The service sector also includes essential services that don't directly help in goods production, such as teachers, doctors, washermen, barbers, cobblers, lawyers, and administrative/accounting staff. Recently, new services based on information technology, like internet cafes, ATM booths, call centres, and software companies, have become significant.

Economic activities, though grouped into three categories, are highly interdependent.

# Interdependence of Sectors

## Examples of Economic Activities and Their Interdependence

Imagine what would happen if farmers refuse to sell sugarcane to a particular sugar mill. The mill will have to shut down.
> **Answer:** This shows the secondary sector (sugar mill) is dependent on the primary sector (sugarcane farming).

Imagine what would happen to cotton cultivation if companies decide not to buy from the Indian market and import all cotton they need from other countries. Indian cotton cultivation will become less profitable and the farmers may even go bankrupt, if they cannot quickly switch to other crops. Cotton prices will fall.
> **Answer:** This shows the primary sector (cotton cultivation) is dependent on demand from the secondary sector (textile companies) and international trade (tertiary sector).

Farmers buy many goods such as tractors, pumpsets, electricity, pesticides and fertilisers. Imagine what would happen if the price of fertilisers or pumpsets go up. Cost of cultivation of the farmers will rise and their profits will be reduced.
> **Answer:** This shows the primary sector (farming) is dependent on the secondary sector (manufacturing of equipment, fertilisers) and tertiary sector (electricity supply, trade).

People working in industrial and service sectors need food. Imagine what would happen if there is a strike by transporters and lorries refuse to take vegetables, milk, etc. from rural areas. Food will become scarce in urban areas whereas farmers will be unable to sell their products.
> **Answer:** This shows the secondary and tertiary sectors (urban workers) are dependent on the primary sector (farmers for food) and the tertiary sector (transport for delivery).
