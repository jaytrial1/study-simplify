# Factors That Have Enabled Globalisation

Several factors have stimulated the globalisation process:

## Technology
Rapid improvements in technology have been a major enabler.

*   **Transportation Technology:** Significant improvements over the past fifty years allow for much faster delivery of goods across long distances at lower costs.
    *   **Containers:** Standardised containers allow goods to be easily loaded onto ships, railways, planes, and trucks, dramatically reducing port handling costs and increasing speed.

## Liberalisation of Foreign Trade and Foreign Investment Policy
Governments use **trade barriers** (like taxes on imports) to regulate foreign trade, deciding what goods can enter and in what quantity. This is done to protect domestic producers from foreign competition.

*   **India's Policy (Post-Independence):** Barriers were initially placed on foreign trade and investment to protect nascent domestic industries in the 1950s and 60s. Only essential items were imported.
*   **Policy Change (Around 1991):** India made significant policy changes, deciding that Indian producers should compete globally. It was believed competition would improve quality and performance.
*   **Liberalisation:** Barriers on foreign trade and investment were largely removed. This meant easier import/export and foreign companies setting up factories. Removing these government restrictions is called **liberalisation**. It allows businesses more freedom in import/export decisions with fewer government restrictions.

## World Trade Organisation (WTO)
The liberalisation in countries like India was supported by powerful international organisations that advocate for the removal of trade barriers, promoting 'free trade' among all countries.

*   **Aim:** To liberalise international trade.
*   **Establishment:** Started at the initiative of developed countries.
*   **Function:** Establishes rules for international trade and monitors compliance.
*   **Membership:** Around 160 countries are members.

However, the practice is often seen as uneven:
*   Developed countries have often retained trade barriers.
*   WTO rules have pressured developing countries to remove barriers.
*   **Example: Debate on Agricultural Products:** Developed countries like the US provide massive subsidies to their small percentage of farmers, enabling them to sell products at abnormally low prices globally, harming farmers in developing countries. Developing countries argue this is unfair, as they have reduced barriers while developed countries maintain support for their producers.

1. What do you understand by liberalisation of foreign trade?
> **Answer:** Liberalisation of foreign trade means the removal or significant reduction of government barriers or restrictions on trade between countries, such as taxes on imports (tariffs) or limits on quantities that can be imported (quotas). This allows goods to be imported and exported more freely.
2. Tax on imports is one type of trade barrier. The government could also place a limit on the number of goods that can be imported. This is known as quotas. Can you explain, using the example of Chinese toys, how quotas can be used as trade barriers? Do you think this should be used? Discuss.
> **Answer:** Using the Chinese toys example, if the Indian government imposed a quota, it would set a maximum limit on the *number* of Chinese toys that can be imported into India each year. Even if Chinese toys are cheaper, once the quota is reached, no more can be imported, regardless of demand. This would restrict the supply of Chinese toys, reduce competition for Indian toy makers, and likely keep the prices of toys in India higher than they would be under free trade.
> Whether quotas *should* be used is debatable.
> *   **Arguments for using quotas:** They can protect domestic industries and jobs from overwhelming foreign competition, especially for nascent or vulnerable industries. They can also help manage trade deficits.
> *   **Arguments against using quotas:** They limit consumer choice, can lead to higher prices for consumers, reduce efficiency by protecting less competitive domestic firms, and can invite retaliatory trade barriers from other countries.
> The decision depends on balancing the interests of domestic producers, consumers, and the overall economy, as well as international trade relations.

2. What do you think can be done so that trade between countries is more fair?
> **Answer:** To make trade fairer, developed countries should reduce or eliminate the subsidies and trade barriers they maintain, especially in sectors like agriculture, which harm producers in developing countries. Developing countries should also have a stronger voice in WTO negotiations to ensure rules benefit them and address their development needs. Additionally, international labour and environmental standards could be incorporated into trade agreements to prevent unfair competition based on exploitation.
3. In the above example (Debate on Trade Practices), we saw that the US government gives massive sums of money to farmers for production. At times, governments also give support to promote production of certain types of goods, such as those which are environmentally friendly. Discuss whether these are fair or not.
> **Answer:** Subsidies like those given to US farmers are often seen as unfair in the context of international trade because they distort market prices and make it difficult for unsubsidised farmers in other countries (especially developing ones) to compete. This is seen as violating the principle of free and fair trade advocated by the WTO.
> However, subsidies to promote environmentally friendly goods might be viewed differently. If they address market failures (like the cost of pollution not being included in prices) or promote global public goods (like reducing carbon emissions), they could be argued as justifiable, provided they don't become disguised protectionism. The fairness often depends on the purpose of the subsidy, its impact on other countries, and whether it aligns with broader global goals.
