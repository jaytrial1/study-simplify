This section explores the effects of globalisation on the Indian economy and its people, highlighting the benefits for some consumers and producers, as well as the significant challenges faced by small producers and workers.

# Impact of Globalisation in India
Globalisation has significantly impacted the Indian economy over the last two decades.

*   **Benefits for Consumers:** Increased competition (from local and foreign producers) has benefited well-off urban consumers with:
    *   Greater choice of goods.
    *   Improved quality.
    *   Lower prices for many products.
    This has led to higher standards of living for these sections.

*   **Impact on Producers and Workers:** The impact has been uneven.
    *   **Benefits for some Indian Companies:** MNC investments have increased in industries like cell phones, automobiles, electronics, soft drinks, fast food, and services (banking), which have many well-off buyers. This created new jobs and benefited local suppliers to these industries.
        *   Some top Indian companies have benefited from increased competition, investing in technology and improving standards.
        *   Some have gained from collaborations with foreign companies.
        *   Some large Indian companies (Tata Motors, Infosys, Ranbaxy, Asian Paints, Sundaram Fasteners) have become MNCs themselves.
    *   **New Opportunities in Services:** Globalisation created opportunities in services, especially IT-enabled services like call centres, data entry, accounting, and engineering, which are done cheaply in India and exported.

    ## Steps to Attract Foreign Investment
    Indian governments are creating **Special Economic Zones (SEZs)** with world-class facilities (electricity, water, roads, transport, storage, recreation, education) to attract foreign companies. Companies in SEZs get tax exemptions for an initial five years.
    Government has also allowed flexibility in **labour laws** to attract foreign investment. This means companies can hire workers 'flexibly' for short periods instead of permanently, reducing labour costs. However, foreign companies demand even more flexibility.

## Small Producers: Compete or Perish
For many small producers and workers, globalisation has brought major challenges due to rising competition.

## Rising Competition
*   **Example: Ravi, a capacitor producer:** Ravi started a capacitor company in 1992. After liberalisation removed import restrictions in 2001 (as per WTO agreement), he faced competition. His main clients (television companies) started importing cheaper capacitors or moved to assembling for MNCs. Ravi's production and workforce significantly reduced. Many small producers in similar industries (batteries, plastics, toys, tyres, dairy products, vegetable oil) have been hit hard and shut down, leading to job losses.
*   Small and medium industries employ the largest number of workers in India after agriculture (11 crore workers).

## Competition and Uncertain Employment
Globalisation and competition pressure have changed workers' lives, making jobs less secure. Employers prefer to employ workers 'flexibly'.

*   **Example: Garment Export Industry:** Large MNCs order from Indian exporters, seeking the cheapest goods to maximise profits. Exporters cut costs, primarily labour costs.
    *   Factories shifted from permanent to **temporary workers**.
    *   Workers are not paid for the whole year.
    *   Long working hours and night shifts during peak season are common.
    *   Wages are low, forcing workers to do overtime to survive.

*   While MNCs profit, workers are often denied their fair share of benefits from globalisation.
*   **Sushila's Story:** A former permanent garment worker with benefits (health insurance, provident fund) lost her job when her factory closed. She found a temporary job far away, earning less than half her previous wage, working seven days a week with no benefits or paid days off.
*   These conditions of work are becoming common. Many workers are in the unorganised sector, and even conditions in the organised sector are starting to resemble the unorganised sector, with workers losing previously held protections and benefits.

> *   Increased competition, which can improve the efficiency and quality of domestic industries.

2. Should producers such as Ravi stop production because their cost of production is higher compared to producers in other countries? What do you think?
> **Answer:** Whether producers like Ravi *should* stop production is a complex question. From a purely economic efficiency standpoint in a perfectly free market, producers with higher costs might struggle to survive. However, stopping production has significant social costs (job losses, loss of livelihood, impact on local economy). Instead of simply stopping, efforts could be made to help them become more competitive (e.g., through technological upgrades, better infrastructure, access to cheaper credit, support for finding niche markets) or to provide alternative livelihoods. Simply letting them perish ignores the human and social impact.

1. In what ways has competition affected workers, Indian exporters and foreign MNCs in the garment industry?
> **Answer:**
> *   **Workers:** Faced increased job insecurity (temporary employment), longer working hours, lower wages, and loss of benefits previously available to permanent workers.
> *   **Indian exporters:** Faced pressure to cut costs, particularly labour costs, to secure orders from large MNCs. They have to meet stringent quality and delivery standards set by MNCs.
> *   **Foreign MNCs:** Have benefited significantly by sourcing garments at cheaper prices due to the intense competition among Indian exporters, allowing them to maximise their profits.
2. What can be done by each of the following so that the workers can get a fair share of benefits brought by globalisation?
(a) government
(b) employers at the exporting factories
(c) MNCs
(d) workers.
> **Answer:**
> (a) **Government:** Can enforce labour laws strictly, ensure minimum wages are paid, regulate working hours and conditions, promote job security, and provide social security benefits.
> (b) **Employers at exporting factories:** Can improve working conditions, offer fair wages and benefits, provide stable employment contracts, and ensure a safe working environment.
> (c) **MNCs:** Can use their power to ensure their suppliers (the exporting factories) adhere to fair labour practices, pay decent wages, and provide good working conditions, rather than just seeking the lowest cost. They can incorporate labour standards into their sourcing policies.
> (d) **Workers:** Can form unions or associations to collectively bargain for better wages, working conditions, and job security. They can raise awareness about their rights and advocate for stronger legal protections.
3. One of the present debates in India is whether companies should have flexible policies for employment. Based on what you have read in the chapter, summarise the point of view of the employers and workers.
> **Answer:**
> *   **Employers' point of view:** Employers (especially those competing globally or attracting foreign investment) favour flexible labour policies. They argue it allows them to hire and fire workers easily based on fluctuating demand, reducing labour costs and increasing efficiency and competitiveness in a global market. This flexibility is seen as necessary to adapt quickly to changing economic conditions and production needs.
> *   **Workers' point of view:** Workers oppose flexible employment policies because it leads to job insecurity, uncertain income, lack of benefits (like health insurance, provident fund), longer working hours, and poor working conditions. They argue it makes them vulnerable to exploitation and denies them a stable livelihood and basic rights.
