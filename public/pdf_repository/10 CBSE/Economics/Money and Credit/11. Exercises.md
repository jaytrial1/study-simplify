# Exercises

1.  In situations with high risks, credit might create further problems for the borrower. Explain.
    > **Answer:** If a borrower takes a loan for a risky activity (like farming dependent on unpredictable weather or markets) and the activity fails, they may be unable to repay the loan. The debt accumulates, especially with high interest, potentially forcing them to sell assets or falling into a debt trap, making their situation worse than before they took the loan.

2.  How does money solve the problem of double coincidence of wants? Explain with an example of your own.
    > **Answer:** Money acts as an intermediate step. Instead of needing to find someone who wants exactly what you have *and* has exactly what you want, you can sell what you have for money, and then use that money to buy what you want from anyone who is selling it. Example: A potter wants to buy shoes. Without money, the potter needs to find a shoemaker who wants pottery. With money, the potter sells pottery for money, and then uses the money to buy shoes from any shoemaker.

3.  How do banks mediate between those who have surplus money and those who need money?
    > **Answer:** People with surplus money deposit it in banks. Banks use the majority of these deposits to give loans to people who need money for various activities. Banks pay interest to depositors and charge a higher interest rate to borrowers, with the difference being the bank's income. This process channels savings from those who don't need funds immediately to those who do.

4.  Look at a 10 rupee note. What is written on top? Can you explain this statement?
    > **Answer:** On top of a 10 rupee note, it usually says "RESERVE BANK OF INDIA". Below that, it states "GUARANTEED BY THE CENTRAL GOVERNMENT". This statement means that the Reserve Bank of India is the official authority issuing the currency, and its value is guaranteed and backed by the government of India, making it legal tender that must be accepted for transactions.

5.  Why do we need to expand formal sources of credit in India?
    > **Answer:** Expanding formal credit is needed because informal sources often charge very high interest rates, trapping poor borrowers in debt. Formal sources like banks and cooperatives offer cheaper credit. Expanding formal credit allows more people, especially the poor, to access affordable loans for productive activities, leading to increased income and contributing to overall economic development.

6.  What is the basic idea behind the SHGs for the poor? Explain in your own words.
    > **Answer:** The basic idea of SHGs is to group poor people, especially women, to pool their small savings. This collective saving creates a fund from which members can take small loans. After establishing a record of regular savings and internal lending, the group can get larger loans from banks without needing individual collateral, using the group's collective guarantee. This helps poor people access affordable credit and become financially independent.

7.  What are the reasons why the banks might not be willing to lend to certain borrowers?
    > **Answer:** Banks may be unwilling to lend if borrowers lack collateral (assets to guarantee the loan), do not have proper documents proving income or identity, have a poor credit history (previous failures to repay loans), or if the proposed loan purpose is deemed too risky or unproductive.

8.  In what ways does the Reserve Bank of India supervise the functioning of banks? Why is this necessary?
    > **Answer:** The RBI supervises banks by monitoring their cash reserves, ensuring they meet mandatory requirements. It also checks that banks lend not just to large businesses but also to small borrowers. Banks must submit regular reports on their lending activities (loan amounts, recipients, interest rates). This supervision is necessary to ensure banks operate responsibly, maintain financial stability, protect depositors' money, and provide credit fairly across different sectors of the economy.

9.  Analyse the role of credit for development.
    > **Answer:** Credit plays a crucial role in development by providing necessary funds for economic activities. Affordable credit enables farmers to buy inputs, businesses to invest in equipment or operations, and individuals to acquire assets like homes or education. This leads to increased production, employment, income generation, and overall economic growth. However, if credit is too expensive or leads to debt traps, it can hinder development and worsen poverty.

10. Manav needs a loan to set up a small business. On what basis will Manav decide whether to borrow from the bank or the moneylender? Discuss.
    > **Answer:** Manav will decide based on several factors:
    > *   **Interest Rate:** Banks usually offer much lower interest rates than moneylenders.
    > *   **Collateral/Documentation:** Banks require collateral and proper documents (business plan, identity, address, income proof). Moneylenders might require less documentation or collateral but could demand other forms of security or use unfair recovery methods.
    > *   **Ease of Access:** Getting a bank loan can be time-consuming and involve paperwork. A moneylender might provide funds faster and with less hassle, especially if they know Manav.
    > *   **Terms of Repayment:** Bank loans often have structured, predictable repayment schedules. Moneylenders' terms might be less transparent and more flexible initially but potentially more exploitative later.
    > *   **Risk of Debt Trap:** Borrowing from a high-interest moneylender carries a much higher risk of falling into a debt trap if the business faces difficulties.

11. In India, about 80 per cent of farmers are small farmers, who need credit for cultivation.
    (a) Why might banks be unwilling to lend to small farmers?
    > **Answer:** Banks might be unwilling to lend to small farmers because they often lack proper collateral (like land titles), may not have formal documents proving income or land ownership, and their farming income can be highly unpredictable due to dependence on weather and market prices, making repayment risky for the bank.
    (b) What are the other sources from which the small farmers can borrow?
    > **Answer:** Small farmers can borrow from informal sources like village moneylenders, agricultural traders (who might also buy their crop), relatives, and friends. They can also potentially access credit through cooperative societies or Self-Help Groups.
    (c) Explain with an example how the terms of credit can be unfavourable for the small farmer.
    > **Answer:** Using Shyamal's example: Borrowing from an agricultural trader at 3% per month (36% per annum) is much higher than a bank rate (like Arun's 8.5% per annum). Additionally, the trader requires Shyamal to sell his crop to him after harvest. This forces Shyamal to sell when prices are typically low, reducing his potential earnings and making it harder to repay the high-interest loan, potentially pushing him towards debt.
    (d) Suggest some ways by which small farmers can get cheap credit.
    > **Answer:** Ways for small farmers to get cheap credit include: increasing the reach of formal banking in rural areas, strengthening and expanding cooperative societies that provide agricultural loans, promoting and linking Self-Help Groups (SHGs) to banks, and government schemes offering subsidised credit or support for obtaining bank loans.
