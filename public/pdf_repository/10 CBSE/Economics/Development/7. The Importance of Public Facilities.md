This section explains why access to collective goods and services like healthcare and education is crucial for development, often being more effective than relying solely on individual income.

# Public Facilities

How is it that the average person in Haryana has more income than the average person in Kerala but lags behind in these crucial areas (IMR, Literacy, Attendance Ratio)? The reason is - money in your pocket cannot buy all the goods and services that you may need to live well. So, income by itself is not a completely adequate indicator of material goods and services that citizens are able to use.

For example, normally, your money cannot buy you a pollution-free environment or ensure that you get unadulterated medicines, unless you can afford to shift to a community that already has all these things. Money may also not be able to protect you from infectious diseases, unless the whole of your community takes preventive steps.

Actually for many of the important things in life the best way, also the cheapest way, is to provide these goods and services collectively.
*   Will it be cheaper to have collective security for the whole locality or for each house to have its own security staff?
*   What if no one, other than you, in your village or locality is interested in studying? Would you be able to study? Not unless your parents could afford to send you to some private school elsewhere. So you are actually able to study because many other children also want to study and because many people believe that the government should open schools and provide other facilities so that all children have a chance to study.

Even now, in many areas, children, particularly girls, are not able to go to high school because the government/society has not provided adequate facilities.

Kerala has a low Infant Mortality Rate because it has adequate provision of basic health and educational facilities. Similarly, in some states, the Public Distribution System (PDS) functions well. Health and nutritional status of people of such states is certainly likely to be better.

2.  Think of other examples where collective provision of goods and services is cheaper than individual provision.
    > **Answer:**
    > *   Public parks and playgrounds vs. each family building their own private recreation area.
    > *   Public transportation (buses, trains) vs. everyone owning and using their own car for every trip.
    > *   Public libraries vs. each person buying every book they want to read.
    > *   Collective garbage collection and disposal vs. each household managing their own waste.
    > *   Street lighting vs. each house lighting the street in front of it.
3.  Does availability of good health and educational facilities depend only on amount of money spent by the government on these facilities? What other factors could be relevant?
    > **Answer:** No, the availability and effectiveness of good health and educational facilities do not depend *only* on the amount of money spent. Other crucial factors include:
    > *   **Quality of services:** Are the teachers well-trained? Are doctors and nurses available and skilled?
    > *   **Accessibility:** Are facilities located where people can easily reach them (especially in rural or remote areas)?
    > *   **Infrastructure:** Are schools and hospitals well-built and equipped?
    > *   **Management and efficiency:** Are the funds spent effectively? Is there corruption?
    > *   **Awareness and utilization:** Do people know about the facilities and feel encouraged to use them?
    > *   **Social factors:** Are there social barriers (like gender or caste discrimination) preventing certain groups from accessing facilities?
    > *   **Community participation:** Is the local community involved in supporting and monitoring the facilities?
4.  In a study, it was found that in Tamil Nadu, 90 per cent of the people living in rural areas used a ration shop, whereas in West Bengal only 35 per cent of rural people did so. Where would people be better off and why?
    > **Answer:** People would likely be better off in Tamil Nadu. Ration shops (part of the Public Distribution System - PDS) provide essential food grains and other necessities at subsidised prices. High usage of ration shops (90%) in rural Tamil Nadu suggests that the PDS is functioning effectively and reaching a large portion of the population, helping to ensure food security and potentially better nutritional status, especially for poorer households. The low usage (35%) in West Bengal suggests the system might be less effective, leaving a larger portion of the rural population potentially vulnerable to food insecurity and higher market prices.
