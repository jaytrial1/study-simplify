# Land Resources

We live on land, we perform our economic activities on land and we use it in different ways. Thus, land is a natural resource of utmost importance. It supports natural vegetation, wild life, human life, economic activities, transport and communication systems. However, land is an asset of a finite magnitude, therefore, it is important to use the available land for various purposes with careful planning.

## Land Utilisation

Land resources are used for the following purposes:
1.  **Forests**
2.  **Land not available for cultivation**
    (a) Barren and waste land
    (b) Land put to non-agricultural uses, e.g. buildings, roads, factories, etc.
3.  **Other uncultivated land** (excluding fallow land)
    (a) Permanent pastures and grazing land,
    (b) Land under miscellaneous tree crops groves (not included in net sown area),
    (c) Culturable waste land (left uncultivated for more than 5 agricultural years).
4.  **Fallow lands**
    (a) Current fallow-(left without cultivation for one or less than one agricultural year),
    (b) Other than current fallow-(left uncultivated for the past 1 to 5 agricultural years).
5.  **Net sown area** - the physical extent of land on which crops are sown and harvested is known as net sown area.
    *   Area sown more than once in an agricultural year plus net sown area is known as **gross cropped area**.

## Land Use Pattern in India

The use of land is determined both by physical factors such as topography, climate, soil types as well as human factors such as population density, technological capability and culture and traditions etc.

## Activity

Try to do a comparison between the two pie charts (Fig. 1.4) given for land use and find out why the net sown area and the land under forests have changed from 1960-61 to 2019-20 very marginally.
> **Answer:**
> **Net Sown Area (NSA):** The net sown area has remained remarkably stable (45.26% in 1960-61 to 45.64% in 2019-20) because:
> *   **Food Security Priority:** India is a densely populated country with a high demand for food. Maintaining a large area under cultivation is crucial for national food security.
> *   **Limited Arable Land:** Most of the easily cultivable land was already brought under cultivation by 1960-61. Further expansion faces constraints from topography, climate, and competing land uses.
> *   **Intensification vs. Expansion:** Instead of expanding the net sown area significantly, the focus has shifted to intensifying agriculture through better irrigation, fertilizers, and high-yielding varieties to increase productivity from the existing land.
> *   **Urbanization/Industrialization vs. Reclamation:** While some agricultural land is lost to urbanization and industrialization, this is often offset by bringing culturable wasteland or fallow lands under cultivation.
>
> **Land under Forests:** The land under forests has increased, but not drastically (18.11% in 1960-61 to 23.41% in 2019-20) because:
> *   **National Forest Policy (1952):** The policy aimed for 33% forest cover, indicating a conscious effort to increase it. The observed increase reflects afforestation efforts and improved forest management.
> *   **Competing Demands:** Despite afforestation, there are continuous pressures on forest land from development projects (infrastructure, mining), agriculture, and urbanization.
> *   **Slow Growth Rate:** Forests take a long time to grow and establish, so a significant increase in percentage takes many decades.
> *   **Definition and Reporting:** Changes in forest survey methods and definitions can also influence reported figures.

The land under permanent pasture has also decreased. How are we able to feed our huge cattle population on this pasture land and what are the consequences of it?
> **Answer:** The decrease in permanent pasture land, despite a large cattle population, is managed through several strategies, but it also has significant consequences:
> *   **Intensification of Fodder Cultivation:** Farmers are increasingly cultivating fodder crops (like maize, sorghum, alfalfa) on agricultural land, rather than relying solely on pastures.
> *   **Crop Residues:** Cattle are fed a large proportion of agricultural crop residues (e.g., wheat straw, rice husk, sugarcane bagasse).
> *   **Supplements and Concentrates:** The use of commercial feed, oilcakes, and other nutritional supplements has increased.
> *   **Zero Grazing/Stall Feeding:** More cattle are kept in stalls and fed cut fodder, reducing the need for open grazing lands.
>
> **Consequences of Decreased Pasture Land:**
> *   **Overgrazing:** Remaining pasture lands often suffer from severe overgrazing, leading to land degradation, soil erosion, and reduced productivity.
> *   **Increased Cost of Livestock Rearing:** Reliance on cultivated fodder and commercial feed increases the cost of raising livestock, impacting farmers' profitability.
> *   **Nutritional Deficiencies:** Cattle may suffer from nutritional deficiencies if their diet is not balanced, affecting milk production and overall health.
> *   **Environmental Impact:** Overgrazing contributes to desertification and loss of biodiversity in fragile ecosystems.
> *   **Pressure on Agricultural Land:** Cultivating fodder crops competes with food crops for arable land and water resources.

Most of the other than the current fallow lands are either of poor quality or the cost of cultivation of such land is very high. Hence, these lands are cultivated once or twice in about two to three years and if these are included in the net sown area then the percentage of NSA in India comes to about 54 per cent of the total reporting area.

The pattern of net sown area varies greatly from one state to another. It is over 80 per cent of the total area in Punjab and Haryana and less than 10 per cent in Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Manipur and Andaman Nicobar Islands.

Find out reasons for the low proportion of net sown area in these states.
> **Answer:** The low proportion of net sown area (NSA) in states like Arunachal Pradesh, Mizoram, Manipur, and Andaman Nicobar Islands is due to a combination of geographical, climatic, and socio-economic factors:
> *   **Topography:** These regions are predominantly hilly and mountainous (e.g., Himalayas in Arunachal Pradesh, Lushai Hills in Mizoram, Patkai Hills in Manipur). Steep slopes are unsuitable for extensive cultivation and are prone to soil erosion.
> *   **Dense Forest Cover:** These states have a very high percentage of their geographical area under dense forests, which are protected and cannot be converted to agricultural land.
> *   **Accessibility and Infrastructure:** Remote and rugged terrain makes it difficult to develop irrigation facilities, transport infrastructure, and access markets, discouraging large-scale agriculture.
> *   **Traditional Farming Practices:** Shifting cultivation (Jhum cultivation) is still practiced in some areas, where land is cultivated for a few years and then left fallow, contributing to a lower *net* sown area at any given time.
> *   **Ecological Sensitivity:** Many areas are ecologically fragile and are protected for biodiversity conservation, limiting agricultural expansion.
> *   **Island Ecology (Andaman & Nicobar):** The Andaman & Nicobar Islands have limited flat land, dense forests, and a focus on marine resources and tourism, rather than extensive agriculture. The island ecosystem is also very sensitive.

Forest area in the country is far lower than the desired 33 per cent of geographical area, as it was outlined in the National Forest Policy (1952). It was considered essential for maintenance of the ecological balance. The livelihood of millions of people who live on the fringes of these forests depends upon it. A part of the land is termed as waste land and land put to other non-agricultural uses. Waste land includes rocky, arid and desert areas and land put to other non-agricultural uses includes settlements, roads, railways, industry etc. Continuous use of land over a long period of time without taking appropriate measures to conserve and manage it, has resulted in land degradation. This, in turn, has serious repercussions on society and the environment.
