This section introduces the fundamental concept of water as a renewable resource, yet paradoxically, highlights the widespread issue of water scarcity. It explores the various factors contributing to this scarcity, such as population growth, agricultural demands, industrialization, urbanization, and pollution.

# Water Resources: Abundance and Scarcity

Three-fourths of the Earth's surface is covered with water, but only a small proportion of it is freshwater, which is usable by humans. This freshwater is primarily obtained from surface runoff and groundwater, continually renewed and recharged through the *hydrological cycle*. This cycle ensures that water is a **renewable resource**.

Given the abundance and renewability of water, it might seem puzzling that countries and regions around the globe suffer from water scarcity. It is predicted that by 2025, nearly two billion people will live in absolute water scarcity.

## Water Scarcity: Beyond Low Rainfall

When we speak of water shortages, we often immediately associate it with regions having low rainfall or those that are drought-prone, like the deserts of Rajasthan. While the availability of water resources does vary over space and time due to variations in seasonal and annual precipitation, water scarcity in most cases is caused by:

*   **Over-exploitation:** Excessive withdrawal of water from sources.
*   **Excessive use:** Inefficient or wasteful consumption.
*   **Unequal access:** Disparities in water distribution among different social groups.

It is possible for an area or region to have ample water resources but still face water scarcity. Many of our cities are such examples. Thus, water scarcity can be an outcome of a large and growing population, leading to greater demands for water and unequal access to it.

## Causes of Water Scarcity

A large population requires more water not only for domestic use but also to produce more food.

*   **Agricultural Demands:** To facilitate higher food-grain production, water resources are being over-exploited to expand irrigated areas for dry-season agriculture. *Irrigated agriculture* is the largest consumer of water. This can lead to falling groundwater levels, adversely affecting water availability and food security. There is a need to revolutionize agriculture through developing drought-resistant crops and dry farming techniques.

*   **Industrialization and Urbanization:** Post-independent India witnessed intensive industrialization and urbanization, which created vast opportunities but also exerted pressure on existing freshwater resources.
    *   **Industrial Use:** Industries are heavy users of water and also require power to run them. Much of this energy comes from *hydroelectric power*, further linking industries to water resources.
    *   **Urban Lifestyles:** Multiplying urban centers with large and dense populations and urban lifestyles have not only added to water and energy requirements but have further aggravated the problem. Many housing societies and colonies in cities have their own groundwater pumping devices, leading to the over-exploitation and depletion of fragile water resources in several cities.

## Water Quality and Pollution

Water scarcity is not only a quantitative problem but also a qualitative one. Even if there is ample water to meet the needs of the people, much of it may be polluted by:

*   Domestic and industrial wastes
*   Chemicals
*   Pesticides and fertilizers used in agriculture

This pollution makes water hazardous for human use.

### Key Concepts & Examples

*   **Atal Bhujal Yojana (Atal Jal):** This scheme is being implemented in water-stressed Gram Panchayats across seven states (Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, and Uttar Pradesh). It aims to bring about behavioral changes in communities, shifting from a consumption-oriented attitude to one of **conservation and smart water management**.
*   **Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM):** This Government of India initiative prioritizes improving the quality of life, especially in rural areas. Its goal is to ensure every rural household receives an assured supply of potable piped water at a service level of 55 litres per capita per day on a long-term basis, by ensuring the functionality of tap water connections.
