This section highlights the contemporary relevance and successful adaptation of traditional rainwater harvesting methods in various parts of India. It showcases examples like Shillong and Gendathur, demonstrating how these age-old techniques are being revitalized to address modern water needs.

# Modern Adaptations and Success Stories in Rainwater Harvesting

Today, in western Rajasthan, the practice of rooftop rainwater harvesting is sadly on the decline due to the abundance of water available from the perennial Indira Gandhi Canal. However, some houses still maintain their *tankas* because they prefer the taste of *palar pani* over tap water.

Fortunately, in many parts of rural and urban India, rooftop rainwater harvesting is being successfully adapted to store and conserve water.

## Key Examples

*   **Shillong, Meghalaya:** Rooftop rainwater harvesting is the most common practice in Shillong, Meghalaya. This is particularly interesting because Cherapunjee and Mawsynram, situated only 55 km from Shillong, receive the highest rainfall in the world. Yet, Shillong, the state capital, faces an acute shortage of water. Nearly every household in the city has a rooftop rainwater harvesting structure, with about 15-25 percent of the total household water requirement met through this method.
*   **Gendathur, Mysuru, Karnataka:** In Gendathur, a remote backward village in Mysuru, villagers have installed rooftop rainwater harvesting systems in their households to meet their water needs. Nearly 200 households have implemented this system, earning the village the rare distinction of being rich in rainwater. Gendathur receives an annual precipitation of 1,000 mm, and with an 80 percent collection efficiency and about 10 fillings, every house can collect and use approximately 50,000 litres of water annually. From the 200 houses, the net amount of rainwater harvested annually amounts to 10,000,000 litres.

### Key Concepts & Examples

*   **Tamil Nadu's Mandatory Rainwater Harvesting:** Tamil Nadu is the first state in India to make rooftop rainwater harvesting structures compulsory for all houses across the state. There are legal provisions to punish defaulters, highlighting the government's strong commitment to this water conservation method.

## Activity: Local Rainwater Harvesting Systems

Find out other rainwater harvesting systems existing in and around your locality.

> **Answer:**
> **Examples of Local Rainwater Harvesting Systems (General Examples):**
>
> Depending on the locality, various rainwater harvesting systems might be found:
>
> 1.  **Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting (Residential/Commercial):** This is common in many urban and semi-urban areas. Rainwater is collected from rooftops, filtered, and stored in underground sumps or overhead tanks for direct use (e.g., flushing toilets, gardening, washing) or for recharging groundwater through borewells/pits.
> 2.  **Percolation Pits/Recharge Pits:** These are small pits dug in the ground, often filled with gravel and sand, designed to collect surface runoff water and allow it to slowly percolate into the ground, thereby recharging the groundwater table. They are commonly seen in parks, open spaces, or even residential compounds.
> 3.  **Check Dams/Bunds:** In rural or semi-rural areas, small check dams or earthen bunds are constructed across small streams or slopes. These structures slow down the flow of rainwater, allowing it to infiltrate the soil and recharge groundwater, while also preventing soil erosion.
> 4.  **Farm Ponds:** Farmers often construct small ponds on their land to collect rainwater runoff from their fields. This stored water can then be used for supplementary irrigation during dry spells.
> 5.  **Rejuvenation of Traditional Water Bodies:** In some localities, efforts are made to desilt and restore traditional wells, stepwells (*baolis*), or village ponds (*talabs*) to enhance their water storage and groundwater recharge capacity.
>
> To find out specific systems in your locality, you could:
> *   Observe construction sites or older buildings for visible pipes leading underground or to tanks.
> *   Ask local residents, especially older generations, about traditional water management practices.
> *   Check with local municipal bodies or environmental NGOs for ongoing water conservation projects.
