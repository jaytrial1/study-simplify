This section explores the rich history of traditional water harvesting systems in India, presenting them as viable and environmentally sound alternatives to large-scale projects. It details various ingenious methods developed by communities across different regions to collect and manage water based on local ecological conditions.

# Rainwater Harvesting

Many people believe that given the disadvantages and rising resistance against multi-purpose projects, water harvesting systems offer a viable alternative, both socio-economically and environmentally. Ancient India had an extraordinary tradition of water-harvesting systems, alongside sophisticated hydraulic structures. People possessed in-depth knowledge of rainfall regimes and soil types, developing wide-ranging techniques to harvest rainwater, groundwater, river water, and floodwater in keeping with local ecological conditions and their water needs.

## Diverse Traditional Methods

*   **Guls or Kuls:** In hill and mountainous regions, particularly the Western Himalayas, people built diversion channels called 'guls' or 'kuls' for agriculture.

*   **Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting:** This was commonly practiced to store drinking water, especially in Rajasthan.
*   **Inundation Channels:** In the flood plains of Bengal, people developed inundation channels to irrigate their fields.
*   **Rain-fed Storage Structures:** In arid and semi-arid regions, agricultural fields were converted into rain-fed storage structures that allowed water to stand and moisten the soil. Examples include the 'khadins' in Jaisalmer and 'Johads' in other parts of Rajasthan.

## Tankas: Underground Storage in Rajasthan

In the semi-arid and arid regions of Rajasthan, particularly in Bikaner, Phalodi, and Barmer, almost all houses traditionally had underground tanks or 'tankas' for storing drinking water.

*   **Size:** These tanks could be as large as a big room; one household in Phalodi had a tank that was 6.1 meters deep, 4.27 meters long, and 2.44 meters wide.
*   **Structure:** The tankas were part of a well-developed rooftop rainwater harvesting system and were built inside the main house or the courtyard. They were connected to the sloping roofs of the houses through a pipe. Rain falling on the rooftops would travel down the pipe and be stored in these underground 'tankas'.
*   **Collection Process:** The first spell of rain was usually not collected, as this would clean the roofs and pipes. The rainwater from subsequent showers was then collected.

*   **Purity and Utility:** The rainwater, or *palar pani* as it's commonly referred to in these parts, is considered the purest form of natural water. It can be stored in the tankas until the next rainfall, making it an extremely reliable source of drinking water, especially during dry summers when other sources dry up. Many houses also constructed underground rooms adjoining the 'tanka' to beat the summer heat, as it would keep the room cool.
