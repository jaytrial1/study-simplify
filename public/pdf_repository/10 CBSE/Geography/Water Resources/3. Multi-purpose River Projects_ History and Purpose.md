This section delves into the historical context of hydraulic structures in India, tracing their evolution from ancient times to modern multi-purpose river projects. It explains the various benefits and objectives of these large-scale projects, which integrate different uses of water resources.

# Multi-purpose River Projects and Integrated Water Resources Management

To conserve and manage water, humans have historically constructed sophisticated hydraulic structures. Archaeological and historical records show that from ancient times, people built dams of stone rubble, reservoirs, lakes, embankments, and canals for irrigation. This tradition has continued in modern India with the construction of dams in most river basins.

## Hydraulic Structures in Ancient India

*   In the first century B.C., *Sringaverapura* near Allahabad had a sophisticated water harvesting system channeling floodwater from the River Ganga.
*   During the time of *Chandragupta Maurya*, dams, lakes, and irrigation systems were extensively built.
*   Evidences of sophisticated irrigation works have also been found in *Kalinga* (Odisha), *Nagarjunakonda* (Andhra Pradesh), *Bennur* (Karnataka), *Kolhapur* (Maharashtra), etc.
*   In the 11th Century, *Bhopal Lake*, one of the largest artificial lakes of its time, was built.
*   In the 14th Century, the tank in *Hauz Khas*, Delhi, was constructed by Iltutmish for supplying water to the Siri Fort area.

## Modern Dams and Their Multi-purpose Nature

Dams were traditionally built to impound rivers and rainwater for irrigating agricultural fields. Today, dams serve multiple purposes beyond just irrigation, including:

*   Electricity generation (hydel power)
*   Water supply for domestic and industrial uses
*   Flood control
*   Recreation
*   Inland navigation
*   Fish breeding

Hence, dams are now referred to as **multi-purpose projects**, where the many uses of the impounded water are integrated. For example, in the Sutlej-Beas river basin, the *Bhakra Nangal project* water is used for both hydel power production and irrigation. Similarly, the *Hirakud project* in the Mahanadi basin integrates water conservation with flood control.

### Key Concepts & Examples

*   **Definition of a Dam:** A dam is a barrier across flowing water that obstructs, directs, or retards the flow, often creating a reservoir, lake, or impoundment. The term "dam" refers to the reservoir rather than the structure itself in common usage. Most dams have a section called a *spillway* or *weir* over which or through which water is intended to flow intermittently or continuously.
*   **Classification of Dams:**
    *   **By Structure and Materials:** Timber dams, embankment dams, or masonry dams.
    *   **By Height:** Large dams, major dams, low dams, medium height dams, and high dams.

Multi-purpose projects, launched after Independence with their integrated water resources management approach, were seen as vehicles for national development and progress, overcoming the handicaps of the colonial past. Jawaharlal Nehru proudly proclaimed dams as the **'temples of modern India'**, believing they would integrate the development of agriculture and the village economy with rapid industrialization and the growth of the urban economy.

## Activity: Traditional Dam Building

Find out more about any one traditional method of building dams and irrigation works.

> **Answer:**
> **Traditional Method: Ahar-Pyne System (Bihar)**
>
> The *Ahar-Pyne* system is an ancient floodwater harvesting system prevalent in the south Bihar plains, particularly in the Magadh region. It is a unique example of traditional hydraulic engineering.
>
> *   **Ahar:** An ahar is a rectangular or irregular-shaped embankment built across a natural depression or a river channel. Its purpose is to impound floodwaters during the monsoon season. These are typically shallow reservoirs with embankments on three sides, with the fourth side being the natural slope of the land.
> *   **Pyne:** A pyne (or pain) is an artificial diversion channel that draws water from rivers (especially seasonal ones) and carries it to the ahars or directly to agricultural fields. These channels are designed with a gentle slope to allow water to flow by gravity.
>
> **How it works:** During the monsoon, when rivers swell, water is diverted into the pynes. These pynes then feed the ahars, which store the water. From the ahars, water is distributed to the agricultural fields through smaller channels or directly by breaching parts of the embankment. This system allowed for extensive irrigation of rice fields during the dry season, utilizing the monsoon floodwaters efficiently. The system also facilitated groundwater recharge.
>
> This method demonstrates a deep understanding of local hydrology and a community-based approach to water management, relying on natural gradients and simple construction techniques.
