## Activity

1.  Collect information on how industries are polluting our water resources.

    > **Answer:**
    > **Industrial Pollution of Water Resources:**
    > Industries are significant contributors to water pollution, discharging a wide range of harmful substances into rivers, lakes, and oceans. The primary ways industries pollute water include:
    >
    > 1.  **Direct Discharge of Untreated Effluents:** Many industries, especially those involved in manufacturing, chemicals, textiles, paper, and metallurgy, discharge wastewater directly into water bodies without adequate treatment. This effluent often contains:
    >     *   **Heavy Metals:** Such as lead, mercury, cadmium, chromium (from mining, electroplating, battery manufacturing), which are highly toxic, non-biodegradable, and bioaccumulate in the food chain.
    >     *   **Toxic Chemicals:** Acids, alkalis, dyes, phenols, cyanides (from chemical, textile, and pharmaceutical industries) that can alter water pH, deplete oxygen, and be lethal to aquatic life and humans.
    >     *   **Organic Pollutants:** Including oils, greases, and other organic compounds that increase the biological oxygen demand (BOD) of water, leading to oxygen depletion and affecting aquatic ecosystems.
    > 2.  **Thermal Pollution:** Industries like power plants (especially thermal power plants) use large quantities of water for cooling. They then discharge heated water back into rivers or lakes. This increase in water temperature reduces dissolved oxygen levels, affecting aquatic life and altering ecological balance.
    > 3.  **Sedimentation:** Mining and construction industries can release large amounts of suspended solids and sediments into water bodies, increasing turbidity, reducing light penetration, and smothering aquatic habitats.
    > 4.  **Radioactive Waste:** Nuclear power plants and industries using radioactive materials generate radioactive waste, which, if improperly disposed of, can contaminate water sources for thousands of years, posing severe health risks.
    > 5.  **Non-point Source Pollution:** Runoff from industrial areas can pick up pollutants like chemicals, oils, and debris, carrying them into water bodies.
    >
    > The consequences of industrial water pollution are severe, including loss of biodiversity, contamination of drinking water sources, health issues in humans and animals, and damage to agricultural land. Regulations and effective wastewater treatment are crucial to mitigate this problem.

## Multiple Choice Questions

(i) Based on the information given below, classify each of the situations as 'suffering from water scarcity' or 'not suffering from water scarcity'.

(a) Region with high annual rainfall.
> **Answer:** Not suffering from water scarcity (potentially, but not necessarily, as quality or access can be issues).

(b) Region having high annual rainfall and large population.
> **Answer:** Suffering from water scarcity (due to high demand and potential over-exploitation).

(c) Region having high annual rainfall but water is highly polluted.
> **Answer:** Suffering from water scarcity (due to bad quality, making it unusable).

(d) Region having low rainfall and low population.
> **Answer:** Not suffering from water scarcity (if population's needs are met by the limited rainfall, or if efficient management is in place).

(a) Multi-purpose projects bring water to those areas which suffer from water scarcity.

> **Answer:** (c) Multi-purpose projects lead to large scale displacements and loss of livelihood. (This is a disadvantage/criticism, not an argument *in favour*.)

## Short Answer Questions (about 30 words)

(i) Explain how water becomes a renewable resource.

> **Answer:** Water is a renewable resource because it is continuously renewed and recharged through the *hydrological cycle*. This cycle involves evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and runoff, ensuring a constant circulation and replenishment of water on Earth.

(ii) What is water scarcity and what are its main causes?

> **Answer:** Water scarcity refers to the shortage of water relative to demand. Its main causes include over-exploitation, excessive use, unequal access among social groups, large populations, increased agricultural and industrial demands, and pollution making existing water unusable.

(iii) Compare the advantages and disadvantages of multi-purpose river projects.

> **Answer:**
> **Advantages:** Provide irrigation, generate electricity, supply water for domestic/industrial use, control floods, offer recreation, and support navigation/fish breeding.
> **Disadvantages:** Lead to displacement, loss of livelihoods, ecological problems like altered sediment flow, fragmented rivers, submergence, induced floods, soil erosion, and water-borne diseases.

## Long Answer Questions (about 120 words)

(i) Discuss how rainwater harvesting in semi-arid regions of Rajasthan is carried out.

> **Answer:** In the semi-arid and arid regions of Rajasthan, particularly in areas like Bikaner, Phalodi, and Barmer, rainwater harvesting is traditionally carried out through a sophisticated system involving underground tanks called *tankas*. These *tankas* are built within the main house or courtyard and are connected to the sloping rooftops of the houses via pipes. When it rains, water from the rooftops flows down these pipes and is collected in the *tankas*. The first spell of rain is usually not collected, as it serves to clean the roofs and pipes. Subsequent rainfall is then collected. This *palar pani* (rainwater) is considered the purest form of natural water and is stored in the *tankas* until the next rainfall, providing a highly reliable source of drinking water, especially during the dry summer months. Many households also construct underground rooms adjoining the *tankas* to benefit from the cool temperatures maintained by the stored water. This system demonstrates an intelligent adaptation to arid conditions for essential water supply.

(ii) Describe how modern adaptations of traditional rainwater harvesting methods are being carried out to conserve and store water.

> **Answer:** Modern adaptations of traditional rainwater harvesting methods are being successfully implemented across rural and urban India to conserve and store water, often integrating contemporary materials and engineering with age-old principles. For instance, in Gendathur village in Mysuru, Karnataka, nearly 200 households have installed rooftop rainwater harvesting systems. Here, PVC pipes are used to collect water from rooftops, which is then filtered using sand and bricks before being channeled into an underground sump for immediate use. Excess water from the sump is directed to a well, recharging the underground aquifer, from which water can be drawn later using hand pumps. This system efficiently collects a significant amount of water annually, making the village water-rich despite its remote location. Similarly, in Shillong, Meghalaya, despite high rainfall in nearby areas, the capital faces water scarcity, prompting nearly every household to adopt rooftop rainwater harvesting to meet a substantial portion of their water needs. These modern adaptations emphasize groundwater recharge and direct storage, ensuring water availability and promoting sustainable practices by combining traditional wisdom with accessible technology.
