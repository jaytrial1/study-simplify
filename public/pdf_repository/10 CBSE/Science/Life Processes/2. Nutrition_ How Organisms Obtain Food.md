This section delves into the fundamental process of nutrition, explaining how different organisms acquire and process food to meet their energy and material requirements.

# Nutrition

All organisms require energy and materials for growth, development, and synthesizing necessary substances. This comes from the food they consume.

## How do living things get their food?

While the need for energy and materials is universal, organisms fulfill this requirement in diverse ways:

*   **Autotrophs:** These organisms use simple inorganic materials like carbon dioxide and water to synthesize their own food. Examples include green plants and some bacteria.
*   **Heterotrophs:** These organisms obtain complex substances from other organisms. They must break down these complex substances into simpler ones using *bio-catalysts* called **enzymes** before they can be used. Heterotrophs, including animals and fungi, depend directly or indirectly on autotrophs for their survival.

### Autotrophic Nutrition

The carbon and energy needs of autotrophic organisms are met through **photosynthesis**. This is the process by which autotrophs take in external substances and convert them into stored forms of energy. Carbon dioxide and water are converted into carbohydrates (like glucose) in the presence of *sunlight* and *chlorophyll*. These carbohydrates are then used to provide energy to the plant.

The carbohydrates not immediately used are stored as **starch**, serving as an internal energy reserve for future use. A similar process occurs in humans, where excess energy from food is stored as *glycogen*.

The following events occur during photosynthesis:
1.  **Absorption of light energy by chlorophyll.**
2.  **Conversion of light energy to chemical energy** and the *splitting of water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen*.
3.  **Reduction of carbon dioxide to carbohydrates.**

These steps do not necessarily happen immediately one after the other. For instance, desert plants absorb carbon dioxide at night and produce an intermediate compound, which is then acted upon by the energy absorbed by chlorophyll during the day.

Each component of the photosynthesis reaction is crucial. If you observe a cross-section of a leaf under a microscope, you will see green dots within some cells. These are **chloroplasts**, the cell organelles that contain *chlorophyll*.

### Activity 5.1: Chlorophyll is Essential for Photosynthesis

*   **Objective:** To demonstrate that chlorophyll is essential for photosynthesis.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Take a potted plant with variegated leaves (e.g., money plant or crotons).
    2.  Keep the plant in a dark room for three days to ensure all stored starch is used up.
    3.  Place the plant in sunlight for about six hours.
    4.  Pluck a leaf, mark its green areas, and trace them on paper.
    5.  Dip the leaf in boiling water for a few minutes.
    6.  Immerse it in a beaker containing alcohol and heat it in a water-bath until the alcohol boils.
    7.  Dip the decolorized leaf in a dilute iodine solution for a few minutes, then rinse it.
    8.  Observe the color of the leaf and compare it with your initial tracing.
*   **Observation:**
    *   What happens to the colour of the leaf? The leaf loses its green color and becomes pale or yellowish as chlorophyll is removed by the alcohol.
    *   What is the colour of the solution? The alcohol solution turns green due to the dissolved chlorophyll.
    *   After dipping in iodine, the areas of the leaf that were originally green turn *blue-black*, while the areas that were yellowish (non-green) remain *yellowish-brown* (the color of iodine).
*   **Conclusion:** The blue-black color indicates the presence of starch. Since only the green parts of the variegated leaf (those containing chlorophyll) turned blue-black, it concludes that **chlorophyll is essential for photosynthesis**, as starch (a product of photosynthesis) is only formed where chlorophyll is present.

### How Plants Obtain Carbon Dioxide

Plants obtain carbon dioxide through **stomata**, tiny pores on the surface of leaves. Massive amounts of gaseous exchange (carbon dioxide for photosynthesis, and oxygen release) occur through these pores. Gas exchange also occurs across the surfaces of stems and roots.

Stomata can lose large amounts of water through transpiration. To prevent excessive water loss, plants close these pores when they don't need carbon dioxide. The opening and closing of stomatal pores are regulated by **guard cells**. Guard cells swell when water flows into them, causing the stomatal pore to open. Conversely, the pore closes if the guard cells shrink.

### Activity 5.2: Carbon Dioxide is Essential for Photosynthesis

*   **Objective:** To demonstrate that carbon dioxide is essential for photosynthesis.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Take two healthy potted plants of similar size.
    2.  Keep them in a dark room for three days to de-starch them.
    3.  Place each plant on separate glass plates.
    4.  Place a watch-glass containing *potassium hydroxide (KOH)* next to one plant (KOH absorbs carbon dioxide).
    5.  Cover both plants with separate bell-jars, sealing the bottom with vaseline to make the setup air-tight.
    6.  Keep the plants in sunlight for about two hours.
    7.  Pluck a leaf from each plant and test for starch using the iodine test (as in Activity 5.1).
*   **Observation:**
    *   Do both the leaves show the presence of the same amount of starch? No. The leaf from the plant kept with potassium hydroxide will show *no or very little starch* (it will remain yellowish-brown with iodine), while the leaf from the plant without KOH will show *starch* (it will turn blue-black with iodine).
*   **Conclusion:** The plant kept with KOH did not perform photosynthesis efficiently because carbon dioxide was absorbed by KOH, indicating that **carbon dioxide is essential for photosynthesis**.

### Other Raw Materials for Plants

Besides carbon dioxide and water, autotrophs need other raw materials for building their body. Water for photosynthesis is absorbed from the soil by the roots in terrestrial plants. Other essential materials like **nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, and magnesium** are also taken up from the soil.

*   **Nitrogen** is vital for synthesizing proteins and other compounds. Plants absorb it in the form of inorganic *nitrates* or *nitrites*, or as organic compounds prepared by bacteria from atmospheric nitrogen.

### Heterotrophic Nutrition

Heterotrophic organisms obtain food from other organisms. The form of nutrition varies depending on the type and availability of food material, and how the organism obtains it.

*   **Saprophytic Nutrition:** Some organisms break down food material *outside* their body and then absorb it. Examples include fungi like bread moulds, yeast, and mushrooms.
*   **Holozoic Nutrition:** Others take in whole food material and break it down *inside* their bodies. What can be taken in and broken down depends on the organism's body design and functioning.
*   **Parasitic Nutrition:** Some organisms derive nutrition from plants or animals *without killing them*. This parasitic strategy is employed by a wide variety of organisms such as *Cuscuta* (amar-bel), ticks, lice, leeches, and tapeworms.

### How do Organisms obtain their Nutrition?

The digestive system varies among organisms based on their food source and method of obtaining food.

*   **Single-celled Organisms:** In single-celled organisms, food may be taken in by the entire cell surface. As organism complexity increases, different parts become specialized.
    *   **Amoeba:** Takes in food using temporary finger-like extensions of the cell surface called **pseudopodia**, which fuse over the food particle to form a **food-vacuole**. Inside the food-vacuole, complex substances are broken down into simpler ones that diffuse into the cytoplasm. Undigested material is moved to the cell surface and expelled.
    *   **Paramoecium:** Has a definite shape and takes food in at a specific spot. Food is moved to this spot by the rhythmic movement of **cilia** covering the cell surface.

### Nutrition in Human Beings

The human digestive system consists of an **alimentary canal**, a long tube extending from the mouth to the anus, with specialized regions for different functions.

### Activity 5.3: Action of Saliva on Starch

*   **Objective:** To demonstrate the action of saliva on starch.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Take 1 mL of 1% starch solution in two test tubes (A and B).
    2.  Add 1 mL of saliva to test tube A. Leave both test tubes undisturbed for 20-30 minutes.
    3.  Add a few drops of dilute iodine solution to both test tubes.
*   **Observation:**
    *   In which test tube do you observe a colour change? Test tube B (without saliva) will show a *blue-black* color change, indicating the presence of starch. Test tube A (with saliva) will show *no color change* or a very faint yellowish-brown color, indicating the absence or significant reduction of starch.
*   **Conclusion:** This indicates that saliva contains an enzyme (salivary amylase) that breaks down starch into simpler sugars.

### Digestion in Human Beings

Food undergoes several processing steps as it moves through the digestive tract:

1.  **Mouth:**
    *   Food is *crushed by teeth* into smaller particles and *wetted by saliva* for smooth passage.
    *   **Saliva**, secreted by salivary glands, contains an enzyme called **salivary amylase** (or ptyalin) which begins the digestion of complex starch molecules into simple sugars.
    *   The muscular tongue thoroughly mixes food with saliva during chewing.

2.  **Oesophagus (Food Pipe):**
    *   Food moves from the mouth to the stomach via the oesophagus.
    *   The lining of the alimentary canal has muscles that contract rhythmically to push food forward. These wave-like contractions are called **peristaltic movements** and occur throughout the gut.

3.  **Stomach:**
    *   The stomach is a large, expandable organ. Its muscular walls mix food thoroughly with digestive juices.
    *   **Gastric glands** in the stomach wall release:
        *   **Hydrochloric acid (HCl):** Creates an *acidic medium* necessary for the action of the enzyme pepsin.
        *   **Pepsin:** A protein-digesting enzyme.
        *   **Mucus:** Protects the inner lining of the stomach from the corrosive action of the acid under normal conditions.
    *   The acidic environment of the stomach also kills bacteria that may enter with food.
    *   The exit of food from the stomach into the small intestine is regulated by a **sphincter muscle**.

4.  **Small Intestine:**
    *   This is the *longest part* of the alimentary canal, extensively coiled to fit into the abdomen. Its length varies with diet (e.g., herbivores have longer intestines for cellulose digestion; carnivores have shorter ones).
    *   It is the primary site for the **complete digestion of carbohydrates, proteins, and fats**.
    *   It receives secretions from the **liver** and **pancreas**:
        *   **Bile juice (from liver):** Makes the acidic food from the stomach *alkaline* for pancreatic enzymes to act. It also *emulsifies large fat globules* into smaller ones, increasing the surface area for enzyme action.
        *   **Pancreatic juice (from pancreas):** Contains enzymes like **trypsin** (for protein digestion) and **lipase** (for breaking down emulsified fats).
    *   The walls of the small intestine also contain glands that secrete **intestinal juice**. The enzymes in this juice complete digestion:
        *   Proteins are converted to **amino acids**.
        *   Complex carbohydrates are converted to **glucose**.
        *   Fats are converted to **fatty acids and glycerol**.
    *   **Absorption:** Digested food is absorbed by the intestinal walls. The inner lining has numerous finger-like projections called **villi**, which significantly *increase the surface area for absorption*. Villi are richly supplied with blood vessels that transport absorbed food to all body cells for energy, tissue building, and repair.

5.  **Large Intestine:**
    *   Unabsorbed food is passed into the large intestine.
    *   Its wall absorbs more water from the remaining material.
    *   The rest of the undigested material is removed from the body via the anus, regulated by the **anal sphincter**.

### Dental Caries

**Dental caries**, or tooth decay, involves the gradual softening of tooth enamel and dentine. It starts when bacteria, acting on sugars, produce acids that demineralize the enamel. Masses of bacterial cells mixed with food particles form **dental plaque** on teeth. Plaque prevents saliva from reaching the tooth surface to neutralize the acid. Regular brushing after eating removes plaque before bacteria can produce acids. If untreated, microorganisms can invade the pulp, leading to inflammation and infection.

2.  Where do plants get each of the raw materials required for photosynthesis?
    > **Answer:** Plants obtain the raw materials for photosynthesis from the following sources:
    > *   **Carbon dioxide (CO₂):** Obtained from the atmosphere through tiny pores called *stomata* present on the surface of leaves.
    > *   **Water (H₂O):** Absorbed from the soil by the roots and transported upwards to the leaves through the xylem vessels.
    > *   **Sunlight:** Absorbed by the *chlorophyll* pigment present in the chloroplasts of leaf cells.
    > *   **Chlorophyll:** Synthesized by the plant itself within the chloroplasts.
    > *   **Other minerals (e.g., Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Iron, Magnesium):** Absorbed from the soil by the roots. These are crucial for building plant structures and components like proteins.

3.  What is the role of the acid in our stomach?
    > **Answer:** The hydrochloric acid (HCl) in our stomach serves two main roles:
    > *   **Creates an acidic medium:** This acidic environment is essential for the activation and optimal functioning of the protein-digesting enzyme, *pepsin*. Pepsin works effectively only in acidic conditions.
    > *   **Kills harmful bacteria:** The strong acidity of the stomach helps to kill most bacteria and other microorganisms that may enter the digestive system along with food, thus preventing infections.

4.  What is the function of digestive enzymes?
    > **Answer:** Digestive enzymes are *biological catalysts* that play a crucial role in the digestion of food. Their main function is to **break down complex, large food molecules into simpler, smaller molecules** that can be easily absorbed by the body. For example:
    > *   **Carbohydrases (like amylase):** Break down complex carbohydrates (starch) into simple sugars (glucose).
    > *   **Proteases (like pepsin, trypsin):** Break down proteins into amino acids.
    > *   **Lipases:** Break down fats into fatty acids and glycerol.
    > Without enzymes, the digestion process would be extremely slow or not occur at all, making it impossible for the body to absorb nutrients.

5.  How is the small intestine designed to absorb digested food?
    > **Answer:** The small intestine is highly adapted for efficient absorption of digested food due to its unique design:
    > *   **Length:** It is very long (about 6.5 meters in adults) and extensively coiled, providing a large surface area for absorption.
    > *   **Villi:** Its inner lining has millions of tiny, finger-like projections called *villi* (singular: villus). These villi dramatically increase the internal surface area for absorption.
    > *   **Microvilli:** The cells lining the villi also have microscopic, brush-like projections called *microvilli*, further enhancing the surface area.
    > *   **Rich Blood Supply:** Each villus is richly supplied with a dense network of blood vessels (capillaries) and lymph vessels (lacteals). This ensures that absorbed nutrients are quickly transported away from the intestine into the bloodstream and lymphatic system, maintaining a steep concentration gradient for continuous absorption.
    > *   **Thin Walls:** The walls of the villi are very thin (only one cell thick), allowing for rapid diffusion and active transport of digested nutrients into the blood.
