This section focuses on the circulatory systems in humans and plants, detailing how essential substances like food, oxygen, water, and minerals are transported throughout the organisms.

# Transportation

### Transportation in Human Beings

Blood transports food, oxygen, and waste materials throughout our bodies. Blood is a fluid connective tissue consisting of a fluid medium called **plasma** (which transports food, carbon dioxide, and nitrogenous wastes in dissolved form) and suspended cells. Oxygen is primarily carried by **red blood corpuscles**. Other substances like salts are also transported by blood.

To facilitate this transport, a pumping organ (the heart), a network of tubes (blood vessels), and a system for repairing damage are needed.

### Our Pump - The Heart

The **heart** is a muscular organ about the size of our fist. It has different chambers to prevent oxygen-rich blood from mixing with carbon dioxide-rich blood. This separation is crucial for efficient oxygen supply. Deoxygenated blood goes to the lungs for carbon dioxide removal, and oxygenated blood returns to the heart to be pumped to the rest of the body.

### Blood Flow through the Heart (Double Circulation)

1.  **Oxygen-rich blood** from the lungs enters the thin-walled upper left chamber, the **left atrium**, which relaxes to collect this blood.
2.  The left atrium then contracts, transferring blood to the **left ventricle**, which relaxes at this time.
3.  When the muscular left ventricle contracts, it pumps the oxygenated blood out to the rest of the body via the aorta.
4.  **Deoxygenated blood** from the body enters the upper right chamber, the **right atrium**, as it relaxes.
5.  As the right atrium contracts, blood is transferred to the corresponding lower chamber, the **right ventricle**, which dilates.
6.  The right ventricle then pumps this deoxygenated blood to the lungs for oxygenation via the pulmonary arteries.

Ventricles have thicker muscular walls than atria because they have to pump blood into various organs against higher pressure. **Valves** within the heart ensure that blood flows in only one direction and does not flow backward when atria or ventricles contract.

### Blood Pressure

**Blood pressure** is the force that blood exerts against the wall of a blood vessel. It is significantly higher in arteries than in veins.

*   **Systolic pressure:** The pressure of blood inside the artery during ventricular *systole* (contraction of the ventricles). Normal systolic pressure is about 120 mm of Hg.
*   **Diastolic pressure:** The pressure in the artery during ventricular *diastole* (relaxation of the ventricles). Normal diastolic pressure is 80 mm of Hg.

### The Tubes - Blood Vessels

Blood vessels form a network of tubes that carry blood throughout the body:

*   **Arteries:** These vessels carry blood *away* from the heart to various organs. Since blood emerges from the heart under high pressure, arteries have thick, elastic walls to withstand this pressure.
*   **Veins:** These vessels collect blood from different organs and bring it *back* to the heart. They do not need thick walls because the blood is no longer under high pressure. Instead, veins have **valves** that ensure blood flows only in one direction, preventing backflow.
*   **Capillaries:** On reaching an organ or tissue, arteries divide into smaller and smaller vessels, eventually forming the smallest vessels called capillaries. Capillaries have walls that are only one cell thick, allowing for the efficient exchange of materials (oxygen, nutrients, waste products) between the blood and surrounding cells. Capillaries then join together to form venules, which merge to form veins.

### Maintenance by Platelets

If the circulatory system develops a leak (e.g., from an injury), the loss of blood and pressure must be minimized. **Platelet cells** circulate in the blood and help to clot the blood at points of injury, plugging the leaks and preventing excessive blood loss.

### Lymph

Another fluid involved in transportation is **lymph**, also known as tissue fluid. Some plasma, proteins, and blood cells escape through the pores in capillary walls into the intercellular spaces of tissues, forming this tissue fluid. Lymph is similar to blood plasma but is colorless and contains less protein.

Lymph drains into **lymphatic capillaries** from the intercellular spaces, which then join to form larger lymph vessels. These larger vessels eventually open into larger veins, returning the fluid to the bloodstream. Lymph plays crucial roles:

*   It carries digested and absorbed fat from the intestine.
*   It drains excess fluid from the extracellular space back into the blood, helping to maintain fluid balance.

### Transportation in Plants

Plants also require a transport system. They take in simple compounds like CO₂ and photosynthesize energy in their chlorophyll-containing organs (leaves). Other raw materials for building plant bodies are absorbed separately. The soil is the primary source of raw materials like nitrogen, phosphorus, and other minerals, which are absorbed by the roots.

If the distances between soil-contacting organs (roots) and chlorophyll-containing organs (leaves) are small, simple diffusion might suffice. However, in larger plants, diffusion alone is insufficient to provide raw materials to leaves and energy to roots. Therefore, a specialized transportation system is essential.

Plants have lower energy needs than animals because they do not move, and a large proportion of their tissues consist of dead cells. This allows them to use relatively slow transport systems. Despite this, the distances over which transport systems must operate can be very large in tall trees.

Plant transport systems move energy stores from leaves and raw materials from roots. These two pathways are independently organized conducting tubes:

*   **Xylem:** Transports water and minerals absorbed from the soil.
*   **Phloem:** Transports the products of photosynthesis (food) from the leaves (where they are synthesized) to other parts of the plant, including storage organs (roots, fruits, seeds) and growing regions.

### Transport of Water in Plants

In **xylem tissue**, vessels and tracheids of the roots, stems, and leaves are interconnected, forming a continuous system of water-conducting channels throughout the plant.

*   **Root Pressure:** At the roots, cells actively take up ions from the soil. This creates a concentration difference, causing water to move into the root from the soil by osmosis. This steady movement of water into the root xylem creates a column of water that is steadily pushed upwards. However, root pressure alone is usually not enough to move water to significant heights in tall plants.

*   **Transpiration Pull:** Plants use another strategy to move water upwards: **transpiration**. This is the loss of water in the form of vapor from the aerial parts of the plant, primarily through stomata in the leaves.
    *   Evaporation of water molecules from the cells of a leaf creates a *suction force* (transpiration pull).
    *   This suction pulls water from the xylem vessels in the leaf, which in turn pulls water continuously from the xylem in the stem and roots.
    *   As long as the plant has an adequate water supply, the water lost through stomata is replaced by water drawn from the xylem.

### Activity 5.8: Demonstrating Transpiration

*   **Objective:** To demonstrate the process of transpiration in plants.
*   **Procedure:**
    1.  Take two small pots of similar size with the same amount of soil. One should have a plant, the other a stick of the same height.
    2.  Cover the soil in both pots with a plastic sheet to prevent evaporation from the soil.
    3.  Cover both the plant and the stick (with their pots) with separate plastic sheets (or bell jars) and place them in bright sunlight for half an hour.

*   **Conclusion:** This demonstrates that the plant releases water vapor into the air, a process known as **transpiration**. The stick, being non-living, does not transpire.

Transpiration helps in the absorption and upward movement of water and dissolved minerals from roots to leaves. It also aids in temperature regulation of the plant. While root pressure is more significant at night, the **transpiration pull** becomes the major driving force for water movement in the xylem during the day when stomata are open.

### Transport of Food and Other Substances in Plants

The transport of soluble products of photosynthesis (food) from the leaves, where they are formed, to other parts of the plant is called **translocation**. This process occurs in the **phloem** part of the vascular tissue.

Besides photosynthetic products, the phloem also transports amino acids and other substances. These substances are specifically delivered to:
*   Storage organs (roots, fruits, and seeds).
*   Growing organs (buds, young leaves).

Translocation in phloem takes place in the **sieve tubes** with the help of adjacent **companion cells**. Unlike water transport in xylem (which is largely driven by physical forces like transpiration pull), translocation in phloem is an *active process* that utilizes energy.

*   Materials like sucrose are transferred into phloem tissue using energy from **ATP**.
*   This active loading increases the osmotic pressure within the phloem tissue, causing water to move into it from adjacent xylem by osmosis.
*   This pressure then moves the material in the phloem to tissues that have lower pressure (sink areas, where the substances are being used or stored).
*   This mechanism allows the phloem to transport materials according to the plant's needs. For example, in spring, sugars stored in root or stem tissue are transported to the growing buds that require energy for their development.

### Questions

1.  What are the components of the transport system in human beings? What are the functions of these components?
    > **Answer:** The components of the transport system (circulatory system) in human beings are:
    > *   **Heart:** A muscular pumping organ that continuously pumps blood throughout the body. It ensures that oxygenated blood is sent to the body and deoxygenated blood is sent to the lungs for oxygenation.
    > *   **Blood:** A fluid connective tissue that acts as the transport medium.
    >     *   **Plasma:** Transports food, carbon dioxide, nitrogenous wastes, hormones, and salts in dissolved form.
    >     *   **Red Blood Corpuscles (RBCs):** Contain haemoglobin, which transports oxygen from the lungs to the body cells.
    >     *   **White Blood Cells (WBCs):** Provide immunity by fighting infections and foreign particles.
    >     *   **Platelets:** Help in blood clotting at the site of injury to prevent excessive blood loss.
    > *   **Blood Vessels:** A network of tubes through which blood circulates.
    >     *   **Arteries:** Carry oxygenated blood away from the heart to various organs (except pulmonary artery). They have thick, elastic walls.
    >     *   **Veins:** Carry deoxygenated blood from various organs back to the heart (except pulmonary vein). They have thinner walls and valves to prevent backflow.
    >     *   **Capillaries:** Microscopic, thin-walled vessels that connect arteries and veins. They are the sites where exchange of gases, nutrients, hormones, and wastes occurs between blood and body cells.
    > *   **Lymphatic System (including Lymph):** A secondary transport system that collects tissue fluid (lymph) and returns it to the bloodstream, transports digested fats, and plays a role in immunity.

2.  Why is it necessary to separate oxygenated and deoxygenated blood in mammals and birds?
    > **Answer:** It is necessary to separate oxygenated and deoxygenated blood in mammals and birds because:
    > *   **High Energy Needs:** Mammals and birds are endothermic (warm-blooded) animals, meaning they constantly use energy to maintain a high and constant body temperature, regardless of the external environment.
    > *   **Efficient Oxygen Supply:** Maintaining a constant body temperature and performing high metabolic activities requires a large and efficient supply of oxygen to the cells for aerobic respiration.
    > *   **Preventing Mixing:** Separating oxygenated and deoxygenated blood ensures that the body receives the maximum possible amount of oxygen. If the two types of blood were to mix, the oxygen-rich blood would be diluted with oxygen-poor blood, reducing the efficiency of oxygen delivery and consequently lowering energy production. This separation allows for a highly efficient circulatory system that meets their high energy demands.

3.  What are the components of the transport system in highly organised plants?
    > **Answer:** The transport system in highly organized plants consists of specialized vascular tissues:
    > *   **Xylem:** Responsible for the transport of water and dissolved minerals from the roots upwards to all aerial parts of the plant (leaves, stems, flowers). It consists of vessels and tracheids.
    > *   **Phloem:** Responsible for the transport of soluble products of photosynthesis (sugars like sucrose, amino acids, and other organic substances) from the leaves (where they are synthesized) to other parts of the plant, including storage organs (roots, fruits, seeds) and growing regions. It consists of sieve tubes and companion cells.

4.  How are water and minerals transported in plants?
    > **Answer:** Water and minerals are transported in plants primarily through the **xylem tissue** by two main mechanisms:
    > *   **Root Pressure:** At night, when transpiration is low, root cells actively absorb ions from the soil. This creates an osmotic gradient, causing water to move from the soil into the root xylem. This inward movement of water generates a positive pressure, called root pressure, which pushes the water column upwards to a certain extent.
    > *   **Transpiration Pull (Cohesion-Tension Theory):** This is the major driving force during the day.
    >     *   Water evaporates from the aerial parts of the plant (mainly leaves) through stomata in a process called **transpiration**.
    >     *   As water molecules evaporate from the leaf cells, it creates a negative pressure or "pull" (transpiration pull) in the xylem vessels of the leaf.
    >     *   Due to the cohesive forces between water molecules (they stick to each other) and adhesive forces between water molecules and xylem walls, a continuous column of water is maintained throughout the xylem from roots to leaves.
    >     *   The transpiration pull generated in the leaves pulls this entire water column upwards, drawing water and dissolved minerals from the roots.

5.  How is food transported in plants?
    > **Answer:** Food (primarily in the form of sucrose, a soluble carbohydrate) and other organic substances like amino acids are transported in plants through the **phloem tissue** by a process called **translocation**.
    > *   **Loading of Phloem:** Food synthesized in the leaves (source) is actively transported (requiring ATP energy) into the sieve tubes of the phloem.
    > *   **Osmotic Pressure Gradient:** This active loading of solutes increases the osmotic pressure within the sieve tubes, causing water to move from the adjacent xylem into the phloem by osmosis.
    > *   **Mass Flow:** The increased pressure at the source pushes the phloem sap (water and dissolved food) through the sieve tubes to regions of lower pressure (sink areas), where food is either utilized or stored.
    > *   **Unloading at Sink:** At the sink (e.g., roots, fruits, growing buds), the food is actively unloaded from the phloem, which reduces the osmotic pressure in the sieve tubes, causing water to move back into the xylem.
    > This process allows for the transport of food in both upward and downward directions, depending on the plant's metabolic needs and the location of source and sink tissues.
