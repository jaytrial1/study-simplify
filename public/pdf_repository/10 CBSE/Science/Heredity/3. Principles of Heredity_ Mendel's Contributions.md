# Principles of Heredity: Mendel's Contributions

The primary outcome of reproduction is the generation of individuals with a similar basic design. The rules of *heredity* govern how *traits* and characteristics are reliably *inherited*.

## Inherited Traits

When we talk about similarities and differences, we observe that a child possesses all the basic features of a human being, yet does not look exactly like its parents. Human populations exhibit a significant degree of *variation*.

## Mendel's Contributions to Inheritance

The rules for *inheritance* of traits in human beings are linked to the fact that both the father and the mother contribute practically equal amounts of *genetic material* (*DNA*) to the child. This means that each *trait* can be influenced by both paternal and maternal *DNA*. Consequently, for each *trait*, there will be two *versions* (or *alleles*) in each child. The question then arises: which *trait* will be expressed in the child?

Gregor Mendel, an Augustinian friar and scientist, worked out the main rules of such *inheritance* more than a century ago.

**Key Information Conveyed:**
*   **Subject:** The portrait depicts Gregor Mendel, widely recognized as the "father of modern genetics."
*   **Significance:** Mendel's pioneering work laid the foundation for the scientific field of heredity. Through his meticulous experiments with pea plants in the monastery gardens, he deduced fundamental principles of inheritance.
*   **Contributions to Heredity:** His research elucidated concepts such as dominant and recessive traits, segregation of alleles, and independent assortment of genes, which are now known as Mendel's Laws of Inheritance. These laws provided the first quantitative and predictive understanding of how traits are passed from one generation to the next, forming the cornerstone of classical genetics.*

Mendel, after studying science and mathematics at the University of Vienna, returned to his monastery and began growing peas. Unlike others who had studied *inheritance* before him, Mendel uniquely combined his knowledge of science and mathematics by meticulously counting individuals exhibiting specific *traits* in each generation. This quantitative approach was crucial in helping him formulate the *laws of inheritance*.

Mendel used several *contrasting visible characters* of garden peas, such as:
*   Round/wrinkled seeds
*   Tall/short plants
*   White/violet flowers

He conducted experiments by crossing pea plants with different characteristics – for example, a *tall plant* and a *short plant*. He then observed the progeny and calculated the percentages of tall or short offspring.

### The Monohybrid Cross: Dominant and Recessive Traits

In his first-generation (*F1 progeny*) crosses, Mendel observed no intermediate characteristics – for example, no 'medium-height' plants. All *F1* plants were *tall*. This indicated that only one of the parental *traits* was expressed, not a mixture.

The next question was whether the *tall* plants in the *F1 generation* were genetically identical to the *tall* plants of the *parent generation*. Mendel tested this by allowing both the parental plants and the *F1 tall plants* to reproduce by self-pollination.
*   The progeny of the parental *tall plants* were, of course, all *tall*.
*   However, the second-generation (*F2 progeny*) of the *F1 tall plants* were not all tall. Instead, one quarter of them were *short*.

Figure 8.3 illustrates the *inheritance of traits* over two generations. This experiment indicated that both the *tallness* and *shortness traits* were *inherited* in the *F1 plants*, but only the *tallness trait* was *expressed*. This led Mendel to propose that sexually reproducing organisms possess two copies of factors (now called *genes*) controlling *traits*. These two copies may be identical or different, depending on the parentage. This assumption helps in working out a pattern of *inheritance*, as shown in Fig. 8.3.

In this explanation, both *TT* and *Tt* represent *tall plants*, while only *tt* represents a *short plant*. This means that a single copy of the '*T*' factor is sufficient to make the plant tall, whereas both copies must be '*t*' for the plant to be short. Traits like '*T*' are called *dominant traits*, while those that behave like '*t*' are called *recessive traits*.

## Activity 8.2: Confirming F2 Ratios

In Fig. 8.3, what experiment would we do to confirm that the F2 generation did in fact have a 1:2:1 ratio of TT, Tt and tt trait combinations?

> **Answer to Activity 8.2:**
> To confirm the 1:2:1 genotypic ratio (TT:Tt:tt) in the F2 generation, a *test cross* would be performed.
> *   **Procedure:** Take each individual from the F2 generation and cross it with a *homozygous recessive* parent (tt, short plant).
> *   **Expected Outcomes:**
>     *   If an F2 *tall plant* is *TT* (homozygous dominant), all its offspring from the test cross will be *Tt* (tall).
>     *   If an F2 *tall plant* is *Tt* (heterozygous), its offspring from the test cross will be 50% *Tt* (tall) and 50% *tt* (short).
>     *   If an F2 *short plant* is *tt* (homozygous recessive), all its offspring from the test cross will be *tt* (short).
> By observing the phenotypes of the offspring from these test crosses, one can determine the genotype of each F2 individual, thus confirming the 1:2:1 genotypic ratio.

### Key Concepts & Examples

*   **Gene:** A segment of *DNA* that carries the genetic information for a specific *trait* or protein.
*   **Allele:** Different *versions* of a *gene*. For example, for the *gene* controlling plant height, there are *alleles* for tallness ('T') and shortness ('t').
*   **Dominant Trait/Allele:** A *trait* (or *allele*) that is expressed when only one copy is present (e.g., 'T' for tallness). It masks the expression of the *recessive allele*.
*   **Recessive Trait/Allele:** A *trait* (or *allele*) that is only expressed when two copies are present (e.g., 'tt' for shortness). Its expression is masked by a *dominant allele*.
*   **Genotype:** The *genetic makeup* of an organism, referring to the specific combination of *alleles* it possesses for a *trait* (e.g., TT, Tt, tt).
*   **Phenotype:** The *observable physical characteristics* or *traits* of an organism, resulting from its *genotype* and environmental interactions (e.g., tall, short).
