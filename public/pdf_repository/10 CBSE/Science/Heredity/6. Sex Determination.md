This section explores how the sex of an offspring is determined, highlighting different strategies in various species and focusing on the genetic basis of sex determination in human beings through sex chromosomes.

# Sex Determination

The two sexes involved in *sexual reproduction* often exhibit differences. How is the *sex* of a newborn individual determined? Different species employ diverse strategies:
*   **Environmental Cues:** In some animals, such as certain reptiles, the temperature at which fertilized eggs are incubated determines whether the developing animals will be male or female.
*   **Sex Change:** Animals like snails can change their *sex*, indicating that *sex* is not always genetically determined.
*   **Genetic Determination:** In human beings, the *sex* of an individual is largely *genetically determined*. The *genes* inherited from our parents decide whether we will be boys or girls.

## Sex Chromosomes in Humans

While most human *chromosomes* come in perfectly matched pairs (22 pairs of *autosomes*, with one maternal and one paternal copy), one pair, known as the *sex chromosomes*, is not always a perfect match.
*   **Females:** Have a perfect pair of *sex chromosomes*, both called *X chromosomes* (*XX*).
*   **Males:** Have a mismatched pair, consisting of one normal-sized *X chromosome* and one shorter *Y chromosome* (*XY*).

This difference in *sex chromosomes* explains how *genetic inheritance* determines *sex*.

### Questions

1.  How do Mendel's experiments show that traits may be dominant or recessive?
    > **Answer:** Mendel's monohybrid cross experiments, such as crossing a pure-breeding tall pea plant with a pure-breeding short pea plant, demonstrated dominance and recessiveness.
    > *   **F1 Generation:** All offspring in the F1 generation exhibited only one of the parental traits (e.g., all plants were tall). This showed that one trait (tallness) masked the expression of the other trait (shortness). The expressed trait is *dominant*.
    > *   **F2 Generation:** When the F1 generation was self-pollinated, the hidden trait reappeared in the F2 generation (e.g., about 25% of the plants were short). This proved that the masked trait was still present in the F1 generation but not expressed. The trait that reappears in F2 after being hidden in F1 is *recessive*.

2.  How do Mendel's experiments show that traits are inherited independently?
    > **Answer:** Mendel's dihybrid cross experiments, involving two contrasting traits (e.g., seed shape and seed color), showed independent inheritance.
    > *   When he crossed plants differing in two traits (e.g., tall with round seeds and short with wrinkled seeds), the F1 generation showed only the dominant traits (all tall with round seeds).
    > *   However, in the F2 generation, he observed not only the original parental combinations (tall-round, short-wrinkled) but also *new combinations* (tall-wrinkled, short-round).
    > *   The appearance of these new combinations in a predictable ratio (9:3:3:1 for phenotypes) indicated that the inheritance of one trait (e.g., tall/short) did not influence the inheritance of the other trait (e.g., round/wrinkled). The alleles for different traits segregate and assort independently during gamete formation.

3.  A man with blood group A marries a woman with blood group O and their daughter has blood group O. Is this information enough to tell you which of the traits - blood group A or O - is dominant? Why or why not?
    > **Answer:** Yes, this information is enough to tell you that blood group O is a *recessive trait* and blood group A is *dominant* over O.
    > *   **Reasoning:**
    >     *   Blood group O is caused by the genotype *ii* (two recessive alleles). Therefore, the daughter having blood group O means she inherited an 'i' allele from both her mother and her father.
    >     *   The mother has blood group O, so her genotype must be *ii*. She contributes an 'i' allele to her daughter.
    >     *   The father has blood group A. Since his daughter inherited an 'i' allele from him, his genotype must be *IAi* (heterozygous for A). If A were recessive, he would have to be *ii* to pass on 'i', but he has blood group A, which contradicts this.
    >     *   For the father to have blood group A while carrying an 'i' allele, the 'A' allele (*IA*) must be *dominant* over the 'i' allele (O). If 'A' were recessive, he would express blood group O if he carried 'i'.

4.  How is the sex of the child determined in human beings?
    > **Answer:** In human beings, the sex of the child is determined genetically by the *sex chromosomes* inherited from the parents.
    > *   **Females** have two X chromosomes (XX). All eggs produced by a female carry an X chromosome.
    > *   **Males** have one X and one Y chromosome (XY). Males produce two types of sperm: half carry an X chromosome and half carry a Y chromosome.
    > *   The sex of the child is determined at the moment of fertilization by the type of sperm that fertilizes the egg:
    >     *   If an X-carrying sperm fertilizes the egg (X), the resulting zygote will be XX, developing into a *female*.
    >     *   If a Y-carrying sperm fertilizes the egg (X), the resulting zygote will be XY, developing into a *male*.
    > Therefore, the father is responsible for determining the sex of the child.
