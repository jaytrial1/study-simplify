This section explains the molecular basis of trait expression, detailing the role of DNA, genes, and proteins, and clarifies how genetic material is passed from parents to offspring through chromosomes and germ cells.

# How Traits are Expressed and Inherited at the Cellular Level

## The Role of Genes and Proteins in Trait Expression

The mechanism of *heredity* fundamentally relies on *cellular DNA*, which serves as the information source for making *proteins* in the cell. A specific section of *DNA* that provides information for one *protein* is called a *gene* for that *protein*.

How do *proteins* control the characteristics or *traits* we observe? Let's consider *tallness* in plants as an example:
*   Plants produce *hormones* that trigger growth, and plant height depends on the amount of a particular plant *hormone*.
*   The amount of *hormone* produced is determined by the efficiency of the process that makes it.
*   An *enzyme* is crucial for this process.
    *   If the *enzyme* works efficiently, a lot of *hormone* is made, resulting in a *tall plant*.
    *   If the *gene* for that *enzyme* has an alteration that makes the *enzyme less efficient*, less *hormone* will be produced, leading to a *short plant*.
Thus, *genes* directly control *characteristics* or *traits*.

## Inheritance of Genetic Material

Mendel's experiments suggest that both parents contribute equally to the *DNA* of the progeny during *sexual reproduction*. If both parents influence a *trait* in the offspring, they must each contribute a copy of the same *gene*. This implies that each pea plant, and indeed any sexually reproducing organism, must have two sets of all *genes* – one inherited from each parent.

For this mechanism to function correctly, each *germ cell* (sperm or egg) must contain only *one gene set*. If progeny inherited a complete *gene set* from each parent, the independent inheritance observed in experiments like the dihybrid cross (Fig. 8.5) would not be possible, as *genes* would be linked.

This is explained by the fact that each *gene set* is not a single long thread of *DNA*, but rather separate, independent pieces called *chromosomes*.
*   Each normal body cell has two copies of each *chromosome*, one from the male parent and one from the female parent.
*   During the formation of *germ cells*, each *germ cell* receives one *chromosome* from each pair and these may be of either maternal or paternal origin, ensuring *independent assortment*.
*   When two *germ cells* (sperm and egg) combine during fertilization, they restore the normal number of *chromosomes* in the progeny, ensuring the stability of the species' *DNA*.

This precise mechanism of *inheritance* explains Mendel's experimental results and is employed by all *sexually reproducing organisms*. Asexually reproducing organisms also follow similar rules of *inheritance*, though their variations arise primarily from *DNA copying errors*.
