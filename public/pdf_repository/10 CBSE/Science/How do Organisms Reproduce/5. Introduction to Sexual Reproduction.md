This section introduces sexual reproduction, highlighting its significance in generating genetic variation and addressing the challenges associated with combining genetic material from two different individuals.

# Sexual Reproduction

We are also familiar with modes of reproduction that depend on the involvement of two individuals before a new generation can be created. For example, bulls alone cannot produce new calves, nor can hens alone produce new chicks. In such cases, both sexes, males and females, are needed to produce new generations.

## Why the Sexual Mode of Reproduction?

The creation of two new cells from one involves copying of the DNA as well as the cellular apparatus. The DNA copying mechanism, as noted earlier, cannot be absolutely accurate, and the resulting errors are a source of **variations** in populations of organisms. While every individual organism cannot be protected by variations, in a population, variations are crucial for ensuring the survival of the species. Therefore, it makes evolutionary sense for organisms to develop reproductive modes that allow more and more variation to be generated.

While DNA-copying mechanisms are not absolutely accurate, they are precise enough to make the generation of variation a fairly slow process. If the DNA copying mechanisms were to be less accurate, many of the resulting DNA copies would not be able to function with the cellular apparatus, and would die. So, how can the process of creating variants be sped up?

Each new variation is made in a DNA copy that already contains variations accumulated from previous generations. Thus, two different individuals in a population would have quite different patterns of accumulated variations. Since all of these variations are in living individuals, it is assured that they do not have any really detrimental effects. Combining variations from two or more individuals would thus create new combinations of variants. Each combination would be novel, as it would involve two different individuals. The sexual mode of reproduction incorporates such a process of combining DNA from two different individuals during reproduction.

However, this creates a major difficulty: if each new generation is to be the combination of DNA copies from two pre-existing individuals, then each new generation will end up having *twice* the amount of DNA that the previous generation had. This is likely to disrupt the control of the cellular apparatus by the DNA.

One solution that many multi-cellular organisms have found for this problem is to have special lineages of cells in specialized organs in which only half the number of chromosomes and half the amount of DNA are present, compared to the non-reproductive body cells. This is achieved by a process of cell division called **meiosis**. Thus, when these **germ-cells** (or gametes) from two individuals combine during sexual reproduction to form a new individual, it results in the reestablishment of the normal number of chromosomes and DNA content in the new generation.

If the zygote is to grow and develop into an organism with highly specialized tissues and organs, it must have sufficient stores of energy. In very simple organisms, the two germ-cells are not very different from one another, or may even be similar. But as body designs become more complex, the germ-cells also specialize. One germ-cell is large and contains food stores, while the other is smaller and typically motile. Conventionally, the motile germ-cell is called the **male gamete** (e.g., sperm), and the germ-cell containing the stored food is called the **female gamete** (e.g., egg). The need to create these two different types of gametes gives rise to differences in the male and female reproductive organs and, in some cases, differences in the bodies of the male and female organisms.
