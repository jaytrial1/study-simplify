This section introduces human reproduction, focusing on the significant physical and hormonal changes that occur during adolescence, a period known as puberty, which marks the onset of sexual maturation.

# Reproduction in Human Beings

Humans use a sexual mode of reproduction. All of us know that our bodies change as we become older. We observe continuous height increase from early age, the acquisition of teeth, and the replacement of milk teeth with permanent ones. These are changes grouped under the general process of **growth**, where the body becomes larger.

However, in early teenage years, a whole new set of changes occurs that cannot be explained simply as body enlargement. Instead, the appearance of the body changes significantly. Proportions change, new features appear, and new sensations develop.

Some of these changes are common to both boys and girls:
*   Thick hair begins to grow in new parts of the body, such as armpits and the genital area between the thighs, which can also become darker in color.
*   Thinner hair can appear on legs and arms, as well as on the face.
*   The skin frequently becomes oily, and pimples might develop.
*   Individuals begin to be conscious and aware of both their own bodies and those of others in new ways.

On the other hand, there are also changes specific to boys and girls:
*   **In girls:** Breast size begins to increase, accompanied by darkening of the skin of the nipples at the tips of the breasts. Girls also begin to menstruate around this time.
*   **In boys:** New thick hair growth appears on the face (beard and mustache), and their voices begin to crack or deepen. Furthermore, the penis occasionally begins to become enlarged and erect, either in daydreams or at night.

All these changes take place slowly, over a period of months and years. They do not happen all at the same time in one person, nor do they happen at an exact age. In some people, they happen early and quickly, while in others, they can happen slowly. Each change also does not become complete quickly. For example, thick hair on the face in boys appears as a few scattered hairs first, and only slowly does the growth become uniform. Even so, all these changes show differences between people; just as we have differently shaped noses or fingers, we also have different patterns of hair growth, or size and shape of breasts or penis. All of these changes are aspects of the **sexual maturation** of the body.

Why does the body show sexual maturation at this age? The creation of germ-cells to participate in sexual reproduction is a specialized function, and as we have seen, plants develop special cell and tissue types for this. Human beings also develop special tissues for this purpose. However, while the individual organism's body is growing to its adult size, the body's resources are mainly directed at achieving this growth. During this period, the maturation of reproductive tissue is not likely to be a major priority. Thus, as the rate of general body growth begins to slow down, reproductive tissues begin to mature. This period during adolescence is called **puberty**.

The changes during puberty are linked to the reproductive process. The sexual mode of reproduction requires germ-cells from two individuals to join together. This can happen by the external release of germ-cells (as in flowering plants) or by two individuals joining their bodies for internal transfer of germ-cells for fusion (as in many animals). If animals are to participate in mating, their state of sexual maturity must be identifiable by other individuals. Many changes during puberty, such as new hair-growth patterns, serve as signals that sexual maturation is taking place.

Furthermore, the actual transfer of germ-cells between two people requires special organs for the sexual act, such as the penis when it is capable of becoming erect. In mammals such as humans, the baby is carried in the mother's body for a long period and will be breast-fed later. The female reproductive organs and breasts need to mature to accommodate these possibilities.
