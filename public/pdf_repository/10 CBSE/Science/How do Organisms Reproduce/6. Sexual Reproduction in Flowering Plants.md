# Sexual Reproduction in Flowering Plants

The reproductive parts of angiosperms (flowering plants) are located in the flower. A typical flower consists of several parts:
*   **Sepals:** Leaf-like structures, usually green, that enclose and protect the developing bud.
*   **Petals:** Often brightly colored structures that attract pollinators.
*   **Stamens:** The male reproductive parts.
*   **Pistil:** The female reproductive part, located in the center.

Stamens and pistil are the reproductive parts of a flower which contain the germ-cells.
Flowers may be **unisexual** (e.g., papaya, watermelon) when they contain either stamens or pistil, or **bisexual** (e.g., *Hibiscus*, mustard) when they contain both stamens and pistil.

The **stamen** is the male reproductive part and produces **pollen grains**, which are yellowish in color. You may have noticed this yellowish powder sticking to your hands if you touch the stamen of a flower.

The **pistil** is present in the center of a flower and is the female reproductive part. It is made of three parts:
*   The swollen bottom part is the **ovary**.
*   The middle elongated part is the **style**.
*   The terminal part, which may be sticky, is the **stigma**.

The ovary contains **ovules**, and each ovule has an **egg cell** (female gamete). The male germ-cell produced by a pollen grain fuses with the female gamete present in the ovule. This fusion of the germ-cells, or **fertilization**, gives rise to the **zygote**, which is capable of growing into a new plant.

For fertilization to occur, the pollen needs to be transferred from the stamen to the stigma.
*   If this transfer of pollen occurs within the same flower, it is referred to as **self-pollination**.
*   On the other hand, if the pollen is transferred from one flower to another, it is known as **cross-pollination**. This transfer of pollen from one flower to another is achieved by agents like wind, water, or animals.

After the pollen lands on a suitable stigma, it must reach the female germ-cells which are located in the ovary. For this, a tube grows out of the pollen grain and travels through the style to reach the ovary.

2.  **Pollen Germination and Pollen Tube Growth:** From one of the germinating pollen grains on the stigma, a pollen tube (represented by the brown tubular structure) is shown growing downwards through the style, the stalk-like part connecting the stigma to the ovary.
3.  **Ovule and Embryo Sac:** The swollen basal part of the pistil is the ovary, which contains an ovule. The ovule is depicted with several layers of integuments surrounding an inner structure, the embryo sac.
4.  **Entry of Pollen Tube into Ovule:** The pollen tube continues its growth and is shown entering the ovule, likely through a small opening called the micropyle.
5.  **Fertilization (Double Fertilization):** Inside the embryo sac, the pollen tube releases two male gametes (represented by small, distinct dots). One male gamete is shown fusing with the egg cell (labeled 'S' in the embryo sac, indicating the egg cell or synergids guiding the pollen tube), which will develop into the zygote. The second male gamete is depicted moving towards or fusing with the central cell (containing polar nuclei, not explicitly labeled but implied by the location of the second fusion), which will form the primary endosperm nucleus, responsible for nourishing the developing embryo.
In summary, the diagram provides a clear visual representation of the post-pollination events leading to double fertilization, a characteristic process in flowering plants, where the male gametes delivered by the pollen tube fuse with the egg cell and the central cell within the embryo sac.*

After fertilization, the zygote divides several times to form an **embryo** within the ovule. The ovule develops a tough coat and is gradually converted into a **seed**. The ovary grows rapidly and ripens to form a **fruit**. Meanwhile, the petals, sepals, stamens, style, and stigma may shrivel and fall off.

The seed contains the future plant or embryo, which develops into a seedling under appropriate conditions. This process is known as **germination**.

### Key Concepts & Examples

*   **Advantages of Seed Formation:** Seeds offer several advantages for the plant:
    *   **Protection:** The tough seed coat protects the embryo from harsh environmental conditions.
    *   **Dispersal:** Seeds can be dispersed over long distances by wind, water, or animals, helping in the colonization of new areas.
    *   **Nourishment:** Seeds contain stored food (in cotyledons or endosperm) to nourish the developing embryo until it can photosynthesize.
    *   **Dormancy:** Seeds can remain dormant for long periods, allowing them to survive unfavorable conditions and germinate when conditions are suitable.
