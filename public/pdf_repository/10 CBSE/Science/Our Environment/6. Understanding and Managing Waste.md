This section explores the different types of waste we generate, classifying them as biodegradable or non-biodegradable, and discusses their environmental impact.

# Managing the Garbage We Produce

In our daily activities, we generate a significant amount of material that is thrown away. Let's explore what happens to these waste materials.

## Activity 13.5: Observing Waste Decomposition

**Prompt:** Collect waste material from your homes. This could include all the waste generated during a day, like kitchen waste (spoilt food, vegetable peels, used tea leaves, milk packets and empty cartons), waste paper, empty medicine bottles/strips/bubble packs, old and torn clothes and broken footwear. Bury this material in a pit in the school garden or if there is no space available, you can collect the material in an old bucket/ flower pot and cover with at least 15 cm of soil. Keep this material moist and observe at 15-day intervals.

**Question:** What are the materials that remain unchanged over long periods of time?
> **Answer:** Materials that would likely remain unchanged or decompose extremely slowly over long periods include: empty medicine bottles/strips/bubble packs (plastics), broken footwear (rubber/plastic components), and potentially some synthetic clothing materials.

**Question:** What are the materials which change their form and structure over time?
> **Answer:** Materials that would change their form and structure over time include: kitchen waste (spoilt food, vegetable peels, used tea leaves), waste paper, and old and torn clothes made from natural fibers (cotton, wool).

**Question:** Of these materials that are changed, which ones change the fastest?
> **Answer:** Kitchen waste (spoilt food, vegetable peels) would change the fastest, followed by used tea leaves and waste paper. These materials are rich in organic compounds that are easily broken down by microorganisms.

We've learned that enzymes in our body digest food. However, the same enzymes don't break down everything we eat because enzymes are specific in their action—specific enzymes are needed for the breakdown of a particular substance. This is why many human-made materials like plastics are not broken down by bacteria or other saprophytes. These materials may be acted upon by physical processes like heat and pressure, but under ambient environmental conditions, they persist for a long time.

### Biodegradable and Non-Biodegradable Substances

*   **Biodegradable substances:** These are substances that are broken down by biological processes, primarily by microorganisms (bacteria and fungi). Examples include kitchen waste, paper, cotton, and wood.
*   **Non-biodegradable substances:** These are substances that are not broken down by biological processes in nature. They may be inert and simply persist in the environment for a very long time, or they may harm various members of the ecosystem. Examples include plastics, glass, metals, and certain pesticides.

## Activity 13.6: Exploring Biodegradable and Non-Biodegradable Substances

**Prompt:** Use the library or internet to find out more about biodegradable and non-biodegradable substances. How long are various non-biodegradable substances expected to last in our environment?
> **Answer:** Non-biodegradable substances can last for incredibly long periods in the environment:
> *   **Plastic bags:** 10-1,000 years
> *   **Plastic bottles:** 450 years
> *   **Aluminum cans:** 80-200 years
> *   **Glass bottles:** 1 million years or more (effectively indefinitely)
> *   **Styrofoam:** Never (non-biodegradable and persists indefinitely)
> *   **Diapers:** 250-500 years
> *   **Rubber tires:** 50-80 years

**Prompt:** These days, new types of plastics which are said to be biodegradable are available. Find out more about such materials and whether they do or do not harm the environment.
> **Answer:** New types of plastics, often called "biodegradable" or "compostable" plastics, are made from plant-based materials (like corn starch, sugarcane, or cellulose) or are designed to break down more quickly than traditional plastics.
> *   **Benefits:** If genuinely compostable, they can break down into natural components (water, CO2, biomass) in specific industrial composting facilities, reducing plastic pollution. They can also reduce reliance on fossil fuels for production.
> *   **Environmental Concerns:**
>     *   **Conditions for breakdown:** Many "biodegradable" plastics only break down under very specific industrial composting conditions (high heat, specific microbes) and do not readily decompose in landfills, oceans, or home compost bins. If they end up in regular waste streams, they behave like traditional plastics.
>     *   **Contamination:** They can contaminate plastic recycling streams if mixed with traditional plastics, making recycling more difficult.
>     *   **Resource use:** Growing crops for bioplastics can require significant land, water, and fertilizers, potentially leading to deforestation or competition with food crops.
>     *   **Additives:** Some "biodegradable" plastics still contain additives or are blends with traditional plastics, which may not fully biodegrade and could leave behind microplastic residues or toxic chemicals.
> In summary, while they offer potential advantages, their environmental benefit largely depends on proper disposal infrastructure and clear consumer understanding of their specific degradation requirements.

## Questions

1.  Why are some substances biodegradable and some non-biodegradable?
    > **Answer:** Substances are biodegradable or non-biodegradable based on their chemical structure and composition, which determines whether microorganisms (like bacteria and fungi) possess the specific enzymes required to break them down.
    > *   **Biodegradable substances** (e.g., organic waste, paper) are typically natural, complex organic compounds that have evolved alongside decomposers. Microorganisms have the necessary enzymes to metabolize and break down these materials into simpler, harmless substances.
    > *   **Non-biodegradable substances** (e.g., plastics, glass, metals) are often synthetic, human-made materials with chemical bonds and structures that do not exist in nature, or they are natural materials with very stable bonds (like glass). Microorganisms lack the specific enzymes to break down these unique chemical structures, causing them to persist in the environment for very long periods.

2.  Give any two ways in which biodegradable substances would affect the environment.
    > **Answer:**
    > 1.  **Enrichment of soil/nutrient cycling:** When biodegradable substances decompose, they release nutrients back into the soil, enriching its fertility and supporting plant growth. This is a positive effect vital for ecosystem health.
    > 2.  **Generation of methane gas:** If biodegradable waste decomposes in anaerobic (oxygen-lacking) conditions, such as in landfills, it can produce methane, a potent greenhouse gas that contributes to climate change.

3.  Give any two ways in which non-biodegradable substances would affect the environment.
    > **Answer:**
    > 1.  **Accumulation and pollution:** Non-biodegradable substances persist in the environment for hundreds to millions of years, leading to land, water, and air pollution. They accumulate in landfills, oceans, and natural habitats, creating unsightly litter and occupying vast spaces.
    > 2.  **Harm to wildlife:** Animals can ingest or get entangled in non-biodegradable waste (e.g., plastic bags, fishing nets), leading to injury, starvation, suffocation, or death. Microplastics can also enter the food chain and potentially harm organisms at various trophic levels.
