# Dispersion of White Light and Spectrum

We often admire the spectacular colors of a rainbow. How does the white light from the Sun produce such a variety of colors? To understand this, let's revisit the refraction of light through a prism. The inclined refracting surfaces of a glass prism demonstrate an exciting phenomenon: the splitting of white light.

### Activity: Observing Dispersion

Let's observe this phenomenon through an activity:

1.  Take a thick sheet of cardboard and make a small hole or narrow slit in its middle.
2.  Allow sunlight to fall on the narrow slit, which will produce a narrow beam of white light.
3.  Now, take a glass prism and allow the light from the slit to fall on one of its faces.
4.  Turn the prism slowly until the light that emerges from it appears on a nearby screen.
5.  Observe what you see. You will find a beautiful band of colors.

The prism has split the incident white light into a band of colors. Note the colors that appear at the two ends of this color band. The sequence of colors you see on the screen is: **Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, and Red**. The acronym **VIBGYOR** can help you remember this sequence.

The band of colored components of a light beam is called its **spectrum**. While you might not be able to see all colors separately, each color appears distinct from the others. The splitting of light into its component colors is called **dispersion**.

### Why Does Dispersion Occur?

White light is dispersed into its seven-color components by a prism because different colors of light bend through different angles with respect to the incident ray as they pass through the prism.
*   **Red light** bends the *least*.
*   **Violet light** bends the *most*.

Thus, the rays of each color emerge along different paths, becoming distinct and forming the spectrum that we see.

### Newton's Experiment on Recombination

Isaac Newton was the first to use a glass prism to obtain the spectrum of sunlight. He attempted to split the colors of this spectrum further by using another similar prism, but he could not obtain any more colors.

He then placed a second identical prism in an *inverted position* with respect to the first prism, allowing all the colors of the spectrum to pass through the second prism. He observed that a beam of white light emerged from the other side of the second prism. This observation led Newton to the conclusion that sunlight is composed of seven colors. Any light that produces a spectrum similar to that of sunlight is often referred to as white light.
