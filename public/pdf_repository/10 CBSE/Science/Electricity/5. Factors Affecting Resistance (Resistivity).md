The resistance of a conductor is not a fixed value for all materials or dimensions. It depends on several factors.

On applying Ohm's law, we observe that the resistance of the conductor depends on three main factors:
1.  **Its length ($l$)**: Resistance is *directly proportional* to its length. ($R \propto l$)
2.  **Its area of cross-section ($A$)**: Resistance is *inversely proportional* to its area of cross-section. ($R \propto 1/A$)
3.  **The nature of its material**: Different materials have different inherent abilities to resist current.

Combining these proportionalities, we get:
$R \propto \frac{l}{A}$
or
$$
R=\rho \frac{l}{A}
$$
where $\rho$ (rho) is a constant of proportionality called the **electrical resistivity** of the material of the conductor. The SI unit of resistivity is **ohm-meter ($\Omega \mathrm{m}$)**.

Resistivity is a characteristic property of the material itself.
*   **Metals and alloys** have very low resistivity, typically in the range of $10^{-8} \Omega \mathrm{m}$ to $10^{-6} \Omega \mathrm{m}$. This makes them good conductors of electricity.
*   **Insulators** like rubber and glass have very high resistivity, of the order of $10^{12}$ to $10^{17} \Omega \mathrm{m}$.

Both the resistance and resistivity of a material vary with temperature.

Table 11.2 shows that the resistivity of an alloy is generally higher than that of its constituent metals. Alloys do not oxidize (burn) readily at high temperatures. For this reason, they are commonly used in electrical heating devices, like electric irons and toasters. Tungsten is used almost exclusively for filaments of electric bulbs due to its high melting point, while copper and aluminum are generally used for electrical transmission lines because of their low resistivity.

## Example 11.5

Resistance of a metal wire of length 1 m is $26 \Omega$ at $20^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. If the diameter of the wire is 0.3 mm, what will be the resistivity of the metal at that temperature? Using Table 11.2, predict the material of the wire.

### Solution

Given:
Resistance $R = 26 \Omega$
Length $l = 1 \mathrm{~m}$
Diameter $d = 0.3 \mathrm{~mm} = 0.3 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{~m} = 3 \times 10^{-4} \mathrm{~m}$

First, calculate the area of cross-section $A$. For a circular wire, $A = \pi r^2 = \pi (d/2)^2 = \pi d^2 / 4$.
$A = \frac{\pi \times (3 \times 10^{-4} \mathrm{~m})^2}{4} = \frac{3.14159 \times 9 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{~m}^2}{4}$
$A = 7.068 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{~m}^2$

Now, use the formula for resistivity: $R = \rho \frac{l}{A}$, which means $\rho = \frac{R A}{l}$.
$\rho = \frac{26 \Omega \times 7.068 \times 10^{-8} \mathrm{~m}^2}{1 \mathrm{~m}}$
$\rho = 183.768 \times 10^{-8} \Omega \mathrm{m}$
$\rho = 1.83768 \times 10^{-6} \Omega \mathrm{m} \approx 1.84 \times 10^{-6} \Omega \mathrm{m}$

The resistivity of the metal at $20^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ is $1.84 \times 10^{-6} \Omega \mathrm{m}$. From Table 11.2, this resistivity matches that of **Manganese**.

## Example 11.6

A wire of given material having length $l$ and area of cross-section $A$ has a resistance of $4 \Omega$. What would be the resistance of another wire of the same material having length $l/2$ and area of cross-section $2A$?

### Solution

For the first wire:
Let its resistance be $R_1$.
$R_1 = \rho \frac{l}{A} = 4 \Omega$

For the second wire:
Let its resistance be $R_2$.
Length $l_2 = l/2$
Area of cross-section $A_2 = 2A$
Since it's the *same material*, the resistivity $\rho$ is the same.
$R_2 = \rho \frac{l_2}{A_2} = \rho \frac{l/2}{2A}$
$R_2 = \rho \frac{l}{4A}$
We can rewrite this as:
$R_2 = \frac{1}{4} \left( \rho \frac{l}{A} \right)$
Since $R_1 = \rho \frac{l}{A} = 4 \Omega$:
$R_2 = \frac{1}{4} R_1$
$R_2 = \frac{1}{4} \times 4 \Omega$
$R_2 = 1 \Omega$

The resistance of the new wire is $1 \Omega$.

## Questions

1.  On what factors does the resistance of a conductor depend?
2.  Will current flow more easily through a thick wire or a thin wire of the same material, when connected to the same source? Why?
3.  Let the resistance of an electrical component remains constant while the potential difference across the two ends of the component decreases to half of its former value. What change will occur in the current through it?
4.  Why are coils of electric toasters and electric irons made of an alloy rather than a pure metal?
5.  Use the data in Table 11.2 to answer the following:
    (a) Which among iron and mercury is a better conductor?
    (b) Which material is the best conductor?

> **Answer:**
> 1.  The resistance of a conductor depends on the following factors:
>     *   **Length ($l$)**: Resistance is directly proportional to its length.
>     *   **Area of cross-section ($A$)**: Resistance is inversely proportional to its area of cross-section.
>     *   **Nature of the material**: Different materials have different inherent resistivities.
>     *   **Temperature**: Resistance generally increases with temperature for metallic conductors.
> 2.  Current will flow more easily through a **thick wire** of the same material when connected to the same source. This is because resistance is inversely proportional to the area of cross-section ($R \propto 1/A$). A thicker wire has a larger cross-sectional area, which means it offers less resistance to the flow of electrons, allowing more current to pass through easily (according to Ohm's Law, $I = V/R$).
> 3.  According to Ohm's Law, $I = V/R$. If the resistance ($R$) remains constant and the potential difference ($V$) decreases to half of its former value (i.e., $V' = V/2$), then the new current ($I'$) will be:
>     $I' = V'/R = (V/2)/R = \frac{1}{2} (V/R) = \frac{1}{2} I$
>     So, the current through the component will also **decrease to half** of its former value.
> 4.  Coils of electric toasters and electric irons are made of an alloy (like nichrome) rather than a pure metal for two main reasons:
>     *   **Higher Resistivity**: Alloys generally have a much higher resistivity than their constituent pure metals (as seen in Table 11.2). This means they offer greater resistance, leading to more heat generation ($H=I^2Rt$) for a given current, which is desirable for heating elements.
>     *   **Resistance to Oxidation at High Temperatures**: Alloys do not oxidize (burn) readily at high temperatures. This prevents them from melting or deteriorating quickly when heated intensely, ensuring a longer lifespan for the appliance.
> 5.  Using the data in Table 11.2:
>     (a) Resistivity of Iron = $10.0 \times 10^{-8} \Omega \mathrm{m}$
>         Resistivity of Mercury = $94.0 \times 10^{-8} \Omega \mathrm{m}$
>         A better conductor has lower resistivity. Since Iron has a lower resistivity than Mercury, **Iron** is a better conductor.
>     (b) Looking at the "Conductors" section in Table 11.2, **Silver** has the lowest resistivity ($1.60 \times 10^{-8} \Omega \mathrm{m}$). Therefore, silver is the best conductor among the listed materials.
