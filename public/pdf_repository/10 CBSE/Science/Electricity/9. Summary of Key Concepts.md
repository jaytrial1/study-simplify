This section summarizes the main concepts learned in this chapter about electricity.

# What you have learnt

*   A stream of electrons moving through a conductor constitutes an **electric current**. Conventionally, the direction of current is taken opposite to the direction of flow of electrons.
*   The SI unit of electric current is **ampere (A)**.
*   To set the electrons in motion in an electric circuit, a **cell or a battery** is used, which generates a **potential difference** across its terminals. The potential difference is measured in **volts (V)**.
*   **Resistance** is a property of a conductor that resists the flow of electrons. It controls the magnitude of the current. The SI unit of resistance is **ohm ($\Omega$)**.
*   **Ohm's Law** states that the potential difference across the ends of a resistor is directly proportional to the current through it, provided its temperature remains the same ($V=IR$).
*   The resistance of a conductor depends directly on its length, inversely on its area of cross-section, and also on the material of the conductor (quantified by **resistivity, $\rho$**). The formula is $R=\rho \frac{l}{A}$.
*   The **equivalent resistance of several resistors in series** is equal to the sum of their individual resistances: $R_{s} = R_{1} + R_{2} + R_{3} + \ldots$.
*   A set of resistors connected in **parallel** has an equivalent resistance $R_{p}$ given by the reciprocal sum of their individual reciprocals: $\frac{1}{R_{p}}=\frac{1}{R_{1}}+\frac{1}{R_{2}}+\frac{1}{R_{3}}+\ldots$.
*   The **electrical energy dissipated as heat** in a resistor is given by **Joule's Law of Heating**: $H = VIt = I^2Rt = V^2t/R$.
*   **Electric power (P)** is the rate at which electrical energy is dissipated or consumed. The unit of power is **watt (W)**. One watt of power is consumed when 1 A of current flows at a potential difference of 1 V ($P=VI$).
*   The commercial unit of electrical energy is **kilowatt-hour (kWh)**, commonly known as a 'unit'. $1 \mathrm{~kW h} = 3,600,000 \mathrm{~J} = 3.6 \times 10^{6} \mathrm{~J}$.

> 4.  **Solution:**
>     Let the resistance of each wire be $R$.
>     **Case 1: Series Connection**
>     Equivalent resistance $R_s = R + R = 2R$.
>     Heat produced $H_s = V^2t / R_s = V^2t / (2R)$.
>     **Case 2: Parallel Connection**
>     Equivalent resistance $R_p$: $\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{R} + \frac{1}{R} = \frac{2}{R} \implies R_p = R/2$.
>     Heat produced $H_p = V^2t / R_p = V^2t / (R/2) = 2V^2t / R$.
>     **Ratio of heat produced in series and parallel combinations ($H_s : H_p$):**
>     $H_s / H_p = (V^2t / (2R)) / (2V^2t / R)$
>     $H_s / H_p = (1 / (2R)) \times (R / 2) = 1/4$.
>     The ratio is $1:4$.
>     **Answer:** (c) 1:4

> 6.  **Solution:**
>     Given: Diameter $d = 0.5 \mathrm{~mm} = 0.5 \times 10^{-3} \mathrm{~m} = 5 \times 10^{-4} \mathrm{~m}$.
>     Resistivity $\rho = 1.6 \times 10^{-8} \Omega \mathrm{~m}$.
>     Desired resistance $R = 10 \Omega$.
>
>     **Part 1: Length of the wire ($l$)**
>     Area of cross-section $A = \pi (d/2)^2 = \pi (5 \times 10^{-4} / 2)^2 = \pi (2.5 \times 10^{-4})^2$
>     $A = 3.14159 \times (6.25 \times 10^{-8}) \mathrm{~m}^2 \approx 1.963 \times 10^{-7} \mathrm{~m}^2$.
>
>     Using $R = \rho l / A$, we get $l = RA / \rho$.
>     $l = (10 \Omega \times 1.963 \times 10^{-7} \mathrm{~m}^2) / (1.6 \times 10^{-8} \Omega \mathrm{~m})$
>     $l = (19.63 \times 10^{-7}) / (1.6 \times 10^{-8}) = (19.63 / 1.6) \times 10^{(-7 - (-8))} = 12.26875 \times 10^1 = 122.6875 \mathrm{~m}$.
>     The length of the wire is approximately **122.7 m**.
>
>     **Part 2: Change in resistance if diameter is doubled**
>     If the diameter is doubled, the new diameter $d' = 2d$.
>     The new area of cross-section $A' = \pi (d'/2)^2 = \pi (2d/2)^2 = \pi d^2 = 4 \times (\pi (d/2)^2) = 4A$.
>     So, the area of cross-section becomes four times the original area.
>     The new resistance $R'$ will be:
>     $R' = \rho l / A' = \rho l / (4A) = (1/4) (\rho l / A) = (1/4) R$.
>     Since the original resistance $R = 10 \Omega$, the new resistance $R' = (1/4) \times 10 \Omega = 2.5 \Omega$.
>     The resistance will **decrease to one-fourth** of its original value (from $10 \Omega$ to $2.5 \Omega$).
>

>     Voltage $V = 220 \mathrm{~V}$
>     Resistance of each resistor $R_{single} = 176 \Omega$.
>     Let $n$ be the number of $176 \Omega$ resistors connected in parallel.

>     Given three resistors, each of resistance $6 \Omega$.
>     (i) **To get a total resistance of $9 \Omega$**:
>         *   Connect two $6 \Omega$ resistors in parallel.
>             $\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{6 \Omega} + \frac{1}{6 \Omega} = \frac{2}{6 \Omega} = \frac{1}{3 \Omega} \implies R_p = 3 \Omega$.

>     (ii) **To get a total resistance of $4 \Omega$**:

>             $\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{6 \Omega} + \frac{1}{6 \Omega} + \frac{1}{6 \Omega} = \frac{3}{6 \Omega} = \frac{1}{2 \Omega} \implies R_p = 2 \Omega$.

>             $\frac{1}{R_{total}} = \frac{1}{12 \Omega} + \frac{1}{6 \Omega} = \frac{1}{12 \Omega} + \frac{2}{12 \Omega} = \frac{3}{12 \Omega} = \frac{1}{4 \Omega}$.
>             $R_{total} = 4 \Omega$.
>         *   **Configuration**: Two $6 \Omega$ resistors in series, connected in parallel with the third $6 \Omega$ resistor.

>         $P_{2\Omega, parallel} = (4 \mathrm{~V})^2 / 2 \Omega = 16 / 2 = 8 \mathrm{~W}$.

> 18. **Solution:**
>     (a) **Why is tungsten used almost exclusively for the filament of electric lamps?**
>         Tungsten is used for electric lamp filaments because it has an extremely **high melting point** (approx. $3380^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$) and maintains its mechanical strength even at very high temperatures. This allows it to heat up to incandescence (white hot) and emit light without melting or breaking, providing a longer lifespan for the bulb.
>
>     (b) **Why are the conductors of electric heating devices, such as bread-toasters and electric irons, made of an alloy rather than a pure metal?**
>         Alloys (like nichrome) are used for heating elements for two main reasons:
>         1.  **High Resistivity**: Alloys have significantly higher electrical resistivity compared to pure metals. This means they offer greater resistance to current flow, leading to more heat generation ($H=I^2Rt$) for a given current and voltage, which is essential for heating applications.
>         2.  **High Melting Point and Oxidation Resistance**: Alloys generally have higher melting points and do not oxidize (burn) readily at high temperatures. This prevents them from quickly melting or deteriorating when heated intensely, ensuring the durability and safety of the appliance.
>
>     (c) **Why is the series arrangement not used for domestic circuits?**
>         The series arrangement is not used for domestic circuits due to several disadvantages:
>         1.  **Voltage Division**: In a series circuit, the voltage divides across each appliance. Appliances would not receive the full 220 V supply, leading to dim lighting or inefficient operation.
>         2.  **Single Point of Failure**: If one appliance in a series circuit fails (e.g., a bulb fuses or a device breaks), the entire circuit is broken, and all other appliances in that series stop working.
>         3.  **Varying Current**: All appliances in a series circuit would draw the same current. However, different appliances (e.g., a lamp and a refrigerator) require different amounts of current for proper operation.
>
>     (d) **How does the resistance of a wire vary with its area of cross-section?**
>         The resistance of a wire is **inversely proportional** to its area of cross-section ($R \propto 1/A$). This means that a thicker wire (larger cross-sectional area) will have lower resistance, while a thinner wire (smaller cross-sectional area) will have higher resistance, assuming the material and length are the same.
>
>     (e) **Why are copper and aluminum wires usually employed for electricity transmission?**
>         Copper and aluminum wires are usually employed for electricity transmission because:
>         1.  **Low Resistivity**: They are excellent conductors of electricity, meaning they have very low electrical resistivity (as shown in Table 11.2). This minimizes energy loss as heat ($H=I^2Rt$) during transmission, making the process efficient.
>         2.  **Ductility and Malleability**: They are highly ductile (can be drawn into thin wires) and malleable (can be hammered into sheets), making them easy to manufacture and install.
>         3.  **Cost-Effectiveness**: While silver is a better conductor, copper and aluminum are far more abundant and economical, making them practical choices for large-scale transmission networks.
