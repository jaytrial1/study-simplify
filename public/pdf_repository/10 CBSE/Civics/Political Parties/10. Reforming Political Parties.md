This section discusses the efforts and suggestions made to reform political parties in India to address the challenges they face.

# Reforming Political Parties

To address the challenges, political parties need reforms. The question is whether they are willing, what prevents reform, and if they can be forced to change. Since party leaders make decisions in a democracy, and people can only replace them with other party leaders, forcing reform is complex if all leaders resist.

Here are some recent efforts and suggestions for reforming parties and leaders in India:

**Recent Efforts:**

*   **Anti-Defection Law:** The Constitution was amended to prevent elected MLAs and MPs from changing parties (**defection**) for ministerial positions or cash rewards. If an MLA or MP changes parties, they lose their legislative seat. This has reduced defection but also makes dissent harder, as legislators must follow party leadership decisions.

    **Glossary**
    *   **Defection:** Changing party allegiance from the party on which a person was elected to a legislative body, to a different party.

*   **Supreme Court Order on Money and Criminals:** The Supreme Court mandated that candidates contesting elections file an **AFFIDAVIT** providing details of their property and pending criminal cases. This increases public information but lacks a system to verify the truthfulness of the information and hasn't clearly reduced the influence of the rich and criminals yet.

*   **Election Commission Order on Internal Functioning:** The Election Commission required parties to hold organizational elections and file income tax returns. Parties comply, but sometimes it's a mere formality, and it's unclear if this has led to greater internal democracy.

**Suggestions for Reform:**

*   **Legal Regulation of Internal Affairs:** A law could mandate parties maintain member registers, follow their constitution, have an independent authority for disputes, and hold open elections for top posts.
*   **Mandatory Quotas for Women:** A law could require parties to give a minimum number of tickets (e.g., one-third) to women candidates and allocate quotas for women in decision-making bodies.
*   **State Funding of Elections:** The government could provide financial support to parties for election expenses, either in kind (petrol, paper, telephone) or cash based on past election performance.

These suggestions have not been accepted by parties yet. While they could bring improvement, legal solutions to political problems must be approached carefully. Excessive regulation might be counter-productive, leading parties to find ways around the law. Also, parties are unlikely to pass laws they dislike.

Two other ways to reform parties involve public action:

1.  **Public Pressure:** Citizens, pressure groups, movements, and the media can exert pressure through petitions, publicity, and agitations. If parties fear losing public support for resisting reforms, they might become more serious about them.
2.  **Public Participation:** Parties can improve if those seeking reform join them. The quality of democracy relies on public participation. Criticizing from the outside is insufficient; reforming politics requires citizens to engage in it. The solution to bad politics can be found in more and better politics.
