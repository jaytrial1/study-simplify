# Majoritarianism in Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka gained independence in 1948. The Sinhala community leaders, leveraging their majority, sought dominance in government. They adopted **majoritarian** measures to establish Sinhala supremacy.

*   In 1956, an Act made Sinhala the sole official language, ignoring Tamil.
*   Government policies favored Sinhala applicants for university positions and government jobs.
*   A new constitution stipulated that the state must protect and foster Buddhism.

These measures increased the feeling of alienation among Sri Lankan Tamils. They felt ignored by major Sinhala parties, believing the constitution and policies denied them equal political rights, discriminated in jobs, and disregarded their interests. Relations between the communities strained.

> **Answer:** While a majority has the right to rule in a democracy, majoritarianism (imposing the majority's will without considering the minority) is problematic. The chapter shows that it leads to the minority feeling alienated, discriminated against, and denied rights. This can cause social conflict, violence, political instability, and ultimately undermine the unity of the nation, harming both the minority and the majority in the long run, as seen in Sri Lanka's civil war. Ruling means governing for *all* citizens, not just the majority group.

**Civil war:** A violent conflict between opposing groups within a country that becomes so intense that it appears like a war.

Sri Lankan Tamils formed parties and struggled for recognition of Tamil as an official language, regional autonomy, and equal opportunity. Their demands for autonomy were repeatedly denied. By the 1980s, some organizations demanded an independent Tamil Eelam (state) in the north and east. Distrust escalated into widespread conflict and a **civil war**, resulting in thousands of deaths, refugees, and loss of livelihoods. The civil war severely impacted the country's social, cultural, and economic life and ended in 2009.
