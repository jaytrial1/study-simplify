This section explains how Fundamental Rights are secured and enforced through the Right to Constitutional Remedies, highlighting the role of the judiciary, Public Interest Litigation (PIL), and the National Human Rights Commission (NHRC).

# Securing Fundamental Rights: Constitutional Remedies

Rights are effective only if they can be enforced. The Fundamental Rights in the Indian Constitution are enforceable. Citizens have the right to seek their enforcement, known as the **Right to Constitutional Remedies**. This right is itself a Fundamental Right and makes the others effective.

If our rights are violated by fellow citizens, private bodies, or the government, we can seek remedy through the courts. If a Fundamental Right is violated, we can directly approach the **Supreme Court** or the **High Court** of a state. Dr. Ambedkar called the Right to Constitutional Remedies the **'heart and soul'** of our Constitution.

Fundamental Rights are guaranteed against actions by the Legislature, Executive, and governmental authorities. No law or action can violate Fundamental Rights. If any law or executive action infringes upon Fundamental Rights, it is invalid and can be challenged in court. This applies to laws by central and state governments, policies and actions of the government, and governmental organizations. Courts can also enforce Fundamental Rights against private individuals and bodies. The Supreme Court and High Courts can issue **directions, orders, or writs** to enforce these rights and can award compensation to victims and punishment to violators. As discussed in Chapter Five, the Indian judiciary is independent and powerful, capable of protecting citizens' rights.

### Public Interest Litigation (PIL)

While an aggrieved person can go to court for remedy, the concept of **Public Interest Litigation (PIL)** allows *any* citizen or group to approach the Supreme Court or a High Court for the protection of public interest against a law or government action that violates Fundamental Rights, even if they are not the direct victim. A simple letter or postcard to the judges can initiate a PIL if the court finds it in public interest.

## National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)

The news collage shows references to the **National Human Rights Commission (NHRC)**, reflecting growing awareness and efforts to protect human rights. The NHRC is an independent commission established by law in 1993. Like the judiciary, it is independent of the government. Appointed by the President, it includes retired judges, officers, and eminent citizens.

The NHRC focuses on helping victims secure their human rights, which include all rights granted by the Constitution as well as rights mentioned in UN international treaties signed by India.

The NHRC cannot punish the guilty (that's the courts' role) but conducts independent inquiries into human rights violations, abetment, or negligence by government officials. It promotes human rights generally. The Commission presents findings and recommendations to the government or intervenes in court on behalf of victims. It has wide powers, including summoning witnesses, questioning officials, demanding documents, visiting prisons, and sending inquiry teams.

Any citizen can complain to the NHRC about human rights violations by writing to: National Human Rights Commission, Faridkot House, Copernicus Marg, New Delhi 110001. There is no fee or formal procedure. State Human Rights Commissions also exist in many states.
