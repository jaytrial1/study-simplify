This section explores the fundamental concept of rights, defining what they are and explaining why they are essential for the functioning and sustenance of a democratic system.

# Understanding Rights in a Democracy

Consider the victims in the cases discussed: prisoners in Guantanamo Bay, women in Saudi Arabia, Albanians in Kosovo. If you were in their place, what would you want? What would you do to prevent such occurrences?

You would likely desire a system ensuring security, dignity, and fairness for everyone. For instance, you might want assurance against arbitrary arrest and the right to a fair defense if arrested. While not everything can be guaranteed, you would insist on a minimum level of protection for all, regardless of power, wealth, or status. This is the core idea behind rights.

## What are rights?

**Rights** are claims made by a person on other individuals, society, and the government. Everyone desires a life of happiness, without fear or degrading treatment. This requires others to act without causing harm or hurt. Similarly, our own actions should not harm others. A right is thus a claim that is **equally possible for others**. You cannot claim a right that harms or hurts others, like a right to break your neighbour's window while playing. Claims must be **reasonable** and applicable to everyone equally. Therefore, a right implies an **obligation to respect the rights of others**.

A claim does not automatically become a right. It needs to be **recognized by society**. Rights gain meaning only within a social context. Societies establish rules defining right and wrong, and what society recognizes as rightful forms the basis of rights. This explains why the concept of rights evolves across time and societies. Two centuries ago, the idea of women having the right to vote seemed strange; today, denying them the vote in Saudi Arabia appears unusual.

When socially recognized claims are codified into **law**, they gain real force. Otherwise, they remain merely natural or moral rights. The Guantanamo Bay prisoners had a moral right not to be tortured, but no legal recourse to enforce it. When law sanctions claims, they become **enforceable**. We can demand their application. Violating or infringing upon these rights means fellow citizens or the government fail to respect them. In such cases, citizens can approach courts for protection. Thus, a claim qualifies as a right if it is a **reasonable claim**, **recognized by society**, and **sanctioned by law**.

## Why do we need rights in a democracy?

Rights are fundamental to the **sustenance of democracy**. Every citizen in a democracy needs the right to vote and to be elected. For democratic elections to function, citizens must have the right to express opinions, form political parties, and participate in political activities.

Rights play a crucial role in democracy by **protecting minorities from majority oppression**. They ensure the majority cannot act arbitrarily. Rights serve as **guarantees** to be used when things go wrong, especially when the majority might try to infringe upon the rights of minorities. The government is expected to protect citizens' rights in such situations. However, sometimes elected governments themselves may fail to protect or even attack citizens' rights. This is why certain rights must be placed **higher than the government**, making them immune to violation by the state. In most democracies, basic citizen rights are written into the constitution.
