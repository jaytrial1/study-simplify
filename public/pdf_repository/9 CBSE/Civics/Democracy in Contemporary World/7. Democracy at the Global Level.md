This section explores the idea of democracy in international relations, discussing the role of international organizations and the challenges of applying democratic principles globally.

# Democracy at the Global Level?

After discussing the expansion of democracy within countries, the question arises: does an increase in democratic countries lead to democratic relations *among* countries? While there is no single **World Government**, many international institutions perform functions that affect multiple countries. These organizations make rules that limit what governments can do, even if they cannot command citizens directly like a national government.

Consider:
*   Who governs international seas or addresses global environmental issues? The **United Nations (UN)** has developed conventions binding on most countries for cooperation in international law, security, economic development, and social equity.
*   What happens when one country attacks another unjustly? The **UN Security Council** is responsible for maintaining peace and security and can authorize action.
*   Who lends money to governments in need? The **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** and the **World Bank** provide loans, often requiring changes in economic policy.

## International Institutions and Democratic Principles

These international institutions perform some functions of a global authority. However, we must ask how democratic they are. A key test is whether each country has a free and equal say in decisions affecting them.

The **UN General Assembly** includes 193 member states, each with one vote. This appears democratic, like a global parliament for discussion. However, the General Assembly cannot make binding decisions on conflicts. Crucial decisions are made by the 15-member **Security Council**, which includes five permanent members (US, Russia, UK, France, China) and ten non-permanent members elected for two-year terms. The five permanent members hold real power, largely funding the UN and possessing **veto power**, meaning any one of them can block a decision. This structure has led to demands for the UN to become more democratic.

The **International Monetary Fund (IMF)**, a major global lender, has 188 member states, but they do not have equal voting rights. A country's vote is weighted by its financial contribution. Over 52% of voting power is held by just ten countries, leaving the vast majority with very little say. The **World Bank** has a similar voting system, and its President has traditionally been a US citizen nominated by the US government.

## Examining Democracy in Global Bodies

Most global institutions, like the UN Security Council or the IMF/World Bank, fail to meet the simple test of democracy applied to national governments (equal say, representation). Imagine a country where a few individuals have permanent power to veto parliament decisions, or where a small percentage of representatives hold a majority of votes – this would not be considered democratic.

Are these global institutions becoming more democratic? Evidence is not encouraging. While nations are becoming more democratic, international organizations seem less so. The end of the Cold War left the US as the sole superpower, increasing American influence on international organizations.

Despite this, there is an urge towards global democracy, driven by people-to-people connections and global civil society movements organizing against war and domination by a few powerful entities. Like democracy within nations, the push for democracy among nations often originates from people's struggles.

Here are some suggestions to strengthen world democracy. Do you support these changes? Are these changes likely to happen? Give your reasons for each of these.

- More nations should become permanent members of the Security Council.
> **Answer:** Supporting this change would be based on making the Security Council more representative of the global community, reducing the dominance of the current five powers, and reflecting shifts in global influence. It is likely to happen only with great difficulty because the current permanent members have veto power and are unlikely to agree to dilute their own power by adding more permanent members who could also have veto power.
- UN General Assembly should become like a world parliament with representatives from each country in proportion to the population of the country. These representatives should elect a world government.
> **Answer:** Supporting this change would be based on the principle of proportional representation, giving larger populations a greater voice, similar to national parliaments. It would be highly unlikely to happen because it would require nations to surrender significant sovereignty to a global body and would face strong opposition from smaller nations who fear being overwhelmed by the populations of larger countries.
- Individual countries should not have armies. The UN should maintain task forces to bring about peace in case of conflict between nations.
> **Answer:** Supporting this change would be based on the idea of preventing wars between nations by disarming individual states and centralizing military force under a neutral global body. It is extremely unlikely to happen because national sovereignty includes the right to self-defence, and countries are unwilling to rely solely on an external body for their security.
- A UN President should be elected directly by all the people of the world.
> **Answer:** Supporting this change would be based on applying the principle of direct popular election to a global leader, giving every individual citizen a voice in choosing the head of a potential global executive. It is highly unlikely to happen because it would require a level of global unity and administrative capacity far beyond what currently exists, and nations would be reluctant to see their citizens participate in electing a leader outside their national framework.
