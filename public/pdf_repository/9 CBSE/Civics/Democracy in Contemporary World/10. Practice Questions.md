5 What are the difficulties people face in a non-democratic country? Give answers drawing from the examples given in this chapter.
> **Answer:** Based on the examples of Chile under Pinochet, Poland under communist rule, and Myanmar under military rule, people in non-democratic countries face difficulties such as:
> *   Lack of freedom to choose or change their rulers.
> *   Suppression of basic freedoms like expression, assembly, and protest (censorship, illegal strikes).
> *   Imprisonment and torture of political opponents and activists (political prisoners).
> *   Extrajudicial killings and 'missing' persons.
> *   No accountability for rulers; citizens cannot question the government.
> *   Denial of basic rights and freedoms enjoyed in democracies.
> *   Forced displacement due to coercive policies.

6 Which freedoms are usually taken away when a democracy is overthrown by the military?
> **Answer:** When a democracy is overthrown by the military, freedoms usually taken away include:
> *   Freedom of speech and expression (censorship).
> *   Freedom of the press.
> *   Freedom of assembly and association (right to form parties, unions, or hold protests).
> *   The right to vote and elect leaders.
> *   Protection from arbitrary arrest and detention (habeas corpus).
> *   The right to a fair trial.

7 Which of the following positions can contribute to democracy at the global level? Give reasons for your answer in each case.
a My country gives more money to international institutions. Therefore, I want to be treated with more respect and exercise more power.
> **Answer:** This position does not contribute to democracy at the global level. Democracy is based on equality, not on wealth or power. Treating countries unequally based on financial contribution contradicts the democratic principle of equal say.
b My country may be small or poor. But my voice must be heard with equal respect, because these decisions will affect my country.
> **Answer:** This position contributes to democracy at the global level. It aligns with the democratic principle of equality among entities (in this case, countries) regardless of their size or wealth, emphasizing that decisions affecting everyone should involve everyone having an equal voice.
c Wealthy nations will have a greater say in international affairs. They cannot let their interests suffer just because they are outnumbered by poor nations.
> **Answer:** This position does not contribute to democracy at the global level. It prioritizes the interests and power of wealthy nations over the principle of equal representation and decision-making based on collective will, which is fundamental to democracy.
d Big countries like India must have a greater say in international organisations.
> **Answer:** This position is complex. While it might seem reasonable to give larger countries more say (perhaps based on population, as suggested in one proposal), pure democracy among states often implies equal sovereignty and equal voice, regardless of size or population. Giving disproportionate power based on size could be seen as contradicting the principle of equality among member states, thus not fully contributing to a democratic global order based on equal sovereignty. However, proponents might argue it makes the body more representative of the world's population if it were a "world parliament."

8 Here are three opinions heard in a television debate on the struggle for democracy in Nepal. Which of these do you agree with and why?
Guest 1: India is a democracy. Therefore, the Indian government must support the people of Nepal who are struggling against monarchy and for democracy.
> **Answer:** Agreeing with Guest 1 aligns with the idea that democratic nations have a moral obligation to support democratic movements abroad, based on shared values and the belief that the spread of democracy is beneficial.
Guest 2: That is a dangerous argument. We would be in the same position as the US was in Iraq. Remember, no outside force can promote democracy.
> **Answer:** Agreeing with Guest 2 reflects caution about external intervention, citing the negative consequences seen in cases like Iraq. It emphasizes that genuine democracy must arise from internal struggle and popular will, rather than being imposed from outside.
Guest 3: But why should we bother about the internal affairs of another country? We should be worried about our business interests there, not about democracy.
> **Answer:** Disagreeing with Guest 3 prioritizes democratic values and human rights over purely economic interests. While business interests are important, a policy focused solely on them might ignore the suffering of people under non-democratic rule and could be seen as morally questionable.

When someone demanded freedom for the media, the elders thought that too much criticism of the ruler would not help them improve their living standards. "After all, the king is so kind and interested in the welfare of all the subjects. Why create problems for him. Don't we all want to be happy?"

After reading the above passage, Chaman, Champa and Chandru made the following observations:
Chaman: Happyland is a democratic country because people were able to throw out the foreign rulers and bring back the king.
> **Answer:** Chaman's statement is incorrect. While overthrowing a foreign ruler can be a step towards self-determination, bringing back a king who is not elected and cannot be criticized does not establish democracy. Democracy requires the power to choose and change rulers and basic freedoms.
Champa: Happyland is not a democratic country because people cannot criticise the ruler. The king may be nice and may provide economic prosperity, but a king cannot give a democratic rule.
> **Answer:** Champa's statement is correct. The inability to criticize the ruler and the hereditary nature of kingship (bringing back the old royal family) are fundamentally non-democratic features. Democracy is defined by the process of choosing rulers and guaranteeing freedoms, not by the ruler's kindness or economic performance.
Chandru: What people need is happiness. So they are willing to allow their new ruler to take decisions for them. If people are happy it must be a democracy.
> **Answer:** Chandru's statement is incorrect. Democracy is a form of government based on specific principles (elected rulers, freedoms, accountability), not simply on whether people are happy or economically prosperous under a ruler. A benevolent dictatorship might make people happy in the short term, but it is still not a democracy because people lack the fundamental rights and power inherent in democratic rule.
