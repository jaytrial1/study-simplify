# Stories of Democratic Change: Poland

Let us turn to another event, this time from Poland in 1980. At that time, Poland was ruled by the **Polish United Workers' Party**, one of many communist parties governing countries in East Europe. In these countries, other political parties were not allowed, and people could not freely choose their leaders. Dissenters were often imprisoned. The Polish government was supported and controlled by the Soviet Union (USSR).

## The Rise of Solidarity

On 14 August 1980, workers at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdansk went on strike. The shipyard was state-owned, as were most factories and large properties in Poland. The strike began with a demand to reinstate a unjustly dismissed woman crane operator. This strike was illegal because independent trade unions were not permitted. Lech Walesa, a former electrician dismissed in 1976 for demanding higher pay, joined the strikers and quickly emerged as their leader.

The strike spread across the city, and workers raised broader demands, including the right to form **independent trade unions**, the release of political prisoners, and an end to censorship on the press. The movement gained such popularity that the government was forced to negotiate. Walesa and the workers signed a 21-point agreement recognizing the right to form independent trade unions and the right to strike.

## Transition to Democracy in Poland

Following the Gdansk agreement, a new trade union called **Solidarity** (Solidarnosc in Polish) was formed, the first independent union in a communist state. Within a year, Solidarity had about one crore members across Poland. Revelations of government corruption and mismanagement further weakened the rulers. General Jaruzelski's government grew anxious and imposed **martial law** in December 1981, imprisoning thousands of Solidarity members and suppressing freedoms of organization, protest, and expression.

Another wave of strikes organized by Solidarity began in 1988. This time, the government was weaker, support from the Soviet Union was uncertain, and the economy was in decline. Negotiations with Walesa in April 1989 led to an agreement for free elections. Solidarity contested all 100 seats in the Senate and won 99. In October 1990, Poland held its first presidential election with multiple parties, and Lech Walesa was elected President.
