# Historical Phases of Democracy's Expansion

## Early Developments and Suffrage

The story of modern democracy began centuries ago. The **French Revolution of 1789** inspired struggles for democracy across Europe, although democracy in France itself was unstable for a long time. In Britain, progress towards democracy was slow, gradually reducing the power of monarchy and feudal lords and expanding voting rights. The British colonies in North America formed the **United States of America** after declaring independence in 1776, adopting a democratic constitution in 1787, though voting rights were initially limited.

In the nineteenth century, democratic struggles focused on political equality, freedom, and justice, with a major demand being the right for every adult citizen to vote. Initially, voting rights were often restricted by property ownership or gender. In the US, Black people were denied voting rights until 1965. The movement for **universal adult franchise** or **universal suffrage** sought voting rights for all adults, regardless of gender, wealth, or race. By 1900, New Zealand was the only country with universal adult suffrage. However, other countries marked as 'democracies' by then had governments elected by a significant number of people (mostly men) and some political freedoms. Early democracies were established in Europe, North America, and Latin America.

Why were women given voting rights much later than men in most countries? Why did this not happen in India?
> **Answer:** Historically, women were often excluded from voting rights due to patriarchal social structures and beliefs that women were not full citizens or capable of political participation. Struggles by women's suffrage movements over many decades led to the gradual extension of voting rights. In India, when the constitution was adopted after independence in 1950, it granted universal adult suffrage to all citizens, including women, from the very beginning, based on the principles of equality established during the nationalist movement.

## Impact of Decolonization

For a long time, most countries in Asia and Africa were **colonies** under European control. People in these colonies struggled for independence, not only to remove colonial masters but also to choose their own leaders. Many countries became democracies immediately after World War II ended in 1945. India gained independence in 1947 and adopted democracy, which it has maintained. However, many former colonies had a less stable experience.

The case of Ghana, a country in western Africa, illustrates this. Formerly the British colony Gold Coast, Ghana became independent in 1957, inspiring other African countries. Kwame Nkrumah, a leader in the independence struggle, became Ghana's first prime minister and then president. Though initially a democrat, he had himself elected president for life and was overthrown by the military in 1966. Like Ghana, many countries that became democracies after independence could not sustain democratic rule for long.

## Recent Wave of Democratization (Post-1980)

The next significant wave of democratization occurred after 1980, with democracy being revived in several Latin American countries. The **disintegration of the Soviet Union** accelerated this trend. Many communist countries in Eastern Europe, previously controlled by the Soviet Union, became free during 1989-90 and chose democracy. The Soviet Union itself broke up in 1991 into 15 independent republics, most of which also became democracies. This period brought major changes to the world's political map.

Changes also occurred in India's neighbourhood. Pakistan and Bangladesh transitioned from army rule to democracy in the 1990s, though these changes were not always permanent. In Nepal, the king initially became a constitutional monarch guided by elected leaders, but the king later dismissed the government and revoked political freedoms.

Despite setbacks, the overall trend since 1980 has been towards more countries adopting democracy. By 2005, around 140 countries held multi-party elections, a record number. Over 80 previously non-democratic countries made significant progress towards democracy since 1980. However, many countries still restrict freedom of expression and the ability of people to elect their leaders and make major decisions about their lives.

One such country is Myanmar (formerly Burma). It became a democracy after independence in 1948, but military rule began with a coup in 1962. Although elections were held in 1990, the military rulers refused to recognize the results, placing elected leaders, including **Aung San Suu Kyi**, under house arrest. The regime suppressed dissent severely, imprisoning activists and censoring views critical of the government. Due to coercive policies, many people have been displaced within Myanmar or have become refugees.

Despite house arrest, Suu Kyi campaigned for democracy, gaining international recognition and the Nobel Peace Prize. However, the struggle for democratic government in Myanmar continues.
