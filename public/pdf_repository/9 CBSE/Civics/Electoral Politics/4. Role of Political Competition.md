# Role of Political Competition

Elections are fundamentally about political competition, primarily among political parties and, at the constituency level, among candidates. Without competition, elections would be meaningless.

## Is it good to have political competition?

Electoral competition has drawbacks. It can cause disunity and 'factionalism' locally, leading to complaints about 'party-politics'. Parties and candidates often criticize each other and may use unfair tactics. Some argue that the pressure to win prevents sensible long-term policies and discourages good people from entering politics due to the unhealthy competition.

Our Constitution makers were aware of these issues but chose free competition because they believed it works better in the long run. While ideally, leaders would always know what's best and be motivated purely by public service, real life is different. Like other professionals, leaders are motivated by career advancement and power. Depending solely on their sense of duty is risky. Furthermore, even with good intentions, they might not know what people truly want or need.

The realistic approach is to create a system where leaders are rewarded for serving the people and punished for failing to do so. The people decide this through elections. Regular electoral competition incentivizes parties and leaders: they know that addressing people's issues increases their chances of victory, while failing voters leads to defeat.

Thus, even if a party is motivated solely by the desire for power, competition forces it to serve the people. This is similar to how a market works: a shopkeeper focused only on profit must provide good service or lose customers. Political competition may cause divisions and unpleasantness, but ultimately, it compels parties and leaders to serve the public.
