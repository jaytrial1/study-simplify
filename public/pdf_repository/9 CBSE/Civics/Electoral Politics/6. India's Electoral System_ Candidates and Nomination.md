# India's Electoral System: Candidates and Nomination

## Reserved Constituencies

While the Constitution guarantees every citizen the right to elect and be elected, the makers were concerned that weaker sections might struggle to win against influential and resourceful competitors in open elections. This could prevent significant parts of the population from having a voice in Parliament and Assemblies, making democracy less representative.

To address this, a special system of **reserved constituencies** was created for weaker sections. Some constituencies are reserved for **Scheduled Castes (SC)** and **Scheduled Tribes (ST)**. Only candidates belonging to the specified category can contest from these constituencies. As of 1 September 2012, 84 Lok Sabha seats were reserved for SCs and 47 for STs, proportionate to their population share. This system ensures that reserved seats do not reduce the legitimate share of other groups.

This reservation system was later extended to district and local levels. In many states, seats in rural and urban local bodies are reserved for **Other Backward Classes (OBC)**, with proportions varying by state. Additionally, one-third of seats in rural and urban local bodies are reserved for **women candidates**.

-   Why do some states have such a large number of constituencies?

> **Answer:** Lok Sabha constituencies are allocated to states based on their population. States with larger populations have a larger number of Lok Sabha constituencies to ensure that each MP represents a roughly equal number of people across the country.

-   Why are some constituencies small in area while others are very big?

> **Answer:** Constituencies are delimited based on population, not geographical area. Densely populated areas (like cities) will have many constituencies packed into a small geographical area, resulting in small constituencies. Sparsely populated areas (like rural or hilly regions) will require a large geographical area to reach the target population for one constituency, resulting in very large constituencies.

## Nomination of candidates

For a democratic election to offer real choice, there should be minimal restrictions on who can contest. India's system allows anyone who is eligible to vote to also become a candidate, with one main difference: the minimum age for a candidate is 25 years, while for a voter it is 18. There are minor restrictions for extreme cases like serious criminals. Political parties nominate candidates, giving them the party symbol and support, often called a party '**ticket**'.

Every person wishing to contest must fill a **nomination form** and pay a **security deposit**.

Recently, following a Supreme Court directive, a new system requires candidates to make a legal declaration providing full details of:
-   Serious criminal cases pending against them.
-   Assets and liabilities of the candidate and their family.
-   Educational qualifications of the candidate.

This information is made public, allowing voters to make informed decisions based on the candidates' backgrounds.
