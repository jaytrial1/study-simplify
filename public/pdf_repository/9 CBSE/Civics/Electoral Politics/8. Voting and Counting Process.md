# Polling and counting of votes

The final step is the day voters cast their votes, known as **election day**. Voters go to a nearby **polling booth**, usually in a school or government office. Election officials identify the voter, mark their finger, and allow them to vote. An agent from each candidate is allowed inside the booth to ensure fair voting.

## Are the elections too expensive for our country?

Conducting elections in India involves significant expenditure. The government spent about Rs. 1,300 crores on the 2004 Lok Sabha elections (around Rs. 20 per voter). Parties and candidates spent even more, estimated at around Rs. 3,000 crores or Rs. 50 per voter.

Some argue this is a burden for a poor country. However, compare this to other expenses:
-   Buying six nuclear submarines from France in 2005 cost about Rs. 3,000 crores each.
-   Hosting the Commonwealth Games in Delhi in 2010 cost over Rs. 10,000 crores.

# Election result in Gulbarga

Let's revisit the Gulbarga example from 2004. 11 candidates contested. Out of 14.39 lakh eligible voters, 8.28 lakh voted. The Congress candidate, Iqbal Ahmed Saradgi, won with 3.12 lakh votes, about 38% of the total votes polled. He was declared elected because he secured the highest number of votes, even though it was less than half.

-   To win an election is it necessary for a person to secure more than half the votes polled?

> **Answer:** No, it is not necessary. The example of Iqbal Ahmed Saradgi in Gulbarga shows he won with only 37.76% of the votes polled because he secured the highest number of votes among all candidates. The system used is **'first past the post'**, where the candidate with the most votes wins, regardless of whether it's over 50%.

Historically, voters used a **ballot paper** to stamp their choice. A ballot paper lists candidates, parties, and symbols. Nowadays, **Electronic Voting Machines (EVMs)** record votes. EVMs show candidate names, party symbols (independent candidates get symbols too), and voters press a button for their choice.

After polling, EVMs are sealed and stored securely. On a fixed date, EVMs are opened, and votes are counted. Candidate agents are present to ensure proper counting. The candidate with the highest votes in a constituency wins. Counting usually happens simultaneously across constituencies on the same day. Results are declared within hours, determining who forms the next government.

Identify the fair and the unfair electoral practices among the following:

-   A minister flags off a new train in his constituency a week before polling day.
-   A candidate promises that she will get a new train for her constituency if she is elected.
-   Supporters of a candidate take the voters to a temple and make them take an oath that they will vote for him.
-   The supporters of a candidate distribute blankets in slums in return for a promise for vote.

> **Answer:**
> -   A minister flags off a new train in his constituency a week before polling day. **Unfair.** This likely violates the Model Code of Conduct, which prohibits ministers from laying foundation stones or making policy decisions/promises of public facilities once elections are announced, as it can be seen as using governmental power to influence voters.
> -   A candidate promises that she will get a new train for her constituency if she is elected. **Fair.** Candidates making promises about future actions or development projects is a normal part of election campaigns and is not prohibited by law or the Model Code of Conduct, as long as it doesn't involve illegal inducements.
> -   Supporters of a candidate take the voters to a temple and make them take an oath that they will vote for him. **Unfair.** Appealing to voters in the name of religion is prohibited by election law.
> -   The supporters of a candidate distribute blankets in slums in return for a promise for vote. **Unfair.** Bribing voters (distributing goods in return for votes) is prohibited by election law.

Why are party agents present in the polling booth and the counting centre?

> **Answer:** Party agents are present in the polling booth and counting centre to ensure that the voting and counting processes are conducted in a fair and proper manner. They act as representatives of their respective candidates/parties, observing the procedures to prevent any malpractice, rigging, or unfair activities.
