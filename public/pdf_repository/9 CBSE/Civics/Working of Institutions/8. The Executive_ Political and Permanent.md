This section introduces the concept of the executive and differentiates between the political executive (leaders) and the permanent executive (civil servants).

# The Political Executive

We saw in the Mandal case that the person signing the order was executing a decision made by others, like the Prime Minister and Cabinet, who in turn were influenced by Parliament.

At different levels of government, functionaries take daily decisions but don't necessarily hold supreme power. Collectively, these functionaries are called the **executive** because they are in charge of 'executing' or implementing government policies. When we refer to 'the government' in common usage, we often mean the executive.

In a democracy, the executive comprises two categories:

1.  **Political Executive:** Elected by the people for a specific period. This includes political leaders who make major decisions (like Ministers).
2.  **Permanent Executive:** Appointed on a long-term basis. This is the **civil services**, consisting of **civil servants**. They remain in office regardless of changes in the ruling party and assist the political executive in administration.

In the Mandal case, the Minister of Personnel was part of the political executive, while the Joint Secretary who signed the order was part of the permanent executive (civil services).

You might wonder why the political executive has more power than the permanent executive, especially since civil servants often have more expertise. For example, Finance Ministry advisors know more economics than the Finance Minister. However, in a democracy, the **will of the people is supreme**. The minister, elected by the people, is empowered to exercise this will and is ultimately answerable to the people for their decisions.

The minister decides the overall framework and objectives for policy decisions, relying on expert advice for technical details. Experts may offer different options, and the minister chooses based on the broader objectives. This is similar to any large organisation where those with the overall picture make the most important decisions, not just the technical experts. Experts provide the routes, but the elected minister decides the destination.

The race to become minister is not new. Here is a cartoon depicting ministerial aspirants waiting to get a berth in Nehru's Cabinet after the 1962 elections. Why do you think political leaders are so keen to become ministers?
> **Answer:** Political leaders are keen to become ministers because ministerial positions hold significant power and influence. Ministers are in charge of major government departments, allowing them to make important policy decisions, control resources, and implement programs that can affect many people. It provides an opportunity to shape the country's direction and serve the public (or pursue political goals).
