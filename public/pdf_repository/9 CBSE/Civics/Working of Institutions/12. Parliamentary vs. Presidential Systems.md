# Presidential System vs. Parliamentary System

Presidents worldwide are not all nominal executives like in India. In many countries, the President is both the **head of the state** and the **head of the government**. The US President is a prime example.

In the **Presidential form of government**:

*   The President is directly elected by the people.
*   The President personally chooses and appoints all Ministers.
*   The legislature (like the Congress in the US) makes laws, but the President can veto them.
*   The President does not need the support of the majority in the legislature and is not answerable to them.
*   The President has a fixed tenure (e.g., four years in the US), regardless of the legislature's majority.

This system is common in Latin America and many ex-Soviet countries. Due to the President's central role, it's called the Presidential system.

In countries like India, which follow the British model, the Parliament is supreme, and the executive (Prime Minister and Cabinet) is answerable to the legislature. This is called the **Parliamentary system of government**.

What is better for a democracy: A Prime Minister who can do whatever he wishes or a Prime Minister who needs to consult other leaders and parties?
> **Answer:** For a democracy, a Prime Minister who needs to consult other leaders and parties is generally considered better. While a powerful PM can be decisive, a system requiring consultation (like in a parliamentary system or coalition government) ensures:
> *   Decisions are based on broader consensus.
> *   Different perspectives and interests are considered.
>   *   Checks and balances are maintained on the executive's power.
> *   It prevents arbitrary or hasty decisions ("rushing through a bad decision").
> *   It promotes accountability to Parliament and, indirectly, to the people represented by various parties.
> A PM doing whatever they wish resembles authoritarian rule, not democracy, which thrives on deliberation and representation.

Eliamma: What happens if the President and the Prime Minister disagree about some policy? Does the view of the Prime Minister always prevail?
> **Answer:** According to the Constitution, the President must act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister. While the President can ask the Council to reconsider its advice, if the Council reiterates the same advice, the President is bound to accept it. So, yes, the view of the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers generally prevails.

Annakutti: I find it funny that the President is the Supreme Commander of Armed Forces. I doubt if the President can even lift a heavy gun. What is the point in making the President the Commander?
> **Answer:** The President being the Supreme Commander is a symbolic and constitutional role, not a military one requiring physical strength. It signifies that the control of the armed forces ultimately rests with the civilian, elected head of state, ensuring that the military is subordinate to democratic authority. It's a principle of civilian control over the military, essential in a democracy.

Marymol: I would say, what is the point in having a President at all if all the real powers are with the Prime Minister?
> **Answer:** Even with nominal powers, the President plays important roles:
> *   **Head of State:** Represents the nation as a whole, providing continuity and stability regardless of changes in government.
> *   **Constitutional Guardian:** Ensures the government functions according to the Constitution.
> *   **Appointment Authority:** Formally appoints the Prime Minister and other key officials.
> *   **Check on Parliament:** Can return bills for reconsideration.
> *   **Discretionary Power:** Can exercise discretion in appointing a PM when there's no clear majority, helping in government formation.
> The President acts as a constitutional check and symbol of national unity, distinct from the day-to-day political executive led by the Prime Minister.
