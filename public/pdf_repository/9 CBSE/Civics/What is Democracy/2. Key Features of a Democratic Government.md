This section expands on the simple definition of democracy by exploring four essential features that distinguish it from non-democratic forms, using examples from different countries.

# Features of Democracy

Starting with the simple definition – democracy is a form of government in which the rulers are elected by the people – raises several important questions:

*   **Who are the rulers?** Which officials must be elected? Which decisions can be made by non-elected officials?
*   **What kind of election?** What conditions make an election democratic?
*   **Who are the people?** Should all citizens have an equal right to elect and be elected? Can a democracy deny this right?
*   **What form of government?** Can elected rulers do anything they want? Must a democratic government operate within limits? Are citizens' rights necessary?

Let's examine these questions with examples.

## Major decisions by elected leaders

In Pakistan, General Pervez Musharraf overthrew a democratically elected government in October 1999, declaring himself 'Chief Executive' and later President. In 2002, he held a referendum to get a five-year extension. Media, human rights groups, and democracy activists claimed the referendum was based on malpractices. In August 2002, he issued a 'Legal Framework Order' amending the Constitution, giving the President power to dismiss national and provincial assemblies. A National Security Council, dominated by military officers, supervises the civilian cabinet. After this law, elections were held, and elected representatives gained some powers. However, final power remained with military officers and General Musharraf, none of whom were elected by the people.

This leads to the first feature: In a democracy, the **final decision-making power must rest with those elected by the people**.

## Free and fair electoral competition

In China, elections occur every five years for the National People's Congress, which appoints the President. It has nearly 3,000 members, some elected by the army. Candidates need approval from the Chinese Communist Party. Only members of the Communist Party or eight allied parties could contest elections in 2002-03. The government is always formed by the Communist Party.

Since independence in 1930, Mexico has held elections every six years for President and has not been under military or dictator rule. However, until 2000, the PRI (Institutional Revolutionary Party) won every election. Opposition parties contested but never won. The PRI used various unfair methods: forcing government employees to attend party meetings, teachers pressuring parents to vote for PRI, media ignoring opposition except to criticise them, shifting polling booths last minute, and spending large sums on campaigns.

Should these elections be considered examples of people electing their rulers? In China, there's no serious choice; people must choose the ruling party or approved candidates. In Mexico, there seemed to be a choice, but unfair practices ensured the ruling party could not be defeated. These are not **fair elections**.

This gives us the second feature: Holding elections is not enough. Elections must offer a **real choice** between political alternatives, and it must be possible for people to use this choice to remove existing rulers. A democracy must be based on a **free and fair election where those currently in power have a fair chance of losing**. More on this in Chapter Four.

## One person, one vote, one value

The struggle for democracy has often been linked to the demand for **universal adult franchise** (right to vote for all adults). This is now widely accepted, but there are still instances where equal voting rights are denied:

*   In Saudi Arabia, women do not have the right to vote.
*   Estonia's citizenship rules make it difficult for the Russian minority to get voting rights.
*   In Fiji, the electoral system gives the vote of an indigenous Fijian more value than that of an Indian-Fijian.

Democracy is founded on the principle of **political equality**. This gives us the third feature: In a democracy, **each adult citizen must have one vote and each vote must have one value**. More on this in Chapter Four.

## Rule of law and respect for rights

Zimbabwe gained independence in 1980 and has been ruled by ZANU-PF, led by Robert Mugabe, since then. Mugabe is popular but uses unfair election practices. His government has repeatedly changed the constitution to increase presidential powers and reduce accountability. Opposition workers are harassed, meetings disrupted, and protests declared illegal. A law limits criticism of the President. State controls television and radio, only presenting the ruling party's view. Independent newspapers exist but journalists critical of the government are harassed. The government has ignored court judgments and pressured judges.

The Zimbabwe example shows that popular approval is necessary but not sufficient for democracy. Popular governments and leaders can be **undemocratic** or **autocratic**. To assess a democracy, look not just at elections, but also at what happens *before* and *after* them. There must be room for normal political activity, including opposition. This requires the state to respect basic **citizens' rights**: freedom of thought, expression, association, protest, and political action. Everyone must be equal before the law. These rights need protection by an **independent judiciary** whose orders are obeyed. More on rights in Chapter Six.

Similarly, a democratic government must follow rules after winning elections. It cannot do anything it wants. It must respect basic rules, including guarantees to minorities. Major decisions require consultation. Office bearers have rights and responsibilities defined by the constitution and law, and are accountable to the people and independent officials. More on this in Chapter Five.

These aspects lead to the fourth feature: A democratic government **rules within limits set by constitutional law and citizens' rights**.

## Summary definition

To sum up, we started with: **democracy is a form of government in which the rulers are elected by the people**. We found this inadequate and clarified key terms through examples, leading to four features:

1.  Rulers elected by the people take **all the major decisions**.
2.  Elections offer a **choice and fair opportunity** to the people to change the current rulers.
3.  This choice and opportunity is available to **all the people on an equal basis**.
4.  The exercise of this choice leads to a government **limited by basic rules of the constitution and citizens' rights**.
