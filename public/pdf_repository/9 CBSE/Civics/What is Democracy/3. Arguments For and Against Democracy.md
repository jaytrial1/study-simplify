# Why Democracy?

A debate arose in Madam Lyngdoh's class after discussing the features of democracy. Students shared their opinions on whether democracy is the best form of government.

Madam Lyngdoh commented that she was happy to see passionate arguments and the freedom to express opinions, asking if this freedom itself is an argument for democracy.

## Arguments against democracy

The class discussion covered many common arguments against democracy:

*   Leaders change frequently, leading to **instability**.
*   It's about political **competition and power play**, not morality.
*   Consulting many people leads to **delays**.
*   Elected leaders may not know the **best interests of the people**, leading to bad decisions.
*   It leads to **corruption** because it's based on electoral competition.
*   Ordinary people are **ignorant** and shouldn't decide anything.

Consider:
*   Are there other arguments against democracy you can think of?
*   Which of these arguments applies mainly to democracy?
*   Which can apply to the misuse of *any* form of government?
*   Which of these do you agree with?

> **Answer:** Other arguments might include the tyranny of the majority, or that it can be inefficient. Arguments like instability, delays, and corruption are often specifically leveled against democracy, though corruption can occur in any system. Bad decisions and leaders not knowing people's interests can happen anywhere. Arguments about political competition and the alleged ignorance of ordinary people are more specific critiques of democratic processes. Which arguments one agrees with is subjective and depends on one's perspective and experiences.

Democracy is not a perfect solution. It hasn't ended poverty everywhere. It ensures people make their own decisions, but doesn't guarantee good decisions. People make mistakes. Involving many people can cause delays and frequent leadership changes can affect efficiency.

These points show that the democracy we see may not be ideal. But the real question is: Is democracy better than the *alternatives* available?

## Arguments for democracy

The China famine of 1958-1961 killed nearly three crore people. India's economic state was similar, but it didn't have such a famine. Economists believe this difference was due to government policies. Democracy in India made the government respond to food scarcity, which the Chinese government did not. No large-scale famine has occurred in an independent, democratic country. If China had multiparty elections, an opposition, and a free press, fewer people might have died.

This cartoon is from Brazil, which experienced dictatorship. It's titled "The Hidden Side of Dictatorship".
*   Which hidden sides does this cartoon depict?
*   Is it necessary for every dictatorship to have a hidden side?
*   Find this out about the dictators discussed in the first chapter and, if possible, about Sani Abacha in Nigeria and Ferdinand Marcos in the Philippines.

> **Answer:** The cartoon depicts the hidden side of dictatorship as death, brutality, and repression (symbolized by the skull). It suggests that behind the facade of order or strong rule, dictatorships often rely on violence and suppress human rights. While the *degree* of brutality varies, dictatorships inherently concentrate power and lack accountability, making repression a common tool to maintain control. Dictatorships discussed in Chapter 1 (Pinochet, Nkrumah in later phase) also used force and suppressed opposition.

This example highlights a key reason democracy is considered better: it is more **accountable** to the people. A non-democratic government *can* respond to needs, but only if the rulers *wish* to. In a democracy, rulers *have* to attend to people's needs because they face elections.

Another reason for better decisions in democracy is its basis in **consultation and discussion**. Decisions involve many people, discussions, and meetings. This process allows potential mistakes to be identified, reducing the chances of rash or irresponsible decisions, thus improving the **quality of decision-making**.

Thirdly, democracy provides a method to deal with **differences and conflicts**. Societies have diverse opinions and interests, especially in a country like India with many regions, languages, religions, and castes. Conflicts can be resolved by force, but this leads to resentment. Democracy offers a peaceful solution: no one is a permanent winner or loser. Different groups can live together peacefully. In a diverse country, democracy helps keep it together.

These three arguments focus on democracy's effects on government quality and social life. But the strongest argument relates to its impact on **citizens**. Even if it doesn't guarantee better decisions or accountability, democracy enhances the **dignity of citizens**. It is based on **political equality**, recognizing that everyone, rich or poor, educated or uneducated, has the same status. People are rulers, not subjects. Even when they make mistakes, they are responsible.

Finally, democracy allows us to **correct its own mistakes**. No government form guarantees mistake-free rule. But in democracy, mistakes can be discussed publicly and corrected. Rulers can change decisions, or the rulers themselves can be changed. This isn't possible in non-democratic governments.

To summarise: Democracy isn't perfect or a solution to all problems. But it's better than known alternatives. It offers better chances for good decisions, is likely to respect people's wishes, allows diverse people to live together, provides a way to correct mistakes, and offers more dignity to citizens. That's why it's considered the best form of government.

This cartoon was published in Canada before the 2004 parliamentary elections. The cartoonist expected the Liberal party to win, but they lost.
*   Is this cartoon an argument against democracy or for democracy?

> **Answer:** The cartoon could be seen as an argument *against* democracy, highlighting voter apathy or the perceived predictability and ineffectiveness of democratic choice ("governed no better than we deserve"). However, the fact that the Liberal Party actually *lost* the election, contrary to the cartoonist's expectation, demonstrates democracy's potential for change and correcting perceived problems, which is an argument *for* democracy. Thus, while the cartoon *intends* to critique, the real-world outcome it refers to supports the argument that democracy allows for rulers to be changed.
