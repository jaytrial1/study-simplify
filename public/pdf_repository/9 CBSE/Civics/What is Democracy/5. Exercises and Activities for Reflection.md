# Exercises

1.  Here is some information about four countries. Based on this information, how would you classify each of these countries? Write 'democratic', 'undemocratic' or 'not sure' against each of these.
    a Country A: People who do not accept the country's official religion do not have a right to vote.
    > **Answer:** undemocratic (Denies political equality based on religion).
    b Country B: The same party has been winning elections for the last twenty years.
    > **Answer:** not sure (Winning repeatedly doesn't automatically mean it's undemocratic; it could be genuinely popular, although long dominance can sometimes raise questions about the fairness of the electoral field).
    c Country C: Ruling party has lost in the last three elections.
    > **Answer:** democratic (The ruling party losing shows elections are free and fair, allowing for change).
    d Country D: There is no independent election commission.
    > **Answer:** undemocratic (Lack of an independent body to conduct elections compromises fairness and freeness).

2.  Here is some information about four countries. Based on this information, how would you classify each of these countries? Write 'democratic', 'undemocratic' or 'not sure' against each of these.
    a Country P: The parliament cannot pass a law about the army without the consent of the Chief of Army.
    > **Answer:** undemocratic (Unelected military having final say over elected legislature's decisions violates principle of elected rulers making major decisions).
    b Country Q: The parliament cannot pass a law reducing the powers of the judiciary.
    > **Answer:** democratic (This suggests checks and balances and respect for the judiciary's independence and rule of law, which are features of democracy).
    c Country R: The country's leaders cannot sign any treaty with another country without taking permission from its neighbouring country.
    > **Answer:** undemocratic (Lack of sovereign decision-making power limits the independence required for democratic rule).
    d Country S: All the major economic decisions about the country are taken by officials of the central bank which the ministers cannot change.
    > **Answer:** undemocratic (Unelected officials making major decisions without accountability to elected representatives violates the principle of elected rulers taking major decisions).

3.  Which of these is not a good argument in favour of democracy? Why?
    a People feel free and equal in a democracy.
    b Democracies resolve conflict in a better way than others.
    c Democratic government is more accountable to the people.
    d Democracies are more prosperous than others.
    > **Answer:** d Democracies are more prosperous than others. While democracy can create conditions favorable for economic growth, it does not guarantee prosperity and many democracies still face poverty. The text itself mentions poverty in India. Prosperity is not a defining feature or guaranteed outcome of democracy compared to its core principles like equality, conflict resolution, and accountability.

4.  Each of these statements contains a democratic and an undemocratic element. Write out the two separately for each statement.
    a A minister said that some laws have to be passed by the parliament in order to conform to the regulations decided by the World Trade Organisation (WTO).
    > **Answer:** Democratic element: Laws are passed by the parliament (elected body). Undemocratic element: Laws are dictated by an external organisation (WTO), potentially limiting the sovereignty and decision-making power of the elected parliament based on the will of the people.
    b The Election Commission ordered re-polling in a constituency where large-scale rigging was reported.
    > **Answer:** Democratic element: Election Commission ensuring fair elections by ordering re-polling. Undemocratic element: Large-scale rigging occurring in the first place, undermining the freeness and fairness of the election.
    c Women's representation in the parliament has barely reached 10 per cent. This led women's organisations to demand one-third seats for women.
    > **Answer:** Democratic element: Women's organisations demanding representation (exercising freedom of expression and association, participating in political action). Undemocratic element: Low representation of women (lack of political equality and equal opportunity in practice, despite formal rights).

5.  Which of these is not a valid reason for arguing that there is a lesser possibility of famine in a democratic country?
    a Opposition parties can draw attention to hunger and starvation.
    b Free press can report suffering from famine in different parts of the country.
    c Government fears its defeat in the next elections.
    d People are free to believe in and practise any religion.
    > **Answer:** d People are free to believe in and practise any religion. While religious freedom is a key democratic right, it is not directly related to the government's mechanism for responding to food scarcity or preventing famine. Options a, b, and c all relate to accountability, information flow, and political pressure that compel a democratic government to act to prevent famine, as discussed in the text.

6.  There are 40 villages in a district where the government has made no provision for drinking water. These villagers met and considered many methods of forcing the government to respond to their need. Which of these is not a democratic method?
    a Filing a case in the courts claiming that water is part of right to life.
    b Boycotting the next elections to give a message to all parties.
    c Organising public meetings against government's policies.
    d Paying money to government officials to get water.
    > **Answer:** d Paying money to government officials to get water. This is a form of bribery and corruption, which is illegal and undermines democratic processes based on equal access and rule of law, not a legitimate democratic method of protest or influencing policy.

7.  Write a response to the following arguments against democracy:
    a Army is the most disciplined and corruption-free organisation in the country. Therefore army should rule the country.
    > **Answer:** Response: While the army may be disciplined, discipline alone is not sufficient for governing a diverse society. Army rule is undemocratic because military leaders are not elected by the people and are not accountable to them. A government needs legitimacy derived from the consent of the governed, not just order. Furthermore, claiming the army is corruption-free is often debatable, and without public scrutiny and accountability mechanisms present in a democracy, corruption can easily flourish unchecked in a military regime.
    b Rule of the majority means the rule of ignorant people. What we need is the rule of the wise, even if they are in small numbers.
    > **Answer:** Response: This argument is elitist and undemocratic. Firstly, who decides who is "wise"? This power could be easily abused. Secondly, it denies the principle of political equality – that every adult citizen's opinion matters. While the majority can make mistakes, democracy provides mechanisms (like free press, opposition, public debate, and future elections) to identify and correct these mistakes, which is absent in a system ruled by a self-proclaimed "wise" minority who are not accountable to the people.
    c If we want religious leaders to guide us in spiritual matters, why not invite them to guide us in politics as well. The country should be ruled by religious leaders.
    > **Answer:** Response: Religious guidance is based on faith and specific religious texts, which differ among various religions and may not be accepted by everyone in a diverse country. Political rule in a democracy is based on laws and policies intended to serve the interests of all citizens, regardless of their faith. Rule by religious leaders would impose the beliefs of one group on everyone, denying political equality to citizens of other faiths or no faith. It also lacks accountability to the entire population in the way that elected political leaders are.

8.  Are the following statements in keeping with democracy as a value? Why?
    a Father to daughter: I don't want to hear your opinion about your marriage. In our family children marry where the parents tell them to.
    > **Answer:** Not in keeping with democracy as a value. Democracy involves consultation and consent of those affected by a decision, and valuing each individual's opinion. This statement denies the daughter's autonomy and equal say in a major decision about her own life, violating the democratic principle of individual dignity and equal participation.
    b Teacher to student: Don't disturb my concentration by asking me questions in the classroom.
    > **Answer:** Not in keeping with democracy as a value, particularly in the context of a "democratic classroom". A democratic environment encourages participation, questioning, and open discussion, where students feel free to seek clarification and contribute. This statement shuts down interaction and denies the student's right to participate in their own learning process.
    c Employee to the officer: Our working hours must be reduced according to the law.
    > **Answer:** In keeping with democracy as a value. This statement asserts a right based on established law, appealing to the principle of rule of law and the idea that everyone, including officials, is bound by the law. It reflects a citizen/employee being aware of their rights and demanding that rules are respected, which is fundamental in a democratic system.

9.  Consider the following facts about a country and decide if you would call it a democracy. Give reasons to support your decision.
    a All the citizens of the country have right to vote. Elections are held regularly.
    b The country took loan from international agencies. One of the conditions for giving loan was that the government would reduce its expenses on education and health.
    c People speak more than seven languages but education is available only in one language, the language spoken by 52 percent people of that country.
    d Several organisations have given a call for peaceful demonstrations and nation wide strikes in the country to oppose these policies. Government has arrested these leaders.
    e The government owns the radio and television in the country. All the newspapers have to get permission from the government to publish any news about government's policies and protests.
    > **Answer:** Based on points c, d, and e, this country should **not** be called a democracy, despite having elections and voting rights (point a).
    *   Point c shows a denial of political equality and equal opportunity to linguistic minorities by providing education in only one language.
    *   Point d shows the suppression of basic democratic rights like freedom of assembly and protest by arresting leaders of peaceful demonstrations.
    *   Point e shows a severe lack of freedom of the press and access to independent information, which is crucial for citizens to make informed choices in elections and hold the government accountable.
    *   Point b is less conclusive on its own, as elected governments can choose to accept loan conditions, but combined with the other points, it might suggest external influence overriding the needs of the people (e.g., in education and health).
    *   Therefore, the presence of elections and voting rights (a) is insufficient when fundamental rights and freedoms necessary for a truly free and fair political process are suppressed and equality is denied.

10. In 2004 a report published in USA pointed to the increasing inequalities in that country... Write an essay on 'Democracy and Poverty' using the information given in this report but using examples from India.
    > **Answer:**
    ### Essay: Democracy and Poverty in India

    Democracy is founded on the principle of political equality – one person, one vote, one value. However, as highlighted by a 2004 report on the USA, significant economic inequality can pose a challenge to the effective functioning of democracy, even in countries with established democratic institutions. While the report focuses on the US context, its findings about the link between income inequality and political participation resonate strongly in India.

    The US report indicated that affluent families (top 20% income) had significantly higher voting rates (nearly 9 out of 10) compared to low-income families (bottom 20% income, only 5 out of 10). This suggests that poverty can act as a barrier to political participation. In India, similar patterns can be observed. Poorer sections of society often face challenges like lack of access to information, lower literacy levels, and the immediate pressures of earning a livelihood, which can make active political engagement more difficult than for the wealthy.

    Furthermore, the report noted that a large portion of political donations came from the rich, giving them greater opportunity to influence politicians and policy. This is also relevant in India, where election campaigns are expensive, and political parties often rely on funding from wealthy individuals and corporations. This financial leverage can lead to policies that favour business interests and the rich, while the concerns of the poor – such as access to basic necessities, jobs, education, and healthcare – may receive less attention from politicians who are more responsive to their major donors.

    When poor citizens participate less and their concerns are not prioritized, the democratic promise of a government 'for the people' becomes partially unfulfilled. Politicians may primarily hear and respond to the demands of the economically powerful segments of society.

    However, democracy in India also provides crucial avenues for the poor to voice their concerns and fight for their rights. Unlike non-democratic systems, citizens have the right to vote, protest, form associations, and access the judiciary. Political parties, while sometimes influenced by wealth, also need the votes of the poor majority to win elections, creating some pressure to address their issues, at least rhetorically. Civil society organisations and social movements in India frequently use democratic freedoms to highlight poverty-related problems and lobby the government.

    In conclusion, while India is a vibrant democracy with universal adult franchise, economic inequality poses a significant challenge by potentially limiting the effective political participation and influence of the poor. The US report's findings serve as a reminder that true democratic equality requires not just formal rights but also conditions that enable all citizens, regardless of their economic status, to participate meaningfully in the political process and ensure their voices are heard and addressed by the government. The struggle against poverty is thus also a struggle to deepen democracy.
