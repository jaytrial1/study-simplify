This section discusses the challenging circumstances under which the Indian Constitution was drafted, the historical path that led to its formation, the influences on its design, and the composition and working of the Constituent Assembly.

# Making of the Indian Constitution

Similar to South Africa, India's Constitution was crafted under difficult conditions. Creating a constitution for a vast and diverse nation like India was a complex undertaking. At the time, Indians were transitioning from being subjects of colonial rule to citizens of a free country. The nation was born from the Partition based on religious differences, a traumatic event causing immense violence and displacement, resulting in the deaths of at least ten lakh (one million) people on both sides of the border.

Another challenge was the decision left by the British to princely states: merge with India, Pakistan, or remain independent. Integrating these states was a difficult and uncertain process. As the constitution was being written, India's future stability was not guaranteed, leading the constitution-makers to have anxieties about both the present and the future of the country.

## The Path to Constitution

Despite these difficulties, the makers of the Indian Constitution had a significant advantage: a broad consensus on the vision for a democratic India had already developed during the freedom struggle. The national movement was more than just a fight against foreign rule; it was also an effort to revitalize the country and transform its society and politics. Although there were disagreements within the movement about India's future path, some core ideas were widely accepted.

As early as 1928, Motilal Nehru and eight other Congress leaders drafted a constitution for India. In 1931, the Karachi session of the Indian National Congress passed a resolution outlining the vision for an independent India's constitution. Both documents advocated for **universal adult franchise**, the right to freedom and equality, and the protection of minority rights in the future constitution. Thus, many basic values were agreed upon by leaders well before the Constituent Assembly convened.

Familiarity with colonial political institutions also facilitated agreement on the institutional framework. The British had granted voting rights to only a few and established weak legislatures. Elections held in 1937 for Provincial Legislatures and Ministries across British India, though not fully democratic, provided Indians with valuable experience in legislative processes. This experience proved beneficial in establishing India's own institutions. Consequently, the Indian constitution incorporated many institutional details and procedures from colonial laws like the **Government of India Act, 1935**.

Years of reflection and debate on the constitutional framework had another positive outcome: leaders felt confident learning from other countries while adapting ideas to India's specific needs. They were inspired by the ideals of the **French Revolution**, the parliamentary democracy in Britain, and the **Bill of Rights in the US**. The socialist revolution in Russia influenced many to consider a system promoting social and economic equality. However, they did not merely imitate; they critically assessed whether these foreign models suited India. All these factors contributed to the making of the Constitution.

## The Constituent Assembly

The task of drafting the constitution was undertaken by an assembly of elected representatives known as the **Constituent Assembly**. Elections for the Assembly were held in July 1946. Its first meeting took place in December 1946. Shortly after, India was partitioned, and the Assembly was divided into the Constituent Assembly of India and that of Pakistan. The Constituent Assembly that drafted the Indian constitution comprised 299 members. The Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949, and it came into effect on 26 January 1950. This date is celebrated annually as **Republic Day**.

Why should we accept a Constitution drafted over fifty years ago? One reason is that it represented a broad consensus of its time, not just the views of its members. Many countries have had to rewrite their constitutions because the basic rules lacked widespread acceptance among major social groups or political parties. In some nations, the constitution exists only on paper, without being followed in practice. India's experience is different. Although some provisions have been questioned over time, the legitimacy of the Constitution itself has never been challenged by any large social group or political party – a remarkable achievement.

A second reason for its acceptance is that the Constituent Assembly was broadly representative of the Indian people. While universal adult franchise did not exist then, and members were primarily elected by existing Provincial Legislatures, this ensured fair geographical representation from all regions. The Assembly was dominated by the Indian National Congress, the leading party of the freedom struggle, but the Congress itself encompassed diverse political groups and opinions. Many members were not aligned with the Congress. Socially, the Assembly included members from various language groups, castes, classes, religions, and occupations. Even if elected by universal adult franchise, the composition would likely not have been drastically different.

Finally, the process followed by the Constituent Assembly lends sanctity to the Constitution. The Assembly worked systematically, openly, and consensually. They first agreed upon basic principles. Then, a Drafting Committee, chaired by **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar**, prepared a draft constitution. The draft underwent several rounds of detailed clause-by-clause discussion. Over two thousand amendments were considered. Members deliberated for 114 days over three years. Every document presented and every word spoken has been recorded and preserved as the '**Constituent Assembly Debates**', which fill 12 large volumes. These debates provide the reasoning behind each provision and are used to interpret the Constitution's meaning.
