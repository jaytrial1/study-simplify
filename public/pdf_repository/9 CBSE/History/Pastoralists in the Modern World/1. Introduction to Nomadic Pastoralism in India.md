*Bugyals* are vast natural pastures on the high mountains, above 12,000 feet. They are under snow in the winter and come to life after April. At this time the entire mountainside is covered with a variety of grasses, roots and herbs. By monsoon, these pastures are thick with vegetation and carpeted with wild flowers.

This chapter will discuss nomadic pastoralists. Nomads are people who do not live in one place but move from one area to another to earn their living. In many parts of India, nomadic pastoralists can be seen moving with their herds of goats and sheep, or camels and cattle. Their lives and history, though often overlooked in standard history and economics texts, are important in societies like India and Africa. This chapter will explore how pastoralism has been significant, how colonialism impacted their lives, and how they have coped with the pressures of modern society, focusing first on India and then Africa.

# Pastoral Nomads and their Movements

## In the Mountains

The **Gujjar Bakarwals** of Jammu and Kashmir are known herders of goat and sheep. Many migrated to this region in the nineteenth century seeking pastures. Over time, they established a pattern of annual movement between summer and winter grazing grounds.

*   **Winter:** They live in the low hills of the Siwalik range with their herds, where dry scrub forests provide pasture when high mountains are snow-covered.
*   **Summer:** By late April, they move north in groups called a *kafila*, crossing the Pir Panjal passes into the Kashmir valley. As snow melts, the mountainsides become lush with nutritious grasses for their animals.
*   **Return:** By late September, they begin their downward journey back to the Siwalik hills for winter.

In Himachal Pradesh, the **Gaddi shepherds** follow a similar seasonal cycle.

*   **Winter:** They spend winter in the low Siwalik hills, grazing flocks in scrub forests.
*   **Summer:** By April, they move north to Lahul and Spiti. When passes clear, many go to higher mountain meadows.
*   **Return:** They begin their return movement by September. On the way, they may stop in Lahul and Spiti villages to shear sheep, reap summer harvests, and sow winter crops before descending to the Siwalik hills.

## Source A

Writing in the 1850s, G.C. Barnes described the Gujjars of Kangra:
'In the hills the Gujjars are exclusively a pastoral tribe - they cultivate scarcely at all. The Gaddis keep flocks of sheep and goats and the Gujjars, wealth consists of buffaloes. These people live in the skirts of the forests, and maintain their existence exclusively by the sale of the milk, ghee, and other produce of their herds. The men graze the cattle, and frequently lie out for weeks in the woods tending their herds. The women repair to the markets every morning with baskets on their heads, with little earthen pots filled with milk, butter-milk and ghee, each of these pots containing the proportion required for a day's meal. During the hot weather the Gujjars usually drive their herds to the upper range, where the buffaloes rejoice in the rich grass which the rains bring forth and at the same time attain condition from the temperate climate and the immunity from venomous flies that torment their existence in the plains.'
From: G.C. Barnes, Settlement Report of Kangra, 1850-55.

Further east, in Garhwal and Kumaon, the **Gujjar cattle herders** move down to the dry forests of the *bhabar* in winter and up to the high meadows (*bugyals*) in summer. Many originated from Jammu and came to the UP hills in the nineteenth century seeking pastures.

This pattern of cyclical movement is typical for many Himalayan pastoral communities like the **Bhotiyas, Sherpas, and Kinnauris**. They adjust to seasonal changes, using available pastures effectively. When pasture is exhausted in one place, they move herds to new areas. This continuous movement allows pastures to recover and prevents overuse.

### New words

*   **Bhabar:** A dry forested area below the foothills of Garhwal and Kumaun.
*   **Bugyal:** Vast meadows in the high mountains.
