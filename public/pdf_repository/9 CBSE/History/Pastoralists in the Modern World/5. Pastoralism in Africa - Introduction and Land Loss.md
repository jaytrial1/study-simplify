This section shifts focus to Africa, home to a large portion of the world's pastoral population, introducing various communities and detailing how colonial powers drastically reduced their traditional grazing lands, specifically focusing on the Maasai experience.

# Pastoralism in Africa

Over half the world's pastoral population lives in Africa, with over 22 million Africans depending on pastoral activity. Communities include Bedouins, Berbers, Maasai, Somali, Boran, and Turkana. Most live in semi-arid grasslands or deserts where rainfed agriculture is difficult. They raise cattle, camels, goats, sheep, and donkeys, selling milk, meat, skin, and wool. Some trade, transport goods, or combine pastoralism with agriculture or odd jobs.

African pastoralists' lives changed dramatically during colonial and post-colonial periods. We will examine these changes through the **Maasai**, cattle herders primarily in east Africa (Kenya and Tanzania). New laws and regulations took away their land and restricted movement, impacting their lives, especially during drought, and reshaping social relationships.

## Where have the Grazing Lands Gone?

A major problem for the Maasai is the continuous loss of grazing lands. Before colonial times, Maasailand was vast, stretching from north Kenya to northern Tanzania. In the late nineteenth century, European powers divided Africa into colonies. In 1885, Maasailand was split by the boundary between British Kenya and German Tanganyika.

## On Tanganyika

Britain conquered what had been German East Africa during the First World War. In 1919 Tanganyika came under British control. It attained independence in 1961 and united with Zanzibar to form Tanzania in 1964.

Subsequently, the best grazing lands were taken for white settlement, pushing the Maasai into a small, arid zone in south Kenya and north Tanzania. The Maasai lost about 60% of their pre-colonial lands, confined to an area with uncertain rainfall and poor pastures.

From the late nineteenth century, the British encouraged local peasant communities to expand cultivation, turning more pasture into fields. Before colonialism, Maasai pastoralists dominated agricultural neighbours economically and politically. By the end of colonial rule, this was reversed.

Large grazing areas were also turned into game reserves like Maasai Mara, Samburu National Park (Kenya), and Serengeti Park (Tanzania). Pastoralists were forbidden entry to hunt or graze herds. These reserves often covered traditional Maasai grazing grounds. Serengeti National Park, for example, was created over 14,760 sq km of Maasai land.
