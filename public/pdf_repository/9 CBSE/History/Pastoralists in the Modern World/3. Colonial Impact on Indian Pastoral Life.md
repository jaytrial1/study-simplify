This section details how colonial rule in India drastically altered the lives of pastoralists by restricting their movements, reducing their access to grazing lands, increasing taxation, and changing their social status.

# Colonial Rule and Pastoral Life

Under colonial rule, the life of pastoralists changed dramatically. Their grazing grounds shrank, their movements were regulated, and the revenue they had to pay increased. Their agricultural stock declined, and their trades and crafts were adversely affected.

The colonial state aimed to convert grazing lands into cultivated farms for revenue and agricultural produce needed in England. Uncultivated land was seen as 'waste land' that should be cultivated. From the mid-nineteenth century, **Waste Land Rules** were enacted, taking over uncultivated lands and giving them to select individuals, often headmen, to settle and cultivate. These lands were frequently grazing tracts used by pastoralists, so expanding cultivation directly reduced pastures, creating problems for pastoralists.

Secondly, **Forest Acts** were enacted from the mid-nineteenth century. Forests with valuable timber were declared 'Reserved,' denying pastoralists access. Other forests were 'Protected,' allowing some customary grazing rights but with severe movement restrictions. Colonial officials believed grazing harmed saplings and new trees. These acts prevented pastoralists from using forests that were previously valuable forage areas.

## Source C

H.S. Gibson, the Deputy Conservator of Forests, Darjeeling, wrote in 1913:
'... forest which is used for grazing cannot be used for any other purpose and is unable to yield timber and fuel, which are the main legitimate forest produce ...'

## Activity

Write a comment on the closure of the forests to grazing from the standpoint of:
> a forester
> **Answer:** From a forester's standpoint, closing forests to grazing is necessary for conservation and commercial forestry. Grazing animals damage young trees and vegetation, preventing forest regeneration. Protecting forests ensures they can produce valuable timber and fuel, which are considered the primary products of the forest.

> a pastoralist
> **Answer:** From a pastoralist's standpoint, closing forests to grazing is a severe hardship. Forests are crucial for feeding their herds, especially during certain seasons or droughts when other pastures are scarce. Restricting access means their animals have less food, leading to weaker stock, potential starvation, and difficulty maintaining their livelihood. It disrupts their traditional movement patterns and threatens their way of life.

### New words

*   **Customary rights:** Rights that people are used to by custom and tradition.

Even in areas where entry was permitted, pastoralists needed permits specifying entry and departure times and limited days. They could not stay longer even if pasture was ample. Overstaying meant fines.

Thirdly, British officials were suspicious of nomadic people like craftsmen, traders, and pastoralists who moved seasonally. They preferred a settled population that was easy to identify and control for administration and taxation. Settled people were seen as peaceable and law-abiding, while nomads were considered criminal. In 1871, the **Criminal Tribes Act** classified many communities of craftsmen, traders, and pastoralists as 'Criminal Tribes' by nature and birth. These communities were forced to live in notified village settlements, forbidden to leave without a permit, and kept under constant police watch.

Fourthly, to increase revenue, the colonial government imposed taxes on land, water, salt, trade goods, and animals. **Grazing tax** was introduced in most pastoral tracts in the mid-nineteenth century, increasing rapidly. Initially, collection rights were auctioned to contractors who extracted high taxes. By the 1880s, the government collected taxes directly, issuing passes that recorded the number of cattle heads and tax paid for entry into grazing tracts.

## Source D

In the 1920s, a Royal Commission on Agriculture reported:
'The extent of the area available for grazing has gone down tremendously with the extension of area under cultivation because of increasing population, extension of irrigation facilities, acquiring the pastures for Government purposes, for example, defence, industries and agricultural experimental farms. [Now] breeders find it difficult to raise large herds. Thus their earnings have gone down. The quality of their livestock has deteriorated, dietary standards have fallen and indebtedness has increased.'
The Report of the Royal Commission of Agriculture in India, 1928.

### How Did these Changes Affect the Lives of Pastoralists?

These measures caused a serious shortage of pastures. Taking over grazing lands for cultivation and reserving forests reduced available pastureland. The remaining land was continuously and intensively grazed. Traditionally, nomadic movements allowed pastures to recover. Restrictions meant continuous use, declining pasture quality, and forage shortages, leading to large numbers of animal deaths during scarcities and famines.
