This section explores the lives and seasonal movements of pastoral communities inhabiting the plateaus, plains, and deserts of India, highlighting their adaptations to different environments and their interactions with settled agricultural communities.

# On the Plateaus, Plains and Deserts

Pastoralists also live in the plateaus, plains, and deserts of India, not just the mountains.

**Dhangars** are a significant pastoral community in Maharashtra, primarily shepherds, blanket weavers, and buffalo herders.

*   **Monsoon:** They stay in the central plateau of Maharashtra, a semi-arid region with thorny scrub, which becomes a vast grazing ground.
*   **October:** After harvesting *bajra*, they move west to the Konkan, a fertile agricultural area.
*   **Konkan Stay:** They are welcomed by Konkani peasants. Their flocks manure the fields and feed on stubble after the *kharif* harvest. Peasants provide rice in return.
*   **Return:** With the monsoon onset, they leave the Konkan and coastal areas, returning to the dry plateau, as sheep cannot tolerate wet monsoon conditions.

### New words

*   **Kharif:** The autumn crop, usually harvested between September and October.
*   **Rabi:** The spring crop, usually harvested after March.
*   **Stubble:** Lower ends of grain stalks left in the ground after harvesting.

In Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh, the dry central plateau, covered with stone and grass, is home to cattle, goat, and sheep herders like the **Gollas** (cattle herders) and the **Kurumas and Kurubas** (sheep and goat rearers, blanket sellers). They live near woods, cultivate small plots, engage in petty trades, and care for herds. Their movement rhythms are defined by the monsoon and dry seasons, not cold and snow.

*   **Dry Season:** They move to coastal tracts.
*   **Monsoon:** They leave coastal areas for the dry plateau, as only buffaloes tolerate the wet coastal conditions.

**Banjaras** are another well-known group of graziers found in Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, and Maharashtra. They travel long distances seeking good pasture, selling plough cattle and other goods to villagers in exchange for grain and fodder.

## Source B

Buchanan's account of the Gollas in Mysore (early nineteenth century):
'Their families live in small villages near the skirt of the woods, where they cultivate a little ground, and keep some of their cattle, selling in the towns the produce of the dairy. Their families are very numerous, seven to eight young men in each being common. Two or three of these attend the flocks in the woods, while the remainder cultivate their fields, and supply the towns with firewood, and with straw for thatch.'
From: Francis Hamilton Buchanan, A Journey from Madras through the Countries of Mysore, Canara and Malabar (London, 1807).

## Activity

Read Sources A and B.
> Write briefly about what they tell you about the nature of the work undertaken by men and women in pastoral households.
> **Answer:** Source A suggests that Gujjar men are primarily responsible for grazing cattle in the woods, often staying out for weeks, while women handle the sale of dairy products in markets daily. Source B indicates that among the Gollas, some young men attend the flocks in the woods, while others cultivate fields and supply goods like firewood and thatch to towns. Both sources imply a division of labor, with men often focused on herding and related tasks in grazing areas, and women involved in processing and selling produce, though men also contribute to cultivation and trade.

> Why do you think pastoral groups often live on the edges of forests?
> **Answer:** Pastoral groups often live on the edges of forests because these areas provide essential resources for their lifestyle. Forests and their fringes offer grazing land for their animals, especially during certain seasons. Living near forests also allows access to other resources like firewood and materials for shelter, as mentioned in Source B regarding the Gollas supplying firewood and straw. It provides a balance between access to grazing areas (forests/woods) and proximity to settled areas for trade and cultivation (villages/towns).

In the deserts of Rajasthan live the **Raikas**. Due to meagre and uncertain rainfall, cultivation is difficult or impossible over large stretches. Raikas combine cultivation with pastoralism.

*   **Monsoon:** Raikas of Barmer, Jaisalmer, Jodhpur, and Bikaner stay in home villages where pasture is available.
*   **October:** As grazing grounds become dry, they move out seeking other pasture and water.
*   **Return:** They return during the next monsoon.

One group, the **Maru (desert) Raikas**, herd camels, while another rears sheep and goats.
