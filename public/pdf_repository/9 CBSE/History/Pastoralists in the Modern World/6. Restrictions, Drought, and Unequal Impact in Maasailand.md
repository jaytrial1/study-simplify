This section continues the focus on African pastoralism, specifically the Maasai, detailing how colonial borders and restrictions on movement exacerbated the effects of drought and led to growing social inequalities within the community.

# The Borders are Closed

In the nineteenth century, African pastoralists moved freely over vast areas for pasture. Colonial governments imposed restrictions from the late nineteenth century. Like the Maasai, other groups were confined to special reserves with fixed boundaries. They needed permits to move stock, which were difficult to obtain without trouble. Disobeying rules led to severe punishment.

Pastoralists were also barred from white markets and often prohibited from trade. White settlers saw them as dangerous and savage, minimizing contact. However, contact was necessary for black labour in mines, roads, and towns. New boundaries and restrictions severely impacted both pastoral and trading activities. Trading continued but faced restrictions.

## When Pastures Dry

Drought affects pastoralists universally. When rains fail and pastures dry, cattle starve unless moved to areas with forage. This is why pastoralists are traditionally nomadic – movement helps them survive bad times.

From the colonial period, the Maasai were confined to a fixed reserve, cut off from best grazing lands and forced into a semi-arid tract prone to drought. Unable to move cattle to available pastures, large numbers died of starvation and disease during droughts. A 1930 enquiry showed the Maasai in Kenya had 720,000 cattle, 820,000 sheep, and 171,000 donkeys. In just two severe drought years (1933, 1934), over half the cattle in the Maasai Reserve died. As grazing lands shrank, drought effects intensified, leading to a steady decline in animal stock.

## Not All were Equally Affected

Not all Maasai were equally affected by colonial changes. Pre-colonial Maasai society had two social categories: elders and warriors.

*   **Elders:** The ruling group, meeting in councils to decide community affairs and settle disputes.
*   **Warriors:** Younger people mainly responsible for tribe protection, defense, and cattle raids. Raiding was crucial in a society valuing cattle as wealth. Young men became warriors by proving manliness through raiding other groups' cattle and participating in wars. They were subject to elders' authority.

The British administered Maasai affairs by appointing chiefs for sub-groups, responsible for tribal matters. They restricted raiding and warfare, adversely affecting the traditional authority of both elders and warriors.

Chiefs appointed by the colonial government often accumulated wealth, buying animals, goods, and land with their regular income. They lent money to poor neighbours for taxes and sometimes lived in towns, engaging in trade, while wives and children managed animals in villages. These chiefs survived war and drought better, having both pastoral and non-pastoral income to buy animals when stock was depleted.

Poor pastoralists, dependent solely on livestock, faced a different history. They lacked resources to overcome bad times, losing nearly everything during war and famine. They sought work in towns as charcoal burners, odd-jobbers, or in road/building construction.

Social changes in Maasai society occurred on two levels:
1.  The traditional age-based difference between elders and warriors was disturbed but not entirely broken down.
2.  A new distinction developed between wealthy and poor pastoralists.
