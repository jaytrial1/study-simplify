This section details the process of westward expansion by white settlers in the USA, the displacement of Native American populations, and the clearing of land for cultivation, leading to the emergence of the Great Plains as a major wheat-producing area.

## The Westward Move and Wheat Cultivation

The story of agrarian expansion is closely linked to the westward movement of white settlers who took over the land. After the American War of Independence (1775-1783) and the formation of the USA, white Americans began moving westward. By 1800, when Thomas Jefferson became President, over 700,000 white settlers had moved onto the Appalachian plateau through mountain passes. From the east coast, America seemed a land of opportunity. Its wilderness could become cultivated fields. Forests could provide timber for export, animals could be hunted for skins, and mountains mined for gold and minerals. However, this required clearing American Indians from the land. In the decades after 1800, the US government pursued a policy of driving American Indians westward, first beyond the Mississippi River, then further west. Numerous wars were fought, resulting in massacres of Indians and burning of villages. Indians resisted and won many battles but were ultimately forced to sign treaties, surrender their land, and move westward.

As the Indians retreated, settlers poured in waves. They settled on the Appalachian plateau by the first decade of the eighteenth century and moved into the Mississippi valley between 1820 and 1850. They slashed and burned forests, removed stumps, cleared land, and built log cabins. They then cleared larger areas, fenced fields, ploughed, and sowed corn and wheat.

In the early years, fertile soil yielded good crops. When the soil in one area became poor, migrants moved further west to find new land and raise crops. It was only after the 1860s that settlers moved into the Great Plains across the Mississippi River. In subsequent decades, this region became a major wheat-producing area.
