This section introduces the development of modern agriculture in the USA, contrasting it with the English experience and setting the stage for the westward expansion and its consequences.

# Bread Basket and Dust Bowl

Now, let us travel across the Atlantic to the USA. We will examine how modern agriculture developed there, how the USA became the "bread basket" of the world, and what this meant for rural Americans.

When common fields were being enclosed in England at the end of the eighteenth century, settled agriculture had not extensively developed in the USA. Forests covered over 800 million acres and grasslands 600 million acres.

Most of the landscape was not controlled by white Americans. Until the 1780s, white American settlements were limited to a narrow strip of coastal land in the east. Traveling through the country then, you would have encountered various Native American groups. Some were nomadic, some settled. Many subsisted solely by hunting, gathering, and fishing; others cultivated crops like corn, beans, tobacco, and pumpkin. Still others were expert trappers, through whom European traders obtained beaver fur since the sixteenth century.

By the early twentieth century, this landscape had radically transformed. White Americans had moved westward, gaining control up to the west coast, displacing local tribes, and dividing the landscape into different agricultural belts. The USA came to dominate the world market in agricultural produce. How did this happen? Who were the new settlers? How did the spread of cultivation affect the lives of the Indian groups who once lived there?
