This section details the changes in English agriculture starting in the late eighteenth century, focusing on the transition from open fields and commons to enclosed lands, and the social unrest this caused, exemplified by the Captain Swing riots.

# The Coming of Modern Agriculture in England

On June 1, 1830, a farmer in northwest England found his barn and haystack burned down at night. Similar fires were reported in many districts in the following months. Sometimes only the haystack burned, at other times the entire farmhouse. On the night of August 28, 1830, labourers in East Kent, England, destroyed a farmer's threshing machine. Over the next two years, riots spread across southern England, resulting in the destruction of about 387 threshing machines. Farmers received threatening letters urging them to stop using machines that took away workers' jobs. Most letters were signed 'Captain Swing'. Alarmed landlords feared night attacks by armed groups, and many destroyed their own machines. The government responded severely: 1,976 suspected rioters were tried, nine were hanged, 505 were transported (over 450 to Australia), and 644 were imprisoned.

Captain Swing was a fictional name used in these letters. Who were the Swing rioters? Why did they break threshing machines? What were they protesting? To answer these questions, we need to examine developments in English agriculture during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

## The Time of Open Fields and Commons

Over the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, the English countryside changed dramatically. Before this period, large parts of the English countryside were open, not divided into privately owned, enclosed lands. Peasants cultivated strips of land near their village. Annually, at a public meeting, each villager was allocated strips to cultivate. These strips varied in quality and location, ensuring everyone had a mix of good and bad land. Beyond these cultivated strips lay the common land. All villagers had access to the commons. Here they pastured cows and grazed sheep, collected fuelwood, berries, and fruit. They fished in rivers and ponds and hunted rabbits in common forests. For the poor, common land was vital for survival. It supplemented their small income, supported their cattle, and helped them survive when crops failed.

In some parts of England, the open field and common land system began changing around the sixteenth century. When wool prices rose in the world market in the sixteenth century, rich farmers wanted to expand wool production for profit. They aimed to improve sheep breeds and ensure good feed. They sought control of large, compact land areas for better breeding. So, they started dividing and enclosing common land, building hedges to separate their property. They drove out villagers with small cottages on the commons and prevented the poor from entering enclosed fields.

Until the mid-eighteenth century, the enclosure movement was slow. Early enclosures were often by individual landlords and not supported by the state or church. After the mid-eighteenth century, however, the movement rapidly changed the English landscape forever. Between 1750 and 1850, 6 million acres were enclosed. The British Parliament actively supported this by passing 4,000 Acts legalizing these enclosures.

### Key Concepts & Examples
*   **Open Field System:** A traditional agricultural system where villagers cultivated scattered strips of land and shared access to common lands for grazing, collecting resources, etc.
*   **Common Land:** Land accessible to all villagers, essential for the survival of the poor.
*   **Enclosure:** The process of fencing off common land and open fields, turning them into private property. Early enclosures in the 16th century were often for sheep farming; later enclosures were primarily for grain production.
