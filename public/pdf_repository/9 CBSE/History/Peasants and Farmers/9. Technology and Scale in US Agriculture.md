This section examines the role of new technology in enabling the massive expansion of wheat cultivation in the Great Plains, highlighting the transition from traditional tools to mechanical reapers and combined harvesters.

## The Coming of New Technology

This dramatic expansion was possible due to new technology. As settlers moved into new habitats and lands throughout the nineteenth century, they adapted their tools. In the mid-western prairie, traditional ploughs from the east were ineffective against the thick, tough-rooted grass sod. New ploughs were designed locally, some 12 feet long, with small front wheels, pulled by six yokes of oxen or horses, to break the sod and turn the soil. By the early twentieth century, farmers in the Great Plains used tractors and disk ploughs to clear vast stretches for wheat.

Once ripened, the crop needed harvesting. Before the 1830s, grain was harvested with a cradle or sickle, requiring hundreds of men and women in the fields. In 1831, Cyrus McCormick invented the first **mechanical reaper**, which could cut as much grain in one day as five men with cradles or 16 men with sickles. By the early twentieth century, most farmers used **combined harvesters**. With one of these machines, 500 acres of wheat could be harvested in two weeks.

These machines were very attractive to the big farmers of the Great Plains. Wheat prices were high, and demand seemed unlimited. The new machines allowed these large farmers to quickly clear vast areas, break the soil, remove grass, and prepare for cultivation. Work could be done rapidly with minimal labour. With power-driven machinery, four men could plough, seed, and harvest 2,000 to 4,000 acres of wheat in a season.
