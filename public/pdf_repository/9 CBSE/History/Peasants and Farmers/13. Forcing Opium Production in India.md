This section explains how the British East India Company sought to increase opium production in India to fuel the trade with China, detailing the methods used to compel unwilling cultivators.

## Where did Opium come from?

This is where Indian peasants enter the story. When the British conquered Bengal, they actively worked to produce opium in their controlled lands. As the market for opium expanded in China, more opium flowed from Bengal ports. Before 1767, under 500 chests (each about two maunds) were exported from India. Within four years, this tripled. A century later, in 1870, the government exported about 50,000 chests annually.

Supplies needed to increase for this booming export trade, but it was difficult. How could cultivators be convinced to grow poppy? For various reasons, they were unwilling to devote their fields to poppy. First, the crop required the best land, near villages and well-manured, where peasants usually grew pulses. Growing opium meant pulses couldn't be grown there or had to be grown on inferior land with poorer, uncertain harvests. Second, many cultivators owned no land and had to lease land from landlords, paying high rent for good village-adjacent land. Third, opium cultivation was difficult; the plant was delicate and required long hours of nurturing, leaving little time for other crops. Finally, the price the government paid cultivators for opium was very low, making it unprofitable for them to grow.

## How Were Unwilling Cultivators Made to Produce Opium? I

Unwilling cultivators were forced to produce opium through a system of **advances**. In rural Bengal and Bihar, many poor peasants struggled to survive, lacking enough to pay rent or buy food and clothing. From the 1780s, these peasants were offered money advances by their village headmen (**mabato**). Tempted by the loan, cultivators accepted, hoping to meet immediate needs and repay later. But the loan tied the peasant to the headman and, through him, to the government. Government opium agents provided the advances to headmen, who passed them to cultivators. Taking the loan forced the cultivator to grow opium on a specific land area and sell the entire harvest only to the agents at the low price offered. They could not plant other crops on that land or sell to anyone else.

## Activity

Imagine that you are the leader of a group of farmers protesting against having to grow opium. You have been granted a meeting with the local official of the East India Company. How would the conversation proceed? Divide the class into the two groups and act out the conversation you would have.
> **Answer:** The conversation between the protesting farmers' leader and the Company official might proceed like this:
> **Farmer Leader:** Sir, we, the farmers of this region, have come to plead with you. The advances you give us are not enough. The price you pay for opium is too low!
> **Company Official:** The Company sets the price. The advances help you when you are in need. You agreed to grow the poppy.
> **Farmer Leader:** But the poppy takes our best land, land where we used to grow pulses to feed our families. The rent for this good land is high. Growing poppy takes all our time; we cannot properly care for other crops. We suffer loss from growing poppy.
> **Company Official:** The Company needs the opium. It is for trade. You took the advance; you are bound to cultivate the specified area and deliver the produce to our agents. You cannot sell to anyone else.
> **Farmer Leader:** But local traders offer much higher prices! Why can we not sell to them? Why are we beaten by your chuprassies if we complain or refuse? This cultivation feels like a curse!
> **Company Official:** The Company has a monopoly. No one else is allowed to trade in opium. Any other trade is illegal. You must follow the Company's rules. The agents ensure the contracts are fulfilled.
> **Farmer Leader:** But the laws and prices are unfair! They cause us great hardship and debt. We are asking for a fair price so we can survive and feed our families without losing our land or being forced into debt!
> **Company Official:** The Company cannot increase the price significantly. Our profit depends on buying cheap and selling high. You must fulfill your agreement.

The problem could have been partly solved by raising the opium price, but the government was reluctant. It wanted to produce opium cheaply and sell it expensively to agents in Calcutta, who shipped it to China. The difference was the government's opium revenue. Prices for peasants were so low that by the early eighteenth century, angry peasants agitated for higher prices and refused advances. In areas around Benaras, cultivators quit opium, growing sugarcane and potatoes instead. Many sold crops to traveling traders (**pykars**) offering better prices.

By 1773, the British government in Bengal had a **monopoly** on opium trade. No one else was legally permitted to trade it. By the 1820s, the British were alarmed that opium production in their territories was rapidly declining, while increasing outside British control in Central India and Rajasthan, within princely states. In these regions, local traders offered peasants higher prices and exported opium to China. Armed bands of traders were even found conducting this trade in the 1820s. The British considered this trade illegal smuggling and sought to stop it, wanting to keep their monopoly. They instructed agents in princely states to confiscate all opium and destroy crops.

This conflict between the British government, peasants, and local traders continued as long as opium production lasted.

However, not all peasants in colonial India shared the same experiences as opium cultivators. Other experiences will be covered in a later chapter.
