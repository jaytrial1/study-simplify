This section explains the environmental disaster known as the Dust Bowl in the 1930s, linking it to unsustainable agricultural practices and the consequences for the land and its inhabitants.

## Dust Bowl

The expansion of wheat agriculture in the Great Plains created other problems. In the 1930s, terrifying duststorms, called **black blizzards**, began blowing over the southern plains, often reaching 7,000 to 8,000 feet high, like monstrous waves. They occurred day after day, year after year, through the 1930s.

As the skies darkened and dust swept in, people were blinded and choked. Cattle suffocated, their lungs caked with dust and mud. Sand buried fences, covered fields, and coated rivers, killing fish. Dead birds and animals were strewn across the landscape. Tractors and machines used in the 1920s were clogged with dust, damaged beyond repair.

What went wrong? Why these duststorms? Partly, the early 1930s saw persistent drought, failed rains, soaring temperatures, and fierce winds. But ordinary duststorms became black blizzards because the entire landscape had been ploughed over, stripped of the grass that held the soil together. During the dramatic expansion of wheat cultivation in the early twentieth century, enthusiastic farmers recklessly uprooted all vegetation, and tractors turned the soil, breaking the sod into dust. The whole region became a **dust bowl**. The American dream of a land of plenty turned into a nightmare. Settlers had believed they could conquer the landscape and turn all land to profitable crops. After the 1930s, they realized they had to respect each region's ecological conditions.
