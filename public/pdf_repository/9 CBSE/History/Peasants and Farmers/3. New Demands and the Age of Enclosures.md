This section explains the reasons behind the acceleration of the enclosure movement in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, linking it to population growth, industrialization, war, and market demands for grain.

## New Demands for Grain

Why such intense enclosure activity? What did it mean? The new enclosures differed from the old. Unlike the sixteenth-century enclosures for sheep farming, land enclosed in the late eighteenth century was for grain production. These new enclosures occurred in a changing context. From the mid-eighteenth century, England's population grew rapidly. Between 1750 and 1900, it quadrupled, from 7 million in 1750 to 21 million in 1850 and 30 million in 1900. This increased demand for foodgrains. Additionally, Britain was industrializing, with more people living and working in urban areas. Rural men migrated to towns for jobs and had to buy foodgrains. As urban populations grew, the market for foodgrains expanded, and rapidly increasing demand caused prices to rise.

By the end of the eighteenth century, France was at war with England. This disrupted trade and foodgrain imports from Europe. Foodgrain prices in England soared, encouraging landowners to enclose land and expand grain cultivation. Profits increased, and landowners pressured Parliament to pass the Enclosure Acts.

## The Age of Enclosures

One significant difference in the period after the 1780s compared to earlier times in English history is that rapid population growth was not followed by widespread food shortages. In the nineteenth century, grain production in England kept pace with the population increase. By 1868, even with a rapidly growing population, England produced about 80% of the food it consumed, with the rest imported.

This increase in foodgrain production was achieved not primarily through radical technological innovations, but by bringing *new lands* under cultivation. Landlords enclosed pasturelands, open fields, forest commons, and marshes, converting larger areas into agricultural fields.

Farmers used simple agricultural innovations common since the early eighteenth century. Around the 1660s, farmers in many parts of England began growing **turnip** and **clover**. They discovered these crops improved soil fertility. Turnip also served as good fodder for cattle. These crops became part of the regular cropping system. Later findings showed they increased soil nitrogen, essential for crop growth. Cultivating the same soil depleted nitrogen, reducing fertility. By restoring nitrogen, turnip and clover made the soil fertile again. Farmers in the early nineteenth century used these methods more regularly to improve agriculture.

Enclosures were seen as necessary for long-term investments in land and planning crop rotations to improve the soil. Enclosures also allowed richer landowners to expand their land control and produce more for the market.
