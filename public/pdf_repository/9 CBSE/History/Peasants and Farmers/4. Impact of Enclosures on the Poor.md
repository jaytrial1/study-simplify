This section focuses on the negative consequences of the enclosure movement for the poorer sections of rural society, detailing the loss of common rights, displacement, and increased job insecurity.

## What Happened To the Poor?

Enclosures benefited landlords financially. But what happened to those who relied on the commons? When fences were erected, the enclosed land became the exclusive property of one owner. The poor could no longer collect firewood, graze cattle on the commons, gather apples and berries, or hunt small animals. They couldn't even gather leftover stalks after harvest. Everything belonged to the landlords and had a price the poor couldn't afford.

In areas with extensive enclosures, particularly the Midlands and surrounding counties, the poor were displaced from the land. Their traditional rights gradually vanished. Deprived of rights and driven off the land, they wandered in search of work. From the Midlands, some moved to the southern counties, which were intensively cultivated and had demand for agricultural labourers. However, secure jobs were scarce.

Previously, labourers often lived with landowners, eating at their table and helping with various tasks year-round. By 1800, this practice was disappearing. Labourers were paid wages and employed mainly during harvest. As landowners sought more profit, they reduced spending on workmen. Work became insecure, employment uncertain, and income unstable. For much of the year, the poor were unemployed.

## Activity

What happened to the women and children?
Cow keeping, collection of firewood, gleaning, gathering of fruits and berries from the common lands was earlier mostly done by women and children.

Can you suggest how enclosures must have affected the lives of women and children? Can you imagine how the disappearance of common lands might have changed the relationship between men, women and children within the family?
> **Answer:** Enclosures severely affected women and children who traditionally relied on common lands for vital resources. They lost access to firewood, grazing areas for animals they might tend, and sources of food like berries and gleanings. This loss would have made their lives much harder, reducing their contribution to the family's subsistence and potentially increasing their dependence on wage-earning men. The disappearance of common lands likely strained family relationships by removing shared activities and resources, altering traditional gender roles related to resource gathering, and intensifying the economic pressure on the primary wage earner (often the man).
