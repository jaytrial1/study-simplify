This section describes the introduction of threshing machines, the economic context after the Napoleonic Wars, and how these machines became a target during the Captain Swing riots, symbolizing the hardships faced by agricultural laborers.

## The Introduction of Threshing Machines

During the Napoleonic Wars, high foodgrain prices encouraged farmers to expand production vigorously. Fearing labour shortages, they began buying new threshing machines. They complained about labourers' perceived insolence, drinking habits, and difficulty in making them work, believing machines would reduce dependence on them.

After the Napoleonic Wars, thousands of soldiers returned to villages needing jobs. However, grain imports from Europe resumed, prices fell, and an Agricultural Depression began (as seen in Fig. 2). Anxious landowners reduced cultivation area and demanded import stops. They tried to cut wages and the number of workers. The unemployed poor wandered between villages, and those with uncertain jobs feared losing their livelihood.

The Captain Swing riots spread through the countryside during this period. For the poor, threshing machines symbolized these bad times.

## Conclusion

The advent of modern agriculture in England brought many changes. Open fields disappeared, undermining peasants' customary rights. Richer farmers expanded grain production, sold it globally, profited, and gained power. The poor left villages in large numbers, some moving to southern counties for jobs, others to cities. Labourers' incomes became unstable, jobs insecure, and livelihoods precarious.

### Source C

One peasant who lost his rights to common land after the enclosures wrote to the local lord:
'Should a poor man take one of your sheep from the common, his life would be forfeited by law. But should You take the common from a hundred poor men's sheep, the law gives no redress. The poor man is liable to be hung for taking from You what would not supply you with a meal; & You would do nothing illegal by depriving him of his subsistence; ...What should be the inference of the poor...when the laws are not accessible to the injured poor and the government gives them no redress?'

### Source D

In contrast many writers emphasised the advantages of enclosures.
'There can be no question of the superior profit to the farmer of enclosures rather than open fields. In one case he is in chains; he can make no changes in soil or prices, he is like a horse in team, he must jog along with the rest.'
John Middleton, an 18th century writer.
