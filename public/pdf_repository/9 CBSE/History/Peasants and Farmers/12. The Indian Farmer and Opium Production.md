# The Indian Farmer and Opium Production

Let us now move to India and see what was happening in the Indian countryside in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries.

As you know, British rule was gradually established in India after the Battle of Plassey (1757). Over the period of colonial rule, the rural landscape was radically transformed. The British viewed land revenue as a major source of government income. To increase state resources, they implemented a regular land revenue system, raised rates, and expanded cultivated area. As cultivation grew, forest and pasture land declined. This created many problems for peasants and pastoralists. Their access to forests and grazing lands became increasingly restricted by rules. They also struggled to meet the government's revenue demands.

During the colonial period, rural India also produced various crops for the world market. In the early nineteenth century, indigo and opium were major commercial crops. By the century's end, peasants produced sugarcane, cotton, jute, wheat, and other crops for export, feeding urban Europe and supplying mills in England.

How did Indian cultivators respond to entering the modern world of international commerce and trade? Let's examine the history of opium to understand what colonial rule meant for peasants and how the market operated in the colonies.

## A Taste for Tea: The Trade with China

The history of opium production in India is linked to British trade with China. In the late eighteenth century, the English East India Company bought tea and silk from China to sell in England. As tea became popular in England, the tea trade grew. In 1785, about 15 million pounds of tea were imported; by 1830, this jumped to over 30 million pounds. The East India Company's profits came to depend on the tea trade.

This created a problem: England produced nothing easily saleable in China. China's rulers, the Manchu (Qing) dynasty, were suspicious of foreign merchants, fearing they would meddle in politics and disrupt authority. So, they were reluctant to allow foreign goods entry.

How could Western merchants finance the tea trade? How could they balance trade? They could only buy tea with silver coins or bullion. This outflow of treasure from England caused widespread anxiety, as it was believed to impoverish the nation. Merchants sought a commodity to sell in China, something the Chinese would buy.

Opium was this commodity. The Portuguese introduced opium to China in the early sixteenth century. It was mainly known for medicinal uses and used in small quantities. The Chinese were aware of opium addiction dangers, and the Emperor forbade its production and sale except for medicine. But Western merchants began an illegal trade in opium in the mid-eighteenth century. It was unloaded at southeastern Chinese ports and carried inland by local agents. By the early 1820s, about 10,000 crates were smuggled annually. Fifteen years later, over 35,000 crates were unloaded yearly.

While the English developed a taste for Chinese tea, the Chinese became addicted to opium. People of all classes, from shopkeepers to aristocrats, used the drug. Lin Ze-xu, Special Commissioner at Canton in 1839, estimated over 4 million opium smokers in China. A British doctor in Canton put the figure at 12 million. As China became a country of opium addicts, British trade in tea flourished, financed by returns from opium sales in China.

### Key Concepts & Examples
*   **Triangular Trade:** A trade system where goods flow between three points. In this case, British traders took opium from India to China, tea from China to England, and manufactured goods from England to India, while raw materials and foodgrains flowed from India to England.
