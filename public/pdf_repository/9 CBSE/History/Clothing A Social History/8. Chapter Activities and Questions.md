# Activities and Questions

## Activities

1.  Imagine you are the 14-year-old child of a trader. Write a paragraph on what you feel about the sumptuary laws in France.
    > **Answer:** As a 14-year-old child of a trader in France under sumptuary laws, I would likely feel frustrated and perhaps resentful. Seeing wealthier classes wear fine silks, velvets, and furs while my family is restricted to simpler, cheaper materials would highlight the unfairness of the social system. Even if my family could afford better clothes, the law prevents us from wearing them, constantly reminding us of our lower social status. It would feel like a constant barrier, limiting our ability to express ourselves or improve our appearance, simply because of who we are born as.

2.  Can you think of any expectations of proper and improper dress which exist today? Give examples of two forms of clothing which would be considered disrespectful in certain places but acceptable in others.
    > **Answer:** Yes, expectations of proper and improper dress still exist today, often tied to specific contexts like workplaces, religious sites, or formal events.
    >
    > 1.  **Swimwear:** Considered perfectly acceptable at a beach or swimming pool, but would be highly inappropriate and disrespectful in a formal office meeting or a place of worship like a temple or church.
    > 2.  **Shorts and Tank Tops:** Common and acceptable casual wear for everyday activities like shopping or meeting friends in a relaxed setting, but would be considered disrespectful or improper attire for attending a formal wedding ceremony or a court hearing.

## Questions

1.  Explain the reasons for the changes in clothing patterns and materials in the eighteenth century.
    > **Answer:** Changes in clothing patterns and materials in the eighteenth century were driven by factors like the rise of democratic ideals (challenging class-based sumptuary laws, leading to simpler styles), the growth of capitalist markets (increasing availability and variety of materials), and the beginnings of industrialization (leading to mass production later on). Colonization also played a role by introducing new materials like Indian chintz to Europe.

2.  What were the sumptuary laws in France?
    > **Answer:** Sumptuary laws in France were laws that regulated what people could wear based on their social rank and income. They dictated specific materials (like ermine, silk, velvet) and quantities of clothing that were restricted to the aristocracy, preventing lower classes from wearing clothes associated with higher status. These laws aimed to maintain social hierarchy and control the consumption of luxury goods.

3.  Give any two examples of the ways in which European dress codes were different from Indian dress codes.
    > **Answer:** Two examples of differences between European and Indian dress codes discussed in the chapter are:
    > 1.  **Headwear Etiquette:** In the Western tradition, removing one's hat before a social superior was a sign of respect. In India, the turban was a symbol of respectability and identity that was not removed at will, leading to misunderstandings with the British.
    > 2.  **Footwear Etiquette:** In India, removing shoes before entering a sacred place or home was customary due to concerns about cleanliness and pollution, especially since people sat on the ground. In European public spaces like courtrooms, keeping shoes on was the norm, leading to conflict when British officials insisted Indians remove theirs.

4.  In 1805, a British official, Benjamin Heyne, listed the manufactures of Bangalore which included the following:
    > Women's cloth of different musters and names
    > Coarse chintz
    > Muslims
    > Silk cloths
    > Of this list, which kind of cloth would have definitely fallen out of use in the early 1900s and why?
    > **Answer:** "Coarse chintz" would have likely fallen out of extensive use in the early 1900s, particularly for export to Europe. While chintz was popular after 1600 due to trade, the Industrial Revolution in Britain led to the mass manufacture of cotton textiles. Cheap British mill-made cloth flooded the market, making locally produced, coarser Indian chintz less competitive and causing its demand to fall, especially for export purposes.

5.  Suggest reasons why women in nineteenth century India were obliged to continue wearing traditional Indian dress even when men switched over to the more convenient Western clothing. What does this show about the position of women in society?
    > **Answer:** Women in 19th-century India were often obliged to continue wearing traditional dress while men adopted Western styles for several reasons:
    > 1.  **Social Expectations and Norms:** Traditional dress for women was deeply tied to cultural identity, modesty, and community norms. Changing women's dress was often seen as a greater threat to tradition and cultural identity than changing men's dress.
    > 2.  **Limited Public Roles:** Men were more likely to interact with the colonial administration, work in professions influenced by the British, or travel, making Western clothing more practical or necessary. Women's roles were often more confined to the home and traditional social spheres, where traditional dress remained suitable.
    > 3.  **Resistance to Change:** As seen in Source F, there could be strong resistance from family elders to women adopting new clothing styles, viewing them as improper or indicative of undesirable social changes.
    > 4.  **Cost and Practicality:** While Western men's clothing became more convenient, traditional Indian women's clothing might have been more practical or affordable for their daily lives, especially for those not in elite circles.
    >
    > This situation shows that women in society often faced greater pressure to uphold tradition and were less free to adopt new practices compared to men. Their clothing was seen as a marker of family and community honour, placing greater restrictions on their personal choices and reflecting a subordinate position where their attire was subject to stricter social control.

6.  Winston Churchill described Mahatma Gandhi as a 'seditious Middle Temple Lawyer' now 'posing as a half naked fakir'.
    > What provoked such a comment and what does it tell you about the symbolic strength of Mahatma Gandhi's dress?
    > **Answer:** This comment was provoked by Gandhi's deliberate adoption of the simple dress of the poorest Indian peasants – a short dhoti and sometimes a chaddar – after having previously dressed in Western suits as a lawyer. Churchill's remark highlights the stark contrast between Gandhi's educated, professional background and his chosen attire. It was intended to be dismissive and contemptuous, portraying Gandhi as a deceptive agitator ("seditious lawyer") pretending to be a poor ascetic ("half naked fakir").
    >
    > The comment tells us about the immense symbolic strength of Gandhi's dress. By shedding Western clothes and elite Indian attire for the clothing of the poor, Gandhi made a powerful political statement. It symbolized his identification with the masses, his rejection of Western influence and colonial rule, and his commitment to Indian self-reliance (khadi). The fact that a prominent figure like Churchill felt compelled to comment on Gandhi's clothing demonstrates how effectively Gandhi used his dress as a visual symbol of defiance, nationalism, and solidarity with the poor, making it a potent tool in the independence movement.

7.  Why did Mahatma Gandhi's dream of clothing the nation in khadi appeal only to some sections of Indians?
    > **Answer:** Mahatma Gandhi's dream of clothing the nation in khadi appealed mainly to certain sections of Indians, particularly upper castes and classes involved in the nationalist movement, but faced challenges among others for several reasons:
    > 1.  **Cost:** Khadi was often more expensive than cheap mill-made cloth, making it unaffordable for poor peasants and laborers who needed economical options.
    > 2.  **Practicality and Comfort:** Khadi was coarser than mill-made cloth, which some found less comfortable.
    > 3.  **Existing Dress Norms:** Traditional regional dress styles were diverse and deeply ingrained, making a single 'national dress' like khadi difficult to adopt universally. Women, in particular, had specific traditional requirements regarding fabric length or style (like the nine-yard sari) that khadi might not easily fulfill or elders resisted changing.
    > 4.  **Social Status and Liberation:** For groups like Dalits, adopting Western-style clothing was a symbol of self-respect and liberation from oppressive caste norms that dictated their traditional dress, making them less inclined to adopt khadi which was associated with a different form of nationalism that didn't directly address their specific struggle for social equality.
    > 5.  **Availability:** Producing enough khadi to clothe the entire nation was a massive challenge, making it difficult to obtain consistently for everyone.
