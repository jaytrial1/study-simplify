### Mahatma Gandhi and Colonial Sport

Mahatma Gandhi acknowledged the importance of sport for physical development but was critical of games like cricket, hockey, football, and tennis. He believed these games, imported by the British, were mainly for the privileged, reflected a colonial mindset, and were less valuable than the physical labour involved in agriculture. He advocated for the revival of inexpensive, traditional Indian games as a more suitable form of physical education and recreation for the masses.

> ## Mahatma Gandhi and colonial sport
>
> Mahatma Gandhi believed that sport was essential for creating a balance between the body and the mind. However, he often emphasised that games like cricket and hockey were imported into India by the British and were replacing traditional games. Such games as cricket, hockey, football and tennis were for the privileged, he believed. They showed a colonial mindset and were a less effective education than the simple exercise of those who worked on the land.

> 'Now let us examine our body. Are we supposed to cultivate the body by playing tennis, football or cricket for an hour every day? It does, certainly, build up the body. Like a wild horse, however, the body will be strong but not trained. A trained body is healthy, vigorous and sinewy. The hands and feet can do any desired work. A pickaxe, a shovel, a hammer, etc. are like ornaments to a trained hand and it can wield them ... A well-trained body does not get tired in trudging 30 miles .... Does the student acquire such physical culture? We can assert that modern curricula do not impart physical education in this sense.'
> 'What Is Education', 26 February 1926, The Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi, Vol. 34.
> 'I should, however, be exceedingly surprised and even painfully surprised, if I were told that before cricket and football descended upon your sacred soil, your boys were devoid of all games. If you have national games, I would urge upon you that yours is an institution that should lead in reviving old games. I know that we have in India many noble indigenous games just as interesting and exciting as cricket or football, also as much attended with risks as football is, but with the added advantage that they are inexpensive, because the cost is practically next to nothing'
> Speech at Mahindra College, 24 November 1927, The Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi.
> 'A sound body means one which bends itself to the spirit and is always a ready instrument at its service. Such bodies are not made, in my opinion, on the football field. They are made on cornfields and farms. I would urge you to think this over and you will find innumerable illustrations to prove my statement. Our colonial-born Indians are carried away with this football and cricket mania. These games may have their place under certain circumstances .... Why do we not take the simple fact into consideration that the vast majority of mankind who are vigorous in body and mind are simple agriculturists, that they are strangers to these games, and they are the salt of the earth?' Letter to Lazarus, 17 April 1915, The Collected Works of Mahatma Gandhi, Vol. 14.

> **Answer:** Arnold/Hughes (Source A) saw team sports like cricket and rugby in public schools as crucial for moulding boys into disciplined, selfless, hierarchical, and leadership-oriented individuals, fit for building and running the British empire. They believed these games taught valuable virtues like fair play and loyalty. Gandhi (Source C), however, viewed imported games like cricket and football as activities for the privileged, reflecting a colonial mindset. He believed true physical education came from working on the land (agriculture), which produced strong, useful bodies capable of practical labor, and that traditional Indian games, being inexpensive and accessible, were preferable to the expensive, foreign sports.
