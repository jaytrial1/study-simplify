#### Activity Question 1

Imagine a conversation between Thomas Arnold, the headmaster of Rugby School, and Mahatma Gandhi on the value of cricket in education. What would each say? Write out a conversation in the form of a dialogue.

> **Answer:**
> **Arnold:** Mr. Gandhi, I believe games like cricket are vital for shaping young men. On the playing field, boys learn discipline, teamwork, respect for rules and authority, and the spirit of fair play. These are the qualities needed to build character and leadership, essential for serving one's country and, indeed, for governing an empire. It teaches them to sacrifice for the side, not just play for themselves.
>
> **Gandhi:** Mr. Arnold, while discipline is valuable, I question the nature of the games you champion and their relevance to the majority. Cricket, and others like it, are expensive, imported sports, accessible only to a privileged few. They cultivate a certain mindset, perhaps useful for administration or military, but not for the simple, honest labour that sustains a nation. True physical education comes from work on the land, building bodies strong and capable for agriculture and craft. Moreover, these foreign games often replace our own traditional games, which are inexpensive and rooted in our culture, fostering community spirit without the need for elaborate grounds or equipment.
>
> **Arnold:** But surely, the competitive spirit and the lessons in handling victory and defeat, the camaraderie built on the field – these are universal virtues, applicable to any walk of life. Our public schools use these games precisely to instil these values, preparing boys not just for physical prowess, but for moral and social responsibility.
>
> **Gandhi:** Responsibility, yes, but what kind? Is it responsibility towards one's own side in a game, or towards the well-being of all? My concern is that these games, in their very structure and cost, reinforce existing social divisions and promote a mindset disconnected from the realities of ordinary people's lives. A body trained by honest labour on the farm is, in my view, far more valuable and truly disciplined than one merely exercised on a playing field. We need games that unite, not divide, and that are accessible to every villager, not just the wealthy elite in the cities.

#### Question 1

Test cricket is a unique game in many ways. Discuss some of the ways in which it is different from other team games. How are the peculiarities of Test cricket shaped by its historical beginnings as a village game?

> **Answer:** Test cricket is unique because it can last up to five days and still end in a draw, unlike most modern team sports which are strictly time-limited. Also, the pitch length is fixed (22 yards), but the ground size and shape are not, allowing for varying boundaries. These peculiarities are shaped by its village origins. Cricket evolved before the Industrial Revolution, when life rhythms were slower and there were no strict time constraints. It was played on public commons of varying sizes, hence no fixed ground dimensions.

#### Question 2

Describe one way in which in the nineteenth century, technology brought about a change in equipment and give one example where no change in equipment took place.

> **Answer:** Technology brought change in protective equipment: the invention of vulcanised rubber led to the introduction of pads (1848) and gloves. An example where equipment fundamentally remained unchanged is the bat and ball themselves; they are still made from natural, pre-industrial materials like willow wood, cane, leather, twine, and cork, and are largely handmade, resisting the adoption of plastics, fiberglass, or metal seen in other sports.

#### Question 3

Explain why cricket became popular in India and the West Indies. Can you give reasons why it did not become popular in countries in South America?

> **Answer:** Cricket became popular in India and the West Indies primarily because they were British colonies. The British colonial rulers introduced the game, and local elites often adopted it to emulate their masters. In the West Indies, success in cricket also became a symbol of racial equality and political progress during the decolonisation period. Cricket did not become popular in countries in South America because these regions were colonised mainly by Spain and Portugal, not Britain. The sports introduced and popularized there were different, such as football, which became dominant across South America.

#### Question 4

Give brief explanations for the following:

*   The Parsis were the first Indian community to set up a cricket club in India.
*   Mahatma Gandhi condemned the Pentangular tournament.
*   The name of the ICC was changed from the Imperial Cricket Conference to the International Cricket Conference.
*   The shift of the ICC headquarters from London to Dubai

> **Answer:**
> *   **The Parsis were the first Indian community to set up a cricket club in India:** The Parsis were the first Indian community to establish a cricket club because they were brought into close contact with the British through trade and were among the first to adopt Western ways, including British sports.
> *   **Mahatma Gandhi condemned the Pentangular tournament:** Mahatma Gandhi condemned the Pentangular tournament because it was organised on communal and racial lines (teams for Europeans, Parsis, Hindus, Muslims, Rest), which he saw as a divisive competition that went against the nationalist goal of uniting India's diverse population.
> *   **The name of the ICC was changed from the Imperial Cricket Conference to the International Cricket Conference:** The name was changed from Imperial to International Cricket Conference as late as 1965 to reflect the changing global political landscape following decolonisation, as many former British colonies became independent nations.
> *   **The shift of the ICC headquarters from London to Dubai:** The shift of the ICC headquarters from London to Dubai symbolised the change in the game's center of gravity towards South Asia, particularly India, which has the largest market and viewership for cricket globally, driven by the commercialisation and globalisation of the sport.

#### Question 5

How have advances in technology, especially television technology, affected the development of contemporary cricket?

> **Answer:** Television technology, especially satellite TV, transformed contemporary cricket by expanding its audience into small towns and villages, broadening its social base, and creating a global market. It made cricketers celebrities through continuous coverage and commercials, and generated huge revenues through the sale of television rights, shifting the financial power in the game. It also influenced gameplay with innovations like coloured clothing, helmets, field restrictions, and playing under lights, initially introduced to make the game more visually appealing for TV audiences.
