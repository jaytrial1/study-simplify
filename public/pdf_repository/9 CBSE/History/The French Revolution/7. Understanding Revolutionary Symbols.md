# Reading political symbols

The majority of men and women in the eighteenth century could not read or write. So images and symbols were frequently used instead of printed words to communicate important ideas. The painting by Le Barbier (Fig. 8) uses many such symbols to convey the content of the Declaration of Rights. Let us try to read these symbols.

*   **The broken chain:** Chains were used to fetter slaves. A broken chain stands for the act of becoming free.
*   **The bundle of rods or fasces:** One rod can be easily broken, but not an entire bundle. Strength lies in unity.

> **Answer:**
> 1. Symbols standing for liberty, equality, and fraternity:
>    *   Liberty: Broken chain, Red Phrygian cap, The winged woman (personification of law, implying freedom under law).
>    *   Equality: The Law Tablet (law is the same for all, all are equal before it), The bundle of rods or fasces (strength in unity implies collective equality).
>    *   Fraternity: The bundle of rods or fasces (strength in unity implies brotherhood/fraternity).
> 2. The painting (Fig. 8) depicts the Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen. The figure on the right, representing France, and the figure on the left, symbolising Law (a winged woman), stand beside a tablet (Law Tablet) inscribed with the rights. The Eye within the triangle radiating light above suggests that these rights are enlightened or divinely inspired knowledge driving away ignorance. The overall meaning conveyed by the symbols is that the new law, representing France, is based on enlightened principles of rights and equality for all, breaking the chains of the old order.
> 3. The Constitution of 1791 gave political rights (right to vote) only to 'active citizens' (men over 25 who paid significant taxes), while others were 'passive citizens'. Article 1 of the Declaration of Rights states that "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights." Article 6 states, "Law is the expression of the general will. All citizens have the right to participate in its formation, personally or through their representatives. All citizens are equal before it."
>    The two documents are **not consistent**. The Declaration proclaims universal equality in rights and the right of all citizens to participate in law formation, implying equal political rights. However, the Constitution of 1791 explicitly denies voting rights to the majority (passive citizens), creating a distinction based on wealth and gender, which contradicts the universalist claims of the Declaration. The two documents do **not** convey the same idea regarding political participation and equality.
> 4. Groups that gained from the Constitution of 1791:
>    *   The wealthier middle classes (merchants, manufacturers, lawyers, officials) within the Third Estate who qualified as 'active citizens' and could vote or be elected to the Assembly.
>    *   Men over 25 who met the tax criteria for 'active citizens'.
>    Groups forced to relinquish power:
>    *   The King (his powers were limited).
>    *   The nobility and clergy (lost their privileges, feudal dues, and church lands).
>    Sections of society dissatisfied:
>    *   Poor men (classified as passive citizens, denied voting rights).
>    *   Peasants and artisans (many likely passive citizens, denied voting rights).
>    *   Women (classified as passive citizens, denied voting rights).
>    *   Those who believed in universal equality and participation.
>    Marat (Source B) anticipates that because political power and representation have been given to the rich, the situation of the poor will not improve through peaceful means alone. He suggests that the poor and oppressed, having cast off the yoke of the aristocrats, will eventually seek to overthrow the power of the wealthy elite as well.
> 5. Impact on neighbouring absolute monarchies:
>    *   **Kings:** Would likely react with fear and alarm, seeing the French Revolution as a dangerous threat to their own power and the principle of monarchy. They might increase surveillance, suppress dissent, and consider military intervention.
>    *   **Traders:** Those trading with France might face disruption. Some traders in neighbouring countries might be inspired by ideas of economic freedom or changes in trade laws, while others might fear instability.
>    *   **Peasants:** Peasants in neighbouring countries might be inspired by the revolts against feudal dues and the abolition of privileges in France, potentially leading to unrest or demands for similar reforms.
>    *   **Nobles:** Nobles in neighbouring countries would likely be horrified by the loss of privileges and the attacks on the nobility in France. They might rally support for their monarchs and oppose any spread of revolutionary ideas that threatened their status.
>    *   **Members of the clergy:** The clergy in neighbouring countries would likely be alarmed by the confiscation of Church lands and the abolition of tithes in France, fearing similar measures in their own countries. They would likely support their monarchs and condemn the revolution.
