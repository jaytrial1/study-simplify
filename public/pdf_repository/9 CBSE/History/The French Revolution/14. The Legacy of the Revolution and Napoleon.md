## Conclusion

In 1804, *Napoleon Bonaparte* crowned himself Emperor of France. He set out to conquer neighbouring European countries, dispossessing dynasties and creating kingdoms where he placed members of his family. Napoleon saw his role as a *moderniser of Europe*. He introduced many laws such as the protection of private property and a uniform system of weights and measures provided by the decimal system. Initially, many saw Napoleon as a liberator who would bring freedom for the people. But soon the Napoleonic armies came to be viewed everywhere as an invading force. He was finally defeated at Waterloo in 1815. Many of his measures that carried the revolutionary ideas of liberty and modern laws to other parts of Europe had an impact on people long after Napoleon had left.

The ideas of *liberty* and *democratic rights* were the most important legacy of the French Revolution. These spread from France to the rest of Europe during the nineteenth century, where feudal systems were abolished. Colonised peoples reworked the idea of freedom from bondage into their movements to create a *sovereign nation state*. Tipu Sultan and Rammohan Roy are two examples of individuals who responded to the ideas coming from revolutionary France.

> **Answer:**
> 1. The circumstances leading to the outbreak of revolutionary protest in France included:
>    *   Financial crisis: The French treasury was empty due to long wars, the cost of the court, and aiding the American colonies, leading to huge debts and interest payments.
>    *   Burden of taxes: The state needed to increase taxes, but the burden fell solely on the Third Estate, while the First and Second Estates were privileged and exempt.
>    *   Social inequality: French society was divided into three estates with inherent privileges for the clergy and nobility, causing resentment among the vast majority in the Third Estate.
>    *   Economic hardship: Rapid population increase led to increased demand for foodgrains, prices rose faster than wages, widening the gap between rich and poor. Bad harvests worsened the situation, leading to subsistence crises and high bread prices.
>    *   Inspiration from Enlightenment ideas: Philosophers promoted ideas of liberty, equality, social contract, and division of power, inspiring the educated middle class within the Third Estate to demand a society based on merit and equal rights, challenging the system of privileges by birth.
>    *   Immediate trigger: Louis XVI's decision to impose new taxes and the summoning of the Estates General, where the Third Estate demanded voting by head and formed the National Assembly when their demand was rejected, leading to protests and the storming of the Bastille.
> 2. Groups that benefited:
>    *   The wealthier middle classes (initially gained political rights as 'active citizens' under the 1791 Constitution, later seized power under the Directory).
>    *   Peasants (feudal dues and obligations were abolished).
>    *   Merchants and manufacturers (benefited from the abolition of feudal restrictions and later from Napoleon's modern laws like protection of private property and uniform weights/measures).
>    Groups forced to relinquish power:
>    *   The King (lost absolute power, monarchy abolished, executed).
>    *   The nobility (lost privileges, feudal dues, status).
>    *   The clergy (lost privileges, tithes, Church lands confiscated).
>    Sections dissatisfied:
>    *   Poor men and women (classified as passive citizens under the 1791 Constitution, denied voting rights).
>    *   Radicals like the Jacobins (dissatisfied with the constitutional monarchy and limited suffrage).
>    *   Royalists and some clergy/nobles (dissatisfied with the abolition of monarchy and privileges).
>    *   Women (denied equal political rights, though gained some legal improvements).
> 3. The legacy of the French Revolution for the peoples of the world during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries included:
>    *   Spread of ideas of liberty, equality, and fraternity.
>    *   Inspiration for democratic movements and the demand for democratic rights.
>    *   Challenge to feudal systems and absolute monarchies across Europe.
>    *   Influence on anti-colonial movements in Asia, Africa, and South America, inspiring the idea of creating sovereign nation-states and freedom from bondage.
>    *   Establishment of the principle of equality before the law.
>    *   Promotion of secular ideas and the separation of church and state.
> 4. Democratic rights we enjoy today whose origins could be traced to the French Revolution include:
>    *   Right to equality before the law.
>    *   Freedom of speech and expression.
>    *   Freedom of opinion.
>    *   Right to vote (initially limited, but the principle of citizen participation was established).
>    *   Right to resistance to oppression.
>    *   Protection from arbitrary arrest and detention.
>    *   The idea that sovereignty resides in the nation/people.
> 5. Yes, one could agree that the message of universal rights was beset with contradictions. For example:
>    *   The Declaration of the Rights of Man and Citizen proclaimed universal rights for "Man," but the Constitution of 1791 limited political rights (like voting) based on wealth, excluding the poor and all women.
>    *   While proclaiming liberty and equality, the revolutionary governments initially hesitated to abolish slavery in the colonies due to economic interests, and Napoleon later reintroduced it.
>    *   The Reign of Terror, justified by Robespierre as necessary to defend liberty, involved severe repression, mass executions, and the suppression of dissenting voices and women's clubs, contradicting the ideals of freedom and justice.
>    These examples show that the revolutionary ideals were not always applied universally or consistently in practice.
> 6. The rise of Napoleon can be explained by:
>    *   The political instability following the fall of the Jacobin government and the rule of the Directory. The Directory was often in conflict with the legislative councils, leading to a weak and unstable executive.
>    *   The need for a strong military leader to defend France against neighbouring European powers who opposed the revolution. Napoleon gained popularity and power through his military successes.
>    *   The desire among some sections of society for order and a strong government after years of revolution and turmoil (Reign of Terror, Directory instability).
>    *   Napoleon's own ambition, military genius, and political skill in consolidating power, eventually crowning himself Emperor, effectively ending the republican phase and establishing a new form of authoritarian rule, while paradoxically preserving some revolutionary principles like equality before the law and property rights.
