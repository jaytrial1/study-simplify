This section traces Adolf Hitler's background, his early involvement in politics, the growth of the Nazi Party, and the factors that contributed to his rise to become Chancellor of Germany.

# Hitler's Rise to Power

The crisis in the economy, politics, and society provided the backdrop for Hitler's rise. Born in Austria in 1889, Hitler grew up in poverty. He joined the army in World War I, served as a messenger, became a corporal, and earned medals for bravery. The German defeat horrified him, and the Versailles Treaty made him furious. In 1919, he joined a small group called the German Workers' Party, which he later took over and renamed the National Socialist German Workers' Party, known as the *Nazi Party*.

In 1923, Hitler attempted to seize control of Bavaria and march on Berlin but failed, was arrested for treason, and later released. The Nazis struggled to gain significant popular support until the early 1930s. It was during the Great Depression that Nazism became a mass movement. As banks collapsed, businesses shut down, workers lost jobs, and the middle classes faced destitution after 1929, Nazi *propaganda* offered hope for a better future. In 1928, the Nazi Party won only 2.6 percent of votes in the Reichstag, but by 1932, it was the largest party with 37 percent of the votes.

Hitler was a powerful and passionate speaker whose words moved people. He promised to build a strong nation, reverse the injustice of the Versailles Treaty, and restore German dignity. He promised employment, a secure future for the youth, and to eliminate foreign influences and 'conspiracies' against Germany.

Hitler developed a new political style, understanding the power of rituals and spectacle for mass mobilization. Nazis held massive rallies and public meetings to show support for Hitler and create unity. The red banners with the Swastika, the Nazi salute, and ritualized applause were key parts of this spectacle of power.

Nazi propaganda skillfully portrayed Hitler as a *messiah* or *saviour* who would rescue the people from distress. This image resonated with a population whose dignity and pride had been shattered and who faced severe economic and political crises.

### The Destruction of Democracy

On January 30, 1933, President Hindenburg offered Hitler the Chancellorship, the highest position in the cabinet. By then, the Nazis had gained the support of conservatives. Once in power, Hitler began dismantling democratic structures. A mysterious fire in the Reichstag building in February 1933 provided an opportunity. The *Fire Decree* of February 28, 1933, indefinitely suspended civil rights like freedom of speech, press, and assembly, which were guaranteed by the Weimar constitution. Hitler then targeted his enemies, the Communists, sending most to newly established *concentration camps*. Communist repression was severe; in Düsseldorf, 1,440 out of 6,808 arrest files were of Communists, but they were just one of 52 groups persecuted.

On March 3, 1933, the *Enabling Act* was passed, establishing dictatorship in Germany. It gave Hitler the power to rule by decree, bypassing Parliament. All political parties and trade unions, except the Nazi Party and its affiliates, were banned. The state gained complete control over the economy, media, army, and judiciary.

Special surveillance and security forces were created to control society according to Nazi wishes. Besides the regular police and the SA (Storm Troopers), these included the *Gestapo* (secret state police), the *SS* (protection squads), criminal police, and the Security Service (SD). The extra-constitutional powers of these forces made the Nazi state appear as a dreaded criminal state. People could be detained in Gestapo torture chambers, sent to concentration camps, deported, or arrested without legal procedures. Police forces could act with *impunity*.

## New words

**Concentration camp** - A camp where people were isolated and detained without due process of law. Typically, it was surrounded by electrified barbed wire fences.
