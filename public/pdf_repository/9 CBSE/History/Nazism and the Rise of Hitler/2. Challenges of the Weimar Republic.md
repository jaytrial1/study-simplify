This section describes the formation of the Weimar Republic after World War I and the significant political and economic challenges it faced, including the harsh Treaty of Versailles, political radicalism, hyperinflation, and the impact of the Great Depression.

# Birth of the Weimar Republic

Germany, a powerful empire in the early twentieth century, fought the First World War (1914-1918) alongside the Austrian empire against the Allies (England, France, and Russia). They initially hoped for a quick victory but the war stretched on, exhausting Europe's resources. Germany made early gains, occupying France and Belgium, but the Allies, strengthened by the US entry in 1917, won in November 1918.

The defeat of Imperial Germany and the emperor's abdication allowed parliamentary parties to reshape German politics. A National Assembly met at Weimar and established a democratic constitution with a federal structure. Deputies were elected to the German Parliament, the *Reichstag*, based on equal and universal votes for all adults, including women.

However, this republic was not well-received by its people, mainly due to the harsh terms of the peace treaty at Versailles after the war. The treaty with the Allies was humiliating. Germany lost its overseas colonies, a tenth of its population, 13 percent of its territories, 75 percent of its iron, and 26 percent of its coal to countries like France, Poland, Denmark, and Lithuania. The Allied Powers also *demilitarised* Germany to weaken it. The *War Guilt Clause* held Germany responsible for the war and the damages suffered by the Allied countries, forcing Germany to pay £6 billion in compensation. Allied armies occupied the resource-rich Rhineland for much of the 1920s. Many Germans blamed the new Weimar Republic for the defeat and the disgrace at Versailles.

# The Effects of the War

The war had a devastating psychological and financial impact on Europe, turning it from a continent of creditors into one of debtors. The young Weimar Republic inherited the burden of war guilt and national humiliation and was financially crippled by compensation payments. Supporters of the Republic (Socialists, Catholics, Democrats) were attacked by conservative nationalists and mockingly called the '*November criminals*'. This negative attitude significantly affected political developments in the early 1930s.

The First World War deeply affected European society and politics. Soldiers were elevated above civilians, and politicians stressed the need for men to be aggressive, strong, and masculine. Media glorified trench life, though in reality, soldiers suffered miserably with rats, gas, shelling, and rapid losses. Aggressive war propaganda and national honour became central, leading to growing support for conservative dictatorships. Democracy, being a young and fragile idea, struggled to survive the instabilities of interwar Europe.

### Political Radicalism and Economic Crises

The Weimar Republic's birth coincided with the Spartacist League's revolutionary uprising, inspired by the Bolshevik Revolution in Russia. Soviets of workers and sailors formed in many cities, demanding Soviet-style governance. Those opposed, including socialists, Democrats, and Catholics, met in Weimar to establish the democratic republic. The Weimar Republic suppressed the uprising using a war veterans' organization called the Free Corps. The Spartacists later founded the Communist Party of Germany. This conflict made Communists and Socialists irreconcilable enemies, preventing them from uniting against Hitler. Both revolutionaries and militant nationalists desired radical solutions.

Political radicalization worsened with the economic crisis of 1923. Germany had funded the war largely through loans and had to pay reparations in gold, depleting its scarce gold reserves. When Germany refused to pay in 1923, France occupied the industrial area of Ruhr to claim coal. Germany responded with passive resistance and printed paper currency recklessly. This led to *hyperinflation* as the value of the German mark plummeted. Prices soared dramatically; for example, the US dollar was worth 24,000 marks in April, rising to trillions by December 1923. Images of Germans using cartloads of money for simple purchases evoked worldwide sympathy.

### The Years of Depression

Between 1924 and 1928, Germany experienced some stability, but it was precarious. German economic recovery relied heavily on short-term loans, mostly from the USA. This support vanished when the *Wall Street Exchange* crashed in 1929, triggering the Great Economic Depression. People frantically sold shares, leading to a massive sell-off on October 24th. Over the next three years (1929-1932), the US national income halved, factories closed, exports fell, farmers suffered, and speculators withdrew money. The recession's effects were felt globally.

The German economy was hit hardest. By 1932, industrial production dropped to 40 percent of its 1929 level. Workers lost jobs or faced wage cuts. Unemployment reached 6 million. Men with placards offering to do any work were common sights. Unemployed youths engaged in criminal activities or idled despairingly.

The economic crisis caused deep anxiety. Middle classes (salaried employees, pensioners) saw savings vanish due to currency devaluation. Small businessmen, the self-employed, and retailers suffered as businesses failed. These groups feared *proletarianisation*, becoming impoverished like the working class or unemployed. Organized workers struggled, and unemployment weakened their bargaining power. Big business was in crisis. Farmers were hit by falling agricultural prices, and women, unable to feed their children, felt deep despair.

The Weimar Republic was also politically fragile due to inherent constitutional defects. *Proportional representation* made it hard for any single party to gain a majority, leading to unstable coalition governments. *Article 48* gave the President power to impose emergency, suspend civil rights, and rule by decree, which was frequently used. The Republic saw twenty different cabinets in its short life, averaging 239 days. The crisis persisted, and people lost faith in the democratic parliamentary system, which seemed unable to offer solutions.
