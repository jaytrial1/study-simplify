This section examines the sophisticated techniques used by the Nazi regime to control information and shape public opinion through propaganda, as well as exploring the different reactions of ordinary Germans to the regime's policies and crimes.

### The Art of Propaganda

The Nazi regime carefully and effectively used language and media. The terms they invented for their practices were deceptive and chilling. They avoided words like 'kill' or 'murder' in official communication. Mass killings were called '*special treatment*', '*final solution*' (for Jews), '*euthanasia*' (for the disabled), '*selection*', and '*disinfections*'. '*Evacuation*' meant deportation to gas chambers, which were deceptively labelled '*disinfection-areas*' and looked like bathrooms with fake showerheads.

Media was crucial for gaining support and popularizing the Nazi worldview. Ideas were spread through visual images, films, radio, posters, slogans, and leaflets. Posters stereotyped, mocked, abused, and depicted groups identified as 'enemies' as evil. Socialists and liberals were shown as weak and degenerate, attacked as malicious foreign agents. Propaganda films like *The Eternal Jew* aimed to create hatred for Jews. Orthodox Jews were stereotyped with flowing beards and kaftans, even though many German Jews were highly assimilated and outwardly indistinguishable. Jews were called *vermin*, *rats*, and *pests*, their movements compared to rodents. Nazism manipulated public minds and emotions, directing hatred and anger towards 'undesirables'.

Nazis also tried to appeal to all sections of society, promising solutions to their problems to win support.

# Ordinary People and the Crimes Against Humanity

How did the common people react to Nazism? Their reactions varied.
Many Germans adopted the Nazi worldview and language. They felt hatred towards Jews, reported suspicious neighbours, and believed Nazism would bring prosperity.

However, not all Germans were Nazis. Many actively resisted, risking police repression and death. The majority were passive onlookers and apathetic witnesses, too scared to act, differ, or protest, preferring to look away. Pastor Niemoeller, a resistance fighter, noted this silence in the face of Nazi crimes, writing a famous statement about the sequential persecution of different groups and the lack of protest until it was too late.

## Activity

Why does Erna Kranz say, 'I could only say for myself'? How do you view her opinion?

> **Answer:** Erna Kranz says 'I could only say for myself' because she is describing her personal experience and perception of the 1930s under the Nazi regime. She highlights the positive aspects she personally witnessed, such as increased salaries and a regained sense of national purpose, which offered her a " glimmer of hope" and made it "a good time" *for her*. She is acknowledging that her individual experience might not reflect everyone's reality, particularly the victims of the regime. Her opinion reflects the perspective of someone who benefited from or was not directly targeted by the early Nazi policies and propaganda, focusing on the economic improvements and national pride while seemingly overlooking or downplaying the persecution of others. It shows how personal circumstances and lack of direct experience with the atrocities could lead to a positive view of the regime, highlighting the selective impact of Nazi policies on different groups and the varied experiences of ordinary Germans.

## Box 1

Was the lack of concern for Nazi victims only because of the Terror? No, says Lawrence Rees who interviewed people from diverse backgrounds for his recent documentary, 'The Nazis: A Warning from History'.
Erna Kranz, an ordinary German teenager in the 1930s and a grandmother now, said to Rees:
'1930s offered a glimmer of hope, not just for the unemployed but for everybody for we all felt downtrodden. From my own experience I could say salaries increased and Germany seemed to have regained its sense of purpose. I could only say for myself, I thought it was a good time. I liked it.'

What Jews felt in Nazi Germany was entirely different. Charlotte Beradt secretly recorded people's dreams, published in *The Third Reich of Dreams*. She described how Jews internalized Nazi stereotypes, dreaming of hooked noses, black hair, and 'Jewish looks', haunted by images from the Nazi press. Jews suffered psychologically, 'dying many deaths' before reaching the gas chambers.
