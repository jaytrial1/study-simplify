This section explores how the Nazi regime sought to control and indoctrinate the youth through education and youth organizations, and how it defined and enforced a specific role for women, particularly emphasizing motherhood and racial purity.

# Youth in Nazi Germany

Hitler was intensely focused on the youth, believing a strong Nazi society required teaching children Nazi ideology, controlling them both inside and outside school.

Under Nazism, schools were 'cleansed' and 'purified'. Jewish teachers or those deemed 'politically unreliable' were dismissed. Children were segregated by race: Germans and Jews could not sit or play together. Later, 'undesirable children' (Jews, physically handicapped, Gypsies) were expelled and eventually sent to gas chambers in the 1940s.

'Good German' children underwent a process of Nazi schooling and ideological training. Textbooks were rewritten. Racial science was introduced to justify Nazi racial ideas. Anti-Jewish stereotypes were even included in maths classes. Children were taught loyalty, submission, hatred of Jews, and worship of Hitler. Sports aimed to foster violence and aggression; Hitler believed boxing made children 'iron hearted, strong and masculine'.

Youth organizations were tasked with educating German youth in the 'spirit of National Socialism'. Ten-year-olds joined *Jungvolk*. At 14, boys joined the *Hitler Youth*, learning to worship war, glorify aggression, condemn democracy, and hate Jews, Communists, Gypsies, and other 'undesirables'. After rigorous training, they joined the Labour Service at 18, then served in the armed forces and Nazi organizations. The Nazi Youth League, founded in 1922 and renamed Hitler Youth in 1926, systematically dissolved and banned all other youth organizations to unify youth under Nazi control.

# The Nazi Cult of Motherhood

Children were repeatedly told that women were fundamentally different from men and that the democratic struggle for equal rights was wrong and harmful to society. While boys were trained to be aggressive, masculine, and 'steel hearted', girls were instructed to be good mothers and raise pure-blooded Aryan children. Girls were expected to maintain racial purity, avoid contact with Jews, manage the home, and instill Nazi values in their children, acting as bearers of Aryan culture and race.

In 1933, Hitler stated, 'In my state the mother is the most important citizen.' However, Nazi Germany did not treat all mothers equally. Women who bore racially 'undesirable' children were punished, while those who produced racially 'desirable' children were rewarded. They received preferential treatment in hospitals and concessions on shopping, theatre tickets, and railway fares. To encourage large families, Honour Crosses were awarded: bronze for four children, silver for six, and gold for eight or more.

All 'Aryan' women who deviated from the prescribed conduct were publicly condemned and severely punished. Those who associated with Jews, Poles, or Russians were paraded with shaved heads, blackened faces, and placards declaring, 'I have sullied the honour of the nation.' Many were jailed and lost civic honour, husbands, and families for this 'criminal offence'.
