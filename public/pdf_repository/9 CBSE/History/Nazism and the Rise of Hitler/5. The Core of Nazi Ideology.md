This section explains the fundamental beliefs of Nazism, focusing on its racial hierarchy, the concept of the Aryan race's superiority, the scapegoating of Jews, and the idea of acquiring 'living space' (Lebensraum).

# The Nazi Worldview

The crimes committed by the Nazis were rooted in a specific system of belief and practices – the Nazi ideology, synonymous with Hitler's worldview. This ideology rejected the idea of equality among people, asserting a racial hierarchy. At the top were blond, blue-eyed *Nordic German Aryans*, while Jews were placed at the very bottom, seen as an '*anti-race*' and the main enemies of the Aryans. Other coloured people were ranked in between based on their features.

Hitler's racism drew upon thinkers like Charles Darwin and Herbert Spencer. Darwin's concept of evolution and natural selection explained the creation of species, while Spencer added the idea of *survival of the fittest*, where only species adapting to changing conditions survived. It's crucial to remember Darwin did not advocate human intervention in natural selection, but his ideas were misused by racist thinkers and politicians to justify imperial rule. The Nazi argument was simple: the strongest race survives, the weak perish. The Aryan race was deemed the finest and had to maintain its purity, grow stronger, and dominate the world.

Another key aspect of Hitler's ideology was the geopolitical concept of *Lebensraum* (living space). He believed Germany needed to acquire new territories for settlement. This would expand the 'mother country' and allow settlers to maintain ties to their origin while increasing Germany's material resources and power. Hitler planned to expand eastward, aiming to concentrate all Germans geographically in one place, using Poland as a testing ground.

## Source A

'For this earth is not allotted to anyone nor is it presented to anyone as a gift. It is awarded by providence to people who in their hearts have the courage to conquer it, the strength to preserve it, and the industry to put it to the plough... The primary right of this world is the right to life, so far as one possesses the strength for this. Hence on the basis of this right a vigorous nation will always find ways of adapting its territory to its population size.'
Hitler, Secret Book, ed. Telford Taylor.

## Source B

'In an era when the earth is gradually being divided up among states, some of which embrace almost entire continents, we cannot speak of a world power in connection with a formation whose political mother country is limited to the absurd area of five hundred kilometers.' Hitler, Mein Kampf, p. 644.

## Activity

Read Sources A and B

> What do they tell you about Hitler's imperial ambition?
> **Answer:** Sources A and B reveal Hitler's strong belief that Germany needed to expand its territory ("adapt its territory to its population size", "find ways of adapting its territory") through conquest ("courage to conquer it"). Source B explicitly states that Germany's current size ("five hundred kilometers") is too small for it to be considered a world power, implying that territorial expansion is necessary to achieve global dominance. Together, they show his conviction that a "vigorous nation" has the right to take land and that Germany's small size hinders its potential as a world power, thus justifying imperial ambition.
> What do you think Mahatma Gandhi would have said to Hitler about these ideas?
> **Answer:** Based on the letters from Mahatma Gandhi included later in the chapter, he would have strongly opposed Hitler's ideas of conquest and violence. Gandhi advocated for non-violence ("deliberately shunned the method of war," "found in non-violence a force," "no such thing as defeat... without killing or hurting"). He would likely have argued that achieving goals through violence and taking land from others is morally wrong and ultimately destructive, warning that such methods would lead to others using violence against him ("some other power will certainly improve upon your method and beat you with your own weapon"). He would have appealed to humanity and urged Hitler to stop the war, suggesting that violent actions leave a legacy people cannot be proud of.
