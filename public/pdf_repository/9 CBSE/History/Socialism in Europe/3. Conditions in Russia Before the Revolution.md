This section examines the social, economic, and political state of the Russian Empire in the early 20th century, setting the stage for the revolutions.

# The Russian Revolution

In Russia, one of Europe's least industrialised states, the situation was unique: socialists successfully seized state power through the **October Revolution of 1917**. The fall of the monarchy in February 1917 and the subsequent events in October are collectively known as the **Russian Revolution**.

To understand how this revolution occurred, it's necessary to look at the conditions in Russia in the years leading up to 1917.

## The Russian Empire in 1914

In 1914, the vast Russian Empire was ruled by **Tsar Nicholas II**. The empire was geographically extensive, including territories that are now Finland, Latvia, Lithuania, Estonia, parts of Poland, Ukraine, Belarus, Central Asian states, Georgia, Armenia, and Azerbaijan. While the majority religion was Russian Orthodox Christianity, the empire was religiously diverse, also including Catholics, Protestants, Muslims, and Buddhists.

## Economy and Society

At the beginning of the 20th century, the economy of the Russian Empire was overwhelmingly agricultural. About **85 per cent of the population were agriculturists**, a significantly higher proportion than in most European countries (e.g., 40-50% in France and Germany). Russian cultivators produced for both their own needs and the market, making Russia a major grain exporter.

Industry existed in scattered pockets, notably in St Petersburg and Moscow. While craftsmen were numerous, large factories also operated. Industrial growth accelerated in the 1890s with the expansion of the railway network and increased foreign investment, leading to a doubling of coal production and a quadrupling of iron and steel output. By the 1900s, factory workers and craftsmen were nearly equal in number in some areas.

Most industry was privately owned. Although the government supervised large factories to enforce minimum wages and limited working hours, rules were often broken by factory inspectors. In smaller craft units, working days could extend up to 15 hours, compared to 10-12 hours in factories. Accommodation for workers varied, from rooms to dormitories.

Workers formed a divided social group. Some maintained ties to their villages, while others had permanently settled in cities. Workers were also divided by skill level; metalworkers, for example, considered themselves superior due to the training and skill required for their jobs. **Women constituted 31 per cent of the factory workforce by 1914** but were paid less than men (half to three-quarters of a man's wage). Divisions were also visible in dress and manners. Worker associations aimed to provide support during unemployment or hardship, but they were few. Despite these divisions, workers did unite to strike over dismissals or working conditions, with notable strikes occurring in the textile industry (1896-1897) and the metal industry (1902).

In the countryside, peasants cultivated most of the land, but large properties were owned by the nobility, the crown, and the Orthodox Church. Like workers, peasants were divided and deeply religious. Unlike peasants in France, Russian peasants generally had little respect for the nobility, whose power came from service to the Tsar rather than local popularity. Russian peasants desired the land of the nobles. They frequently refused to pay rent and sometimes murdered landlords, with large-scale incidents occurring in south Russia in 1902 and across Russia in 1905.

Russian peasants were also distinct from other European peasants due to their practice of periodically pooling their land in their **commune (mir)** and dividing it according to the needs of individual families.

## Socialism in Russia

Before 1914, all political parties were illegal in Russia. The **Russian Social Democratic Workers Party** was founded in 1898 by socialists inspired by Marx, but it had to operate illegally due to government repression. It used a newspaper, mobilised workers, and organised strikes.

Some Russian socialists believed that the peasants' tradition of pooling and dividing land made them inherently socialist, suggesting peasants, rather than workers, would be the primary force for revolution and that Russia could achieve socialism faster than other countries. These socialists were active in the countryside and formed the **Socialist Revolutionary Party** in 1900, which campaigned for peasants' rights and the transfer of noble land to peasants.

Social Democrats disagreed with the Socialist Revolutionaries about the peasantry. **Vladimir Lenin**, a leader of the Bolshevik faction, argued that peasants were not a unified group. He noted their internal divisions based on wealth (poor vs. rich) and role (labourers vs. capitalist employers), concluding that these differences prevented them from all being part of a single socialist movement.

The Russian Social Democratic Workers Party itself was divided on organisational strategy. Lenin's **Bolshevik** group believed that in a repressive society like Tsarist Russia, the party needed to be disciplined and restrict membership to a select number of high-quality individuals. Others, the **Mensheviks**, argued that the party should be open to all, similar to parties in Germany.
