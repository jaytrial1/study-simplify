This section covers the Russian Civil War, the forces involved, the Bolsheviks' success, and the establishment of the Soviet Union.

## The Civil War

The Bolsheviks' order for land redistribution led to the breakup of the Russian army, as many soldiers, being peasants, deserted to return home for the land division. Non-Bolshevik socialists, liberals, and supporters of the autocracy condemned the Bolshevik takeover and formed troops in south Russia to fight the Bolsheviks (the 'reds'). During 1918 and 1919, the 'greens' (Socialist Revolutionaries) and 'whites' (pro-Tsarists) controlled most of the former Russian empire. These anti-Bolshevik forces received support from French, American, British, and Japanese troops, who were concerned about the spread of socialism in Russia. As the 'reds', 'greens', and 'whites' fought the **Civil War**, widespread looting, banditry, and famine occurred.

The 'whites', particularly those who supported private property, took harsh measures against peasants who had seized land, which alienated many and led to a loss of popular support for the non-Bolsheviks. By January 1920, the Bolsheviks controlled most of the former Russian empire. Their success was partly due to cooperation with non-Russian nationalities and Muslim Jadidists. However, this cooperation failed where Russian colonists became Bolsheviks and brutally suppressed local nationalists, as seen in Khiva in Central Asia, causing confusion about the Bolshevik government's true nature.

Partly to address this confusion and gain support, most non-Russian nationalities were granted **political autonomy** (the right to govern themselves) within the **Soviet Union (USSR)**, the state created from the Russian empire in December 1922. However, this autonomy was often undermined by unpopular policies imposed by the central Bolshevik government on local administrations, such as the harsh discouragement of **nomadism** (a lifestyle of moving from place to place). Consequently, attempts to win over different nationalities were only partially successful.

## Activity

Why did people in Central Asia respond to the Russian Revolution in different ways?

> **Answer:** Based on the text (Source F): People in Central Asia responded differently because the revolution had varied impacts on them. Some, like the Kirgiz described by M.N. Roy, saw the revolution as freedom from Tsarist oppression and the return of control over their homeland, greeting it with enthusiasm. Others, like those described by the Kazakh leader, experienced violence, pillage, taxes, and the imposition of a new dictatorial power by the same group of people (bureaucrats) who had served the Tsar, leading to consternation and terror. The Bolsheviks' brutal suppression of local nationalists in places like Khiva also created negative responses among some non-Russian groups.

# Central Asia of the October Revolution: Two Views

This box presents two differing perspectives on the Russian Revolution from Central Asia.
M.N. Roy describes meeting Kirgiz people who welcomed the revolution, seeing it as liberation from the Tsar and generals who had conquered their land, and feeling they were masters of their home again. They appeared enthusiastic and felt a sense of freedom and equality mixing with Europeans.
A Kazakh leader in 1919, quoted by Bennigsen and Quelquejay, states that the Kirghiz welcomed the February Revolution, hoping for autonomy, but viewed the October Revolution with consternation and terror due to accompanying violence, pillage, taxes, and the establishment of dictatorial power by the same types of bureaucrats as under the Tsar.
Description: This box provides two contrasting accounts from Central Asia regarding the Russian Revolution, one highlighting the positive perception of liberation from Tsarist rule and the other focusing on the negative experiences of violence and new forms of oppression under the Bolsheviks.
