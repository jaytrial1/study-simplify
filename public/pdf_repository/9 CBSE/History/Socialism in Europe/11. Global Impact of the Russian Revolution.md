This section explores the international influence of the Russian Revolution and the Soviet Union, its inspiration for socialist movements worldwide, and later disillusionment.

# The Global Influence of the Russian Revolution and the USSR

Existing socialist parties in Europe had mixed reactions to the Bolsheviks' methods of seizing and retaining power. However, the concept of a workers' state deeply inspired people globally. This led to the formation of **communist parties** in many countries, such as the Communist Party of Great Britain. The Bolsheviks encouraged colonial peoples to emulate their revolution. Many non-Russians from outside the USSR attended the **Conference of the Peoples of the East (1920)** and the **Comintern** (Communist International), an international body founded by the Bolsheviks for pro-Bolshevik socialist parties. Some received education at the USSR's Communist University of the Workers of the East. By the start of the Second World War, the USSR had elevated socialism to a prominent global ideology.

Despite this international stature, by the 1950s, it became evident even within the USSR that the government's style was not consistent with the Russian Revolution's original ideals. The global socialist movement also acknowledged problems in the Soviet Union. While Russia had transformed from a backward country into a major power, developing its industries and agriculture and feeding its population, it had done so by denying fundamental freedoms to its citizens and using repressive methods for development projects. By the late 20th century, the USSR's international standing as a model socialist country had diminished, although socialist ideals still held respect among its own people. Across different countries, the ideas of socialism were re-evaluated and adapted in various ways.

## Box 5

## Writing about the Russian Revolution in India

Many Indians were inspired by the Russian Revolution. Some attended the Communist University in the USSR. By the mid-1920s, the Communist Party of India was formed and maintained contact with the Soviet Communist Party. Prominent Indian political and cultural figures, including Jawaharlal Nehru and Rabindranath Tagore, showed interest in the Soviet experiment and visited Russia, writing about Soviet Socialism. Indian writings provided impressions of Soviet Russia; for instance, R.S. Avasthi wrote on the Russian Revolution and Lenin in Hindi, and S.D. Vidyalankar wrote about the rebirth of Russia and the Soviet state. The revolution also inspired writings in various other Indian languages.

## Source F

## An Indian arrives in Soviet Russia in 1920

Shaukat Usmani, an Indian revolutionary and Comintern leader, describes his arrival in Soviet Russia in 1920. He was struck by seeing Europeans mixing freely with Asians for the first time, which convinced him that he had arrived in a land of genuine equality and true freedom. Despite poverty caused by counter-revolutionaries and imperialists, he found the people more joyful and satisfied than before, filled with confidence and fearlessness instilled by the revolution. He witnessed a "real brotherhood of mankind" among people of fifty different nationalities, with no caste or religious barriers, and observed ordinary people like workers, peasants, and soldiers speaking eloquently like professional lecturers.
Description: This source is an account from an Indian revolutionary arriving in Soviet Russia in 1920, expressing admiration for the apparent social equality, freedom, fearlessness, and brotherhood among different nationalities, despite economic hardship, attributing these positive changes to the revolution.

## Source G

## Rabindranath Tagore wrote from Russia in 1930

Rabindranath Tagore, writing from Russia in 1930, observes that Moscow appears less clean and its streets lack the smartness of other European capitals, noting that "the whole place belongs to the workers." He finds that the masses, who were previously in the background, have now come forward. He compares the transformation to the magical work of the Genii, expressing astonishment that people who were as illiterate, helpless, and hungry as India's masses just a decade ago have managed to overcome their ignorance and helplessness so quickly. He contrasts this with the situation of peasants and workers in his own country.
Description: This source is an account from Indian poet Rabindranath Tagore visiting Soviet Russia in 1930, expressing admiration for the elevation of ordinary workers and peasants and the rapid progress made in overcoming ignorance and helplessness, contrasting it positively with the situation in India, while also noting the less polished appearance of the capital.
