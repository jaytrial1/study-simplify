This section describes the Bolsheviks' efforts to construct a socialist society through centralised planning, industrialisation, and social programs, and the resulting challenges.

# Making a Socialist Society

During the Civil War, the Bolsheviks maintained the nationalisation of industries and banks. They permitted peasants to cultivate the land that had been socialised and used confiscated noble land to establish collective work examples.

A system of **centralised planning** was introduced. Officials assessed the economy and set targets for **Five Year Plans**. The government fixed prices to drive industrial growth during the first two Plans (1927-1932 and 1933-1938). This centralised planning led to economic growth, with industrial production significantly increasing (e.g., oil, coal, steel by 100% between 1929 and 1933). New industrial cities were built.

## Box 4

## Socialist Cultivation in a Village in the Ukraine

'A commune was set up using two [confiscated] farms as a base. The commune consisted of thirteen families with a total of seventy persons ... The farm tools taken from the ... farms were turned over to the commune ...The members ate in a communal dining hall and income was divided in accordance with the principles of "cooperative communism". The entire proceeds of the members' labor, as well as all dwellings and facilities belonging to the commune were shared by the commune members.'
Fedor Belov, The History of a Soviet Collective Farm (1955).
Description: This box describes the establishment and operation of an early socialist commune (kolkhoz) in Ukraine, detailing how confiscated land and tools were used, how members lived and ate communally, and how income and property were shared according to cooperative principles.

However, the rapid pace of construction resulted in **poor working conditions**. For example, the construction of a steel plant in Magnitogorsk in three years involved harsh living conditions for workers, leading to numerous work stoppages. Workers' accommodation was often basic and difficult, especially in harsh winters.

The Soviet government also developed an extended schooling system and provided access to universities for factory workers and peasants. Crèches were established in factories to care for the children of women workers, and cheap public health care was provided. Model living quarters were set up for workers. The effectiveness of these social programs was uneven due to limited government resources.

## Source C

## Dreams and Realities of a Soviet Childhood in 1933

This source is a letter from a 13-year-old worker to Soviet President Kalinin in 1933. The child describes a large, poor family with a deceased father (who died "fighting for the worker's cause") and an ailing mother. The child expresses a strong desire to study but cannot attend school because their boots are worn out and the family has no money or bread. Despite Lenin's emphasis on studying, the child feels forced to work in a factory to prevent the family from starving, as they have no relatives to help. The child mentions studying well in Class 5 with no bad reports.
Description: This source is a letter from a child worker in 1933 Soviet Russia, revealing the harsh realities of poverty and necessity that forced children into labour despite the stated socialist ideals of education and welfare, particularly for families who had sacrificed for the revolutionary cause.
