This section introduces the impact of the French Revolution on European society and the emergence of different political ideologies regarding societal change.

# The Age of Social Change

Following the French Revolution, Europe saw the spread of powerful ideas like freedom and equality. This sparked discussions about changing the traditional social structure, which was dominated by the aristocracy and the Church. New ideas about individual rights and who should hold social power began circulating widely, influencing discussions even in places like India.

However, not everyone desired a complete societal overhaul. Responses varied, ranging from those who accepted gradual change to those advocating for radical restructuring. This led to the emergence of different political viewpoints: 'conservatives', 'liberals', and 'radicals'. Understanding these terms in their historical context is crucial to grasping the political landscape of the 19th century and how these ideologies influenced societal transformation.

This chapter will explore some of these important political traditions and their impact on change, culminating in a focus on the Russian Revolution, where socialism became a powerful force shaping the 20th century.

## Liberals, Radicals and Conservatives

One group advocating for change were the **liberals**. They envisioned a nation that *tolerated all religions*, a departure from the common practice in European states at the time which favoured one religion. Liberals also opposed the *uncontrolled power of dynastic rulers* and sought to *safeguard individual rights* against government authority. They supported a *representative, elected parliamentary government*, subject to laws interpreted by an *independent judiciary*. However, they were *not democrats*; they did not believe in universal adult suffrage (the right of every citizen to vote). They generally felt that only men with property should vote and did not support voting rights for women.

In contrast, **radicals** desired a nation where the government was based on the *majority of the country's population*. Many supported *women's suffragette movements* (movements advocating for women's right to vote). Unlike liberals, they *opposed the privileges of great landowners and wealthy factory owners*. While not against private property itself, they disliked the *concentration of property in the hands of a few*.

**Conservatives** initially opposed both radicals and liberals. However, after the French Revolution, even conservatives acknowledged the need for change. By the 19th century, they accepted that some change was *inevitable* but insisted that the *past had to be respected* and change should occur through a *slow process*.

These differing perspectives on societal change led to clashes amidst the political turmoil that followed the French Revolution. The revolutions and national transformations of the 19th century helped define the possibilities and limitations of these political tendencies.

## Industrial Society and Social Change

These political trends emerged during a period of significant social and economic transformation. New cities and industrial regions developed, railways expanded, and the **Industrial Revolution** took hold.

Industrialisation drew men, women, and children into factories, often resulting in *long work hours* and *poor wages*. *Unemployment was common*, especially during economic downturns. Rapid urban growth led to problems with *housing and sanitation*. Liberals and radicals sought solutions to these pressing issues.

Almost all industries were privately owned. Liberals and radicals themselves were often property owners and employers. Having gained wealth through trade and industry, they believed that such enterprise should be encouraged, arguing that a healthy and educated workforce, coupled with the freedom of individuals to labour and capitalists to operate without restraint, would lead to societal development. Many working people seeking change aligned themselves with liberal and radical groups in the early 19th century.

Some nationalists, liberals, and radicals advocated for revolutions to dismantle the governments established in Europe after 1815. They became revolutionaries in France, Italy, Germany, and Russia, working to overthrow existing monarchies. Nationalists aimed to create 'nations' where all citizens would have equal rights. Giuseppe Mazzini, an Italian nationalist, conspired to achieve this in Italy after 1815, and his writings influenced nationalists elsewhere, including India.
