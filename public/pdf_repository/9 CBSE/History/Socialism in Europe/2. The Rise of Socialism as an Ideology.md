This section discusses the development of socialist ideas in Europe, contrasting them with capitalism and exploring early socialist visions and key thinkers.

# The Coming of Socialism to Europe

Socialism emerged as a far-reaching vision for structuring society, gaining significant attention in Europe by the mid-19th century.

Socialists were fundamentally *against private property*, viewing it as the root cause of social problems. They argued that private property owners were primarily concerned with personal gain, not the welfare of those who made the property productive (the workers). Therefore, they believed that if society as a whole, rather than individuals, controlled property, collective social interests would be prioritised. Socialists campaigned for this change.

The question arose: How would a society without private property function? What would be its foundation?

Socialists proposed different models. Some favoured **cooperatives**. Robert Owen, an English manufacturer, attempted to build a cooperative community called New Harmony in the USA. Other socialists felt that cooperatives needed *government encouragement* to succeed on a large scale. Louis Blanc in France, for instance, wanted the government to support cooperatives and replace capitalist enterprises. These cooperatives would be associations where people produced goods together and shared profits based on their work.

**Karl Marx** and **Friedrich Engels** significantly contributed to socialist thought. Marx argued that industrial society was inherently **capitalist**, with capitalists owning the means of production (factories) and profiting from the labour of workers. He believed that workers' conditions could not improve as long as profit accumulated in private hands. Marx called for workers to *overthrow capitalism and private property* to free themselves from exploitation. He envisioned a radically socialist society where all property was socially controlled, which he termed a **communist society**. Marx was convinced that workers would inevitably triumph over capitalists, and a communist society was the natural progression of the future.

## Activity

List two differences between the capitalist and socialist ideas of private property.

> **Answer:** Based on the text:
> 1.  Capitalists believe private property is the source of profit and should be owned by individuals for personal gain. Socialists believe private property is the root of social ills and should be controlled by society as a whole for collective social interests.
> 2.  Capitalists believe that private ownership and the freedom to operate without restraint lead to societal development. Socialists believe that collective control of property is necessary to ensure the welfare of those who make it productive (workers) and pay attention to collective interests.

## Support for Socialism

By the 1870s, socialist ideas had spread across Europe. To coordinate their efforts internationally, socialists formed the **Second International**.

Workers in countries like England and Germany created associations to campaign for *better living and working conditions*. They established funds to support members during hardship and demanded *reduced working hours* and the *right to vote*. In Germany, these associations collaborated with the Social Democratic Party (SPD) and helped it win parliamentary seats. By 1905, Labour and Socialist Parties were formed in Britain and France, respectively. Despite their growing influence and strong representation in parliaments, socialists were unable to form a government in Europe before 1914. However, their ideas did influence legislation, though governments remained dominated by conservatives, liberals, and radicals.

## Activity

Imagine that a meeting has been called in your area to discuss the socialist idea of doing away with private property and introducing collective ownership. Write the speech you would make at the meeting if you are:
*   a poor labourer working in the fields
*   a medium-level landowner
*   a house owner

> **Answer:** Based on the text's description of these groups' likely perspectives:
> *   **Poor labourer:** Would likely express support for collective ownership, hoping it would lead to better wages, working conditions, and access to land or resources currently owned by others. They might speak about the hardship caused by private property owners focused on profit and the hope that collective ownership would prioritise the welfare of workers and the poor.
> *   **Medium-level landowner:** Would likely express concern or opposition. While perhaps not as wealthy as "great landowners," they still own property and might fear losing it under collective ownership. They might argue for the value of their individual effort and the right to own and benefit from their land, perhaps suggesting reforms to address inequality rather than outright abolition of private property.
> *   **House owner:** Similar to the landowner, a house owner would likely be apprehensive about losing their personal property. They might question how collective ownership would work for housing and whether they would still have a secure place to live or control over their home. They might argue for the right to own a home and the security it provides.
