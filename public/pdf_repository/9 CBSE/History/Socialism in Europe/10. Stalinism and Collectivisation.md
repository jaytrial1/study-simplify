This section focuses on Stalin's policies, including the forceful collectivisation of agriculture and the suppression of opposition.

## Stalinism and Collectivisation

The period of early centralised planning under Stalin was marked by the disastrous policy of **collectivisation of agriculture**. By 1927-1928, Soviet towns faced severe grain shortages because peasants refused to sell grain to government buyers at fixed prices.

Stalin, who assumed leadership after Lenin's death, implemented stringent emergency measures. He believed that wealthy peasants and traders (**kulaks**) were hoarding grain to drive up prices. He ordered the cessation of speculation and confiscation of supplies.

In 1928, Party members were sent to grain-producing regions to supervise forced grain collections and raid kulaks. As shortages persisted, the decision was made to **collectivise farms**. The argument was that grain shortages were partly due to the small size of peasant landholdings after 1917, which hindered modernisation. To develop modern, mechanised farms, it was deemed necessary to "eliminate kulaks," seize land from peasants, and establish large, state-controlled farms.

Stalin's collectivisation programme began in 1929, forcing all peasants to cultivate in **collective farms (kolkhoz)**. The bulk of land and tools were transferred to collective farm ownership. Peasants worked on the land, and profits were shared among the kolkhoz members. Enraged peasants resisted by destroying their livestock, resulting in a one-third drop in cattle numbers between 1929 and 1931. Those who resisted collectivisation faced severe punishment, including deportation and exile (forced removal from the country). Resisting peasants argued they were not rich and supported socialism but opposed collective farms for various reasons. Stalin's government allowed some independent cultivation but treated such peasants unsympathetically.

Despite collectivisation, agricultural production did not immediately increase. The bad harvests of 1930-1933 contributed to one of the most devastating famines in Soviet history, resulting in over 4 million deaths.

## Source D

Official view of the opposition to collectivisation and the government response
This source is a report from K.M. Karlson, President of the State Police Administration of the Ukraine, to the Communist Party Central Committee on 19 March 1930. It describes "mass insurrections of the peasantry" in various regions of Ukraine from mid-February 1930, caused by "distortions of the Party's line" by lower-level officials during collectivisation efforts. These disturbances spread, with the most aggressive near the border. The report states that most peasant insurrections demanded the return of collectivised grain, livestock, and tools. Between 1st February and 15th March 1930, 25,000 people were arrested, with 656 executed, 3673 imprisoned in labour camps, and 5580 exiled.
Description: This source provides an official Soviet police report detailing widespread peasant resistance to collectivisation in Ukraine in early 1930, their demands for the return of confiscated property, and the scale of the government's repressive response involving mass arrests, executions, imprisonment, and exile.

Many within the Party criticised the chaos in industrial production under the Planned Economy and the negative consequences of collectivisation. Stalin and his supporters accused these critics of plotting against socialism. Accusations were made throughout the country, leading to over 2 million people being sent to prisons or labour camps by 1939. Many were innocent but were forced to make false confessions under torture and were executed, including talented professionals.

## Source E

This is a letter written by a peasant who did not want to join the collective farm.
The letter, written to the newspaper Krestianskaia Gazeta (Peasant Newspaper) by Afanasii Dedorovich Frebenev, an independent cultivator born in 1879, describes his situation. He mentions having a family of six, including his wife, three children who attend school, and an elderly sister. He explains that from 1932 onwards, heavy and increasing taxes were levied on him which he found impossible to pay. By 1935, local authorities had registered and sold all his property – horse, cow, calf, sheep, implements, furniture, wood – to cover taxes. In 1936, two of his buildings were sold, bought by the kolkhoz. By 1937, one of his two huts was sold and the other confiscated.
Description: This source is a personal letter from an independent peasant describing the severe economic pressure and confiscation of property he faced due to heavy taxation, ultimately leading to the loss of his farm and livelihood as part of the state's policy to eliminate independent farming and enforce collectivisation.
