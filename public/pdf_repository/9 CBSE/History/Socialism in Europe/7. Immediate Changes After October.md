This section outlines the initial policies and transformations implemented by the Bolshevik government immediately following the October Revolution.

# What Changed after October?

The Bolsheviks, fundamentally opposed to private property, implemented radical changes after taking power. Most industry and banks were **nationalised in November 1917**, meaning the government took ownership and control. **Land was declared social property**, and peasants were granted permission to seize land from the nobility. In cities, the Bolsheviks enforced the **partition of large houses** based on family needs. They abolished the old aristocratic titles. To symbolise the new era, new uniforms were designed for the army and officials; the Soviet hat (budeonovka) was chosen in a 1918 competition.

The Bolshevik Party was renamed the **Russian Communist Party (Bolshevik)**. In November 1917, the Bolsheviks held elections for the Constituent Assembly but failed to win a majority. In January 1918, the Assembly rejected Bolshevik policies, leading Lenin to dismiss it. He argued that the All Russian Congress of Soviets, which represented the Soviets that brought about the revolution, was more democratic than an assembly elected under uncertain conditions. In March 1918, despite opposition from their allies, the Bolsheviks signed the **Treaty of Brest Litovsk**, making peace with Germany. In subsequent years, the Bolsheviks became the sole party permitted to participate in elections for the All Russian Congress of Soviets, which became the country's parliament. Russia effectively became a **one-party state**. Trade unions were brought under party control. The secret police, initially called the Cheka and later the OGPU and NKVD, suppressed criticism of the Bolsheviks. Many young writers and artists initially supported the Party's socialist and revolutionary aims, leading to artistic and architectural experiments after October 1917. However, some became disillusioned due to the censorship imposed by the Party.

## The October Revolution and the Russian Countryside: Two Views

This box presents two contrasting accounts of the impact of the October Revolution on the countryside, one from a peasant and one from a landowning family member.
The peasant account describes enthusiastic reception of the news, leading to the looting of the manor house, requisitioning of stock farms, destruction of orchards and farm buildings, and distribution of land among peasants ready for the "new Soviet life."
The landowning family member's account describes the "coup" as painless and peaceful initially, with the estate owner and his family being treated correctly and even politely by the chairman of the new committee. They were allowed to keep some livestock, and servants vouched for their safety and property, wanting them treated humanely. This account mentions rumours of villages trying to restore the estate owner, suggesting some resistance or mixed feelings among peasants.
Description: This box provides two primary source perspectives on the immediate impact of the October Revolution in rural Russia, highlighting both the revolutionary land seizures by peasants and the varied experiences of landowners, ranging from destruction to relatively humane treatment depending on local circumstances.

## Activity

Read the two views on the revolution in the countryside. Imagine yourself to be a witness to the events. Write a short account from the standpoint of:
*   an owner of an estate
*   a small peasant
*   a journalist

> **Answer:** Based on Box 3:
> *   **Owner of an estate:** Would likely describe the initial shock and fear, followed by either the devastation of losing property and facing violence (as in the first account) or a tense period of uncertainty where property was taken but personal safety was initially maintained (as in the second account). They would lament the loss of their ancestral land and way of life, perhaps expressing disbelief or anger at the peasants' actions or the new authorities.
> *   **Small peasant:** Would likely express excitement and relief at the prospect of finally owning land previously controlled by the nobles. They might describe participating in the seizure of land and property, seeing it as long-awaited justice. They would welcome the "new Soviet life" that promises land and an end to the old system.
> *   **Journalist:** Would aim for a balanced report, describing the rapid collapse of the old order in the countryside. They would report on the widespread seizure of land by peasants and the varied reactions of landowners. They might note the enthusiasm among many peasants but also mention instances of violence or confusion, and perhaps the different approaches taken by local committees. They would highlight the fundamental shift in land ownership and power dynamics.
