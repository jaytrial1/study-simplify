# A Turbulent Time: The 1905 Revolution

Russia was an autocracy; unlike other European rulers, the Tsar was not accountable to a parliament even in the early 20th century. Russian liberals campaigned to change this. They joined forces with Social Democrats and Socialist Revolutionaries, as well as peasants and workers, during the **1905 Revolution** to demand a constitution. Nationalists within the empire (like in Poland) and Muslim reformers (Jadidists) also supported this movement, seeking modernised societies.

The year 1904 was particularly difficult for Russian workers. Rising prices of essential goods caused real wages (the purchasing power of wages) to drop by 20 per cent. Membership in workers' associations increased significantly. The dismissal of four members of the Assembly of Russian Workers at the Putilov Iron Works triggered industrial action. Over 110,000 workers in St Petersburg went on strike, demanding an eight-hour working day, wage increases, and improved working conditions.

## Source A

Alexander Shlyapnikov, a socialist worker of the time, gives us a description of how the meetings were organised:
'Propaganda was done in the plants and shops on an individual basis. There were also discussion circles ... Legal meetings took place on matters concerning [official issues], but this activity was skilfully integrated into the general struggle for the liberation of the working class. Illegal meetings were ... arranged on the spur of the moment but in an organised way during lunch, in evening break, in front of the exit, in the yard or, in establishments with several floors, on the stairs. The most alert workers would form a "plug" in the doorway, and the whole mass piled up in the exit. An agitator would get up right there on the spot. Management would contact the police on the telephone, but the speeches would have already been made and the necessary decision taken by the time they arrived ...'
Alexander Shlyapnikov, On the Eve of 1917. Reminiscences from the Revolutionary Underground.
Description: This source describes the methods used by socialist workers to organise meetings and spread propaganda in factories and workshops in pre-revolutionary Russia, highlighting the need for spontaneous, organised actions due to government policing.

On Sunday, 22 January 1905, a procession of workers led by Father Gapon marched to the Winter Palace but was attacked by police and Cossacks. This incident, known as **Bloody Sunday**, resulted in over 100 workers killed and about 300 wounded, initiating the 1905 Revolution. Strikes spread across the country, and universities closed as students protested the lack of civil liberties. Middle-class professionals formed the Union of Unions, demanding a constituent assembly.

During the 1905 Revolution, the Tsar agreed to create an elected consultative Parliament, the **Duma**. For a brief period, numerous trade unions and factory committees emerged. However, after 1905, most operated unofficially as they were declared illegal. Severe restrictions were placed on political activity. The Tsar quickly dismissed the first Duma (within 75 days) and the second (within three months), unwilling to tolerate any challenge to his authority. He altered voting laws to pack the third Duma with conservative politicians, excluding liberals and revolutionaries.

## The First World War and the Russian Empire

In 1914, the **First World War** broke out between two major alliances: the Central Powers (Germany, Austria, Turkey) and the Allied Powers (France, Britain, Russia, later joined by Italy and Romania). As countries had global empires, the war extended beyond Europe.

Initially, the war was popular in Russia, with people supporting Tsar Nicholas II. However, as the war continued, the Tsar's refusal to consult the Duma eroded public support. Anti-German sentiment led to the renaming of St Petersburg (a German name) to Petrograd. The unpopularity of the autocracy was further fuelled by Tsarina Alexandra's German origins and the influence of advisors like the monk Rasputin.

The war on the 'eastern front' (involving Russia) differed significantly from the 'western front'. While western armies were entrenched, eastern armies moved more, resulting in large casualties. Russia's armies suffered heavy defeats against Germany and Austria between 1914 and 1916, leading to over 7 million casualties by 1917. As Russian troops retreated, they destroyed crops and buildings to prevent the enemy from using them, creating over 3 million refugees in Russia. This situation severely discredited the government and the Tsar, and soldiers became unwilling to fight.

The war also devastated Russian industry. Russia had few industries of its own and was cut off from supplies by German control of the Baltic Sea. Industrial equipment deteriorated rapidly. Railway lines began to break down by 1916. The conscription of able-bodied men led to labour shortages, forcing the closure of small workshops producing essentials. Large quantities of grain were diverted to the army, causing scarcity of bread and flour in cities. By the winter of 1916, bread riots were common.
