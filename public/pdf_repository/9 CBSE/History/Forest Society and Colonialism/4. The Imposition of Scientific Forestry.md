This section explains how the British colonial government, concerned about forest depletion, introduced a system of 'scientific forestry' to manage forests primarily for timber production, leading to new laws and institutions.

# The Imposition of Scientific Forestry

The British required vast amounts of timber for shipbuilding and railways and were concerned that use by local people and unregulated felling by traders would destroy the forests. To address this, they invited **Dietrich Brandis**, a German expert, and appointed him as the first **Inspector General of Forests in India**.

Brandis recognized the need for a structured system to manage forests and train people in conservation science. This system required legal backing. Rules were framed to regulate forest resource use, restricting tree felling and grazing to preserve forests for timber. Anyone cutting trees outside this system faced punishment.

Brandis established the **Indian Forest Service** in 1864 and contributed to formulating the **Indian Forest Act of 1865**. The **Imperial Forest Research Institute** was founded at Dehradun in 1906. The forest management system taught here was termed '**scientific forestry**'. Today, many, including ecologists, question whether this system was truly scientific.

In scientific forestry, diverse natural forests were cleared and replaced with plantations of a single tree type planted in straight rows. Forest officials surveyed forests, estimated tree types and areas, and created working plans. These plans dictated how much plantation area to cut annually and replant for future harvests.

The Forest Act of 1865 was amended in 1878 and 1927. The 1878 Act categorized forests into three types: **reserved, protected, and village forests**. 'Reserved forests' were the best quality, and villagers were prohibited from taking anything from them, even for personal use. Wood for housing or fuel could only be taken from protected or village forests.
