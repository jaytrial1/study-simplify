This section examines how the new forest laws drastically changed the lives of forest communities, restricting their access to resources, impacting their traditional practices like gathering and shifting cultivation, and altering hunting rights.

# Impact of Forest Laws on Traditional Forest Life

The introduction of scientific forestry and the new Forest Act had profound effects on people living in and around forests.

## Different Ideas of a Forest

Foresters and villagers held contrasting views on what constituted a 'good' forest. Villagers preferred forests with a variety of tree species to meet diverse needs for fuel, fodder, and leaves. The forest department, however, prioritized trees suitable for shipbuilding and railways—hardwood, tall, and straight species like teak and sal, which were promoted while others were cut down.

## Restriction of Resource Access

In forest areas, people traditionally used forest products like roots, leaves, fruits, and tubers for numerous purposes. Fruits and tubers provided nutrition, especially during the monsoon season before harvests. Herbs were used for medicine, wood for agricultural tools, bamboo for fences, baskets, and umbrellas, and dried gourds as water bottles. Forests provided materials for disposable plates (from leaves), ropes (from creepers), vegetable graters (from thorny bark), and oil for cooking and lamps (from mahua fruit).

The Forest Act imposed severe hardship on villagers. Everyday practices such as cutting wood for houses, grazing cattle, collecting fruits and roots, and hunting and fishing became illegal. People were forced to steal wood and faced punishment or had to bribe forest guards if caught. Women collecting fuelwood were particularly affected. Police constables and forest guards also commonly harassed people, demanding free food.

## Impact on Shifting Cultivation

A major impact of colonial rule was on the practice of **shifting cultivation** or **swidden agriculture**, a traditional method used in many parts of Asia, Africa, and South America. It has various local names like *lading* (Southeast Asia), *milpa* (Central America), *chitemene* or *tavy* (Africa), *chena* (Sri Lanka), and *dhya, penda, bewar, nevad, jhum, podu, khandad, kumri* (India).

In shifting cultivation, sections of the forest are cut and burnt in rotation. Seeds are sown in the ashes after the first monsoon rains, and crops are harvested later in the year. Plots are cultivated for a couple of years, then left fallow for 12 to 18 years to allow the forest to regenerate. A mix of crops is grown, such as millets in central India and Africa, manioc in Brazil, and maize and beans in Latin America.

European foresters considered shifting cultivation harmful. They believed land used this way could not grow timber trees for railways. Burning forests also posed the risk of fires spreading to valuable timber areas.

Shifting cultivation also made it difficult for the government to calculate taxes. Therefore, the government banned the practice. This led to many communities being forcibly removed from their forest homes. Some were compelled to adopt new occupations, while others resisted through various rebellions.

## Changes in Hunting Practices

Forest laws also altered hunting. Traditionally, forest dwellers hunted deer, partridges, and small animals for subsistence. This practice became illegal under the new laws, and those caught hunting were penalized for **poaching**.

While customary hunting was prohibited, hunting of large animals became a sport, particularly for the British and Indian royalty. Historically, hunting big game was part of court culture in India, depicted in Mughal paintings. However, under colonial rule, hunting increased so drastically that many species faced extinction. The British viewed large animals as symbols of a 'wild' society and believed killing them was part of 'civilizing' India. They offered rewards for killing tigers, wolves, and other large animals deemed threats to cultivators. Between 1875 and 1925, over 80,000 tigers, 150,000 leopards, and 200,000 wolves were killed for rewards. The tiger evolved into a hunting trophy. Notably, the Maharaja of Sarguja reportedly shot 1,157 tigers and 2,000 leopards by 1957, and a British administrator, George Yule, killed 400 tigers. Initially, some forest areas were reserved for hunting. Only much later did environmentalists and conservationists advocate for protecting these animal species.
