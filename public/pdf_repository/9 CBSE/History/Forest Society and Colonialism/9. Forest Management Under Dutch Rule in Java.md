# Forest Management Under Dutch Rule in Java

Java, now known for rice production, was once largely forested. The colonial power here was the Dutch, whose forest control laws bore many similarities to British laws in India. Java was where the Dutch initiated forest management, primarily seeking timber for shipbuilding. Around 1600, Java's population was estimated at 3.4 million, with many villages in fertile plains and communities in the mountains practicing shifting cultivation.

## The Woodcutters of Java: The Kalangs

The **Kalangs** of Java were a community highly skilled in forest cutting and shifting cultivation. Their expertise was so valued that when the Mataram kingdom split in 1755, the 6,000 Kalang families were divided equally between the two new kingdoms. Harvesting teak and building palaces would have been difficult without them. As the Dutch gained control in the eighteenth century, they tried to make the Kalangs work for them. In 1770, the Kalangs resisted by attacking a Dutch fort at Joana, but the uprising was suppressed.

## Dutch Scientific Forestry in Java

In the nineteenth century, as controlling territory became crucial, the Dutch enacted forest laws in Java that restricted villagers' access. Wood could only be cut for specific purposes like making boats or houses, from designated forests, under close supervision. Villagers faced punishment for grazing cattle in young tree stands, transporting wood without permits, or using forest roads with horse carts or cattle.

Similar to India, the demand for timber for shipbuilding and railways led to the creation of a forest service. In 1882, 280,000 sleepers were exported from Java. This large-scale operation required labour for cutting trees, transporting logs, and preparing sleepers. The Dutch initially imposed rents on land cultivated within forests but later exempted some villages from these rents if they collectively provided free labour and buffaloes for timber work. This was called the **blandongdiensten** system. Later, rent exemption was replaced by small wages, but the right to cultivate forest land was still limited.

## Resistance in Java: Samin's Challenge

Around 1890, **Surontiko Samin** of Randublatung village, a teak forest village, challenged the state's ownership of the forest. He argued that the state, not having created the wind, water, earth, and wood, could not own them. His ideas sparked a widespread movement, organized partly by his sons-in-law. By 1907, 3,000 families followed his teachings. Some Saminists protested by lying down on their land during Dutch surveys, while others refused to pay taxes, fines, or perform labour.

Yes, the forests of Java have timber enough to build a respectable navy in a short time, besides as many merchant ships as we require ... In spite of all (the cutting) the forests of Java grow as fast as they are cut, and would be inexhaustible under good care and management.'
Dirk van Hogendorp, cited in Peluso, Rich Forests, Poor People, 1992.
