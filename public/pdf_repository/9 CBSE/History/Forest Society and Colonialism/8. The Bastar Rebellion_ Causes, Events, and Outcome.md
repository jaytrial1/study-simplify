# The Bastar Rebellion: Causes, Events, and Outcome

## The People of Bastar

Bastar is located in the southernmost part of Chhattisgarh, bordering Andhra Pradesh, Orissa, and Maharashtra. The central area is a plateau, bordered by the Chhattisgarh plain to the north and the Godavari plain to the south. The Indrawati river flows east to west across Bastar. Various communities live here, including Maria and Muria Gonds, Dhurwas, Bhatras, and Halbas, who speak different languages but share customs and beliefs. Bastar people believe the Earth granted each village its land, and in return, they care for it, making offerings at agricultural festivals. They also respect the spirits of the river, forest, and mountain. Villages respect each other's boundaries and manage their natural resources. If a village needs wood from another, they pay a small fee (devsari, dand, or man). Some villages employ watchmen to protect their forests, paid with grain contributions from households. Annually, village headmen from a pargana (cluster of villages) hold a large hunt and discuss concerns, including forests.

## The Fears of the People

In 1905, the colonial government proposed reserving two-thirds of the forest, banning shifting cultivation, hunting, and forest produce collection. This caused great worry among the people of Bastar. Some villages were allowed to remain in the reserved forests as 'forest villages' on the condition they provided free labour for cutting and transporting trees and protecting forests from fire. Others were displaced without notice or compensation. Villagers had already suffered from increased land rents and demands for free labour and goods by colonial officials. Terrible famines in 1899-1900 and 1907-1908 worsened conditions. The forest reservation proposal was the final provocation.

People began discussing these issues in village councils, markets, and festivals where headmen and priests gathered. The Dhurwas of the Kanger forest, where reservation began, took the lead. Although there was no single leader, Gunda Dhur from village Nethanar was a prominent figure. In 1910, symbolic messages—mango boughs, earth lumps, chillies, and arrows—circulated among villages, inviting them to rebel. Every village contributed to the rebellion's costs. Markets were looted, houses of officials and traders, schools, and police stations were burnt and robbed, and grain was redistributed. Targets were mainly those associated with the colonial state and its oppressive laws. Missionary William Ward noted the influx of police, merchants, forest peons, schoolmasters, and immigrants into Jagdalpur.

Letter from DeBrett, Political Agent, Chhattisgarh Feudatory States to Commissioner, Chhattisgarh Division, 23 June 1910.

Elders in Bastar recounted stories of the battle passed down from their parents. Podiyami Ganga of Kankapal was told by his father, Podiyami Tokeli, that:
'The British came and started taking land. The Raja didn't pay attention to things happening around him, so seeing that land was being taken, his supporters gathered people. War started. His staunch supporters died and the rest were whipped. My father, Podiyami Tokeli suffered many strokes, but he escaped and survived. It was a movement to get rid of the British. The British used to tie them to horses and pull them. From every village two or three people went to Jagdalpur: Gargideva and Michkola of Chidpal, Dole and Adrabundi of Markamiras, Vadapandu of Baleras, Unga of Palem and many others.'

Chendru, an elder from Nandrasa village, said:
'On the people's side, were the big elders - Mille Mudaal of Palem, Soyekal Dhurwa of Nandrasa, and Pandwa Majhi. People from every pargana camped in Alnar tarai. The paltan (force) surrounded the people in a flash. Gunda Dhur had flying powers and flew away. But what could those with bows and arrows do? The battle took place at night. The people hid in shrubs and crawled away. The army paltan also ran away. All those who remained alive (of the people), somehow found their way home to their villages.'

The British sent troops to suppress the rebellion. Adivasi leaders attempted negotiation, but the British surrounded their camps and fired upon them. Afterward, troops marched through villages, flogging and punishing rebels. Many villages were deserted as people fled into the jungles. It took three months (February-May) for the British to regain control, but Gunda Dhur was never captured. A significant victory for the rebels was the temporary suspension of reservation work and a reduction in the area to be reserved to roughly half of the original plan.
