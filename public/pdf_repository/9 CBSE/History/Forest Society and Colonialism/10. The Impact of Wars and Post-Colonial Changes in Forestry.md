# The Impact of Wars and Post-Colonial Changes in Forestry

The First and Second World Wars significantly impacted forests. In India, working plans were abandoned, and the forest department extensively cut trees to meet British war needs. In Java, before the Japanese occupation, the Dutch implemented a **'scorched earth' policy**, destroying sawmills and burning large teak log piles to prevent them from falling into Japanese hands. The Japanese then exploited forests recklessly for their war industries, forcing villagers to cut trees. Many villagers seized this chance to expand cultivation in the forest. After the war, the Indonesian forest service struggled to reclaim this land. Similar to India, the need for agricultural land brought people into conflict with the forest department's desire to control land and exclude people.

## New Developments in Forestry

Since the 1980s, governments in Asia and Africa have recognized that scientific forestry and excluding forest communities have caused many conflicts. **Conservation** of forests is becoming a more important goal than just timber collection. Governments now acknowledge the necessity of involving people living near forests to achieve conservation goals. In India, from Mizoram to Kerala, dense forests have survived partly because villages protected them as **sacred groves**, known locally by names like *sarnas, denarakudu, kan, rai*. Some villages have organized their own forest patrols, with households taking turns, instead of relying solely on forest guards. Local forest communities and environmentalists are exploring different models of forest management today.
