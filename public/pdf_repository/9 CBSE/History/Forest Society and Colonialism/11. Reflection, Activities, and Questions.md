## Questions

1. Discuss how the changes in forest management in the colonial period affected the following groups of people:
    *   Shifting cultivators
    *   Nomadic and pastoralist communities
    *   Firms trading in timber/forest produce
    *   Plantation owners
    *   Kings/British officials engaged in shikar

> **Answer:**
> *   **Shifting cultivators:** Their traditional practice was banned, leading to forced displacement from their homes in the forests. Some had to change occupations, while others resisted.
> *   **Nomadic and pastoralist communities:** Their grazing and hunting practices were restricted, causing many to lose their livelihoods. Some were labelled 'criminal tribes' and forced into labor in factories, mines, and plantations under government supervision.
> *   **Firms trading in timber/forest produce:** Large European trading firms benefited significantly as the British government granted them sole rights to trade in forest products from specific areas, regulating the trade completely.
> *   **Plantation owners:** They benefited greatly as the colonial government took over large areas of natural forests and gave them to European planters at cheap rates to establish commercial plantations for tea, coffee, and rubber.
> *   **Kings/British officials engaged in shikar:** Hunting of big game became a major sport for them. While traditional hunting by locals was banned, these elites engaged in large-scale hunting, often receiving rewards for killing animals, leading to the near extinction of some species.

2. What are the similarities between colonial management of the forests in Bastar and in Java?

> **Answer:** Similarities between colonial forest management in Bastar (under British) and Java (under Dutch) include:
> *   Both colonial powers sought to gain control over forests, primarily for timber needed for shipbuilding and railways.
> *   Both introduced forest laws that restricted the access and traditional practices of local communities (like shifting cultivation, collecting forest produce, and hunting).
> *   Both implemented a system of 'scientific forestry' that involved clearing natural forests and establishing plantations of commercially valuable trees.
> *   Both relied on the labour of local communities for cutting and transporting timber, often under exploitative conditions (e.g., forced labour or low wages).
> *   Both faced resistance from local forest communities who were negatively impacted by the new laws and restrictions.

3. Between 1880 and 1920, forest cover in the Indian subcontinent declined by 9.7 million hectares, from 108.6 million hectares to 98.9 million hectares. Discuss the role of the following factors in this decline:
    *   Railways
    *   Shipbuilding
    *   Agricultural expansion
    *   Commercial farming
    *   Tea/Coffee plantations
    *   Adivasis and other peasant users

> **Answer:**
> *   **Railways:** Required vast amounts of wood for fuel and especially for sleepers (1,760 to 2,000 per mile of track), leading to extensive felling of trees along railway routes as the network expanded rapidly.
> *   **Shipbuilding:** The demand for strong, durable timber for the Royal Navy after the depletion of oak forests in England led to large-scale exploration and export of Indian timber from the early nineteenth century.
> *   **Agricultural expansion:** As the population increased and demand for food rose, forests were cleared to expand cultivation. The colonial state actively encouraged this process, viewing forests as unproductive land to be brought under the plough for revenue.
> *   **Commercial farming:** The British promoted the production of commercial crops like jute, sugar, wheat, and cotton to meet European demand, which required clearing more forest land for cultivation.
> *   **Tea/Coffee plantations:** Large areas of natural forests were cleared and replaced by tea, coffee, and rubber plantations to meet Europe's growing demand for these commodities. The colonial government facilitated this by giving forest lands to European planters cheaply.
> *   **Adivasis and other peasant users:** While their traditional practices like shifting cultivation were blamed and banned by the colonial government for forest depletion, the text indicates that the *scale* of deforestation driven by colonial demands (timber, agriculture, plantations) was far more systematic and extensive than the practices of local communities, who often used forests sustainably for generations. Their impact was minor compared to the large-scale commercial and infrastructural needs of the colonial state.

4. Why are forests affected by wars?

> **Answer:** Forests are affected by wars because conflicts increase the demand for timber and other forest products for military purposes, such as building ships, railway sleepers for transport, and other war industries. During wartime, standard forest management practices and working plans are often abandoned, leading to unregulated and excessive felling of trees to meet immediate needs. Additionally, strategies like 'scorched earth' policies might involve deliberately destroying forests to prevent resources from falling into enemy hands, and occupying forces may recklessly exploit forests for their own needs. The disruption caused by war can also lead to increased unauthorized use of forest land for cultivation as people face hardship.
