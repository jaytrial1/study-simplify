This section details the traditional method of measuring poverty using the poverty line, based on income or consumption levels, and contrasts it with the newer Multidimensional Poverty Index.

# The Concept of the Poverty Line

The **poverty line** is central to discussions about poverty. A common measurement method uses income or consumption levels. 'Consumption' refers to household spending on necessities like food, clothing, etc. A person is deemed poor if their income or consumption falls below a minimum level required for basic needs. This minimum level varies by time and country, reflecting different development levels and social norms. For example, owning a car might define poverty in the US but is a luxury in India.

In India, the poverty line was historically determined by considering minimum requirements for food, clothing, footwear, fuel, electricity, education, and medical needs. The monetary value of these requirements was calculated. The food requirement formula relied on a desired calorie intake:

*   **Rural areas:** 2400 calories per person per day (higher due to more physical work).
*   **Urban areas:** 2100 calories per person per day.

Food items like cereals, pulses, vegetables, milk, oil, and sugar provided these calories. The minimum monetary expenditure per person needed to buy these food items, along with other basic needs, was considered the poverty line. This amount was periodically revised based on price changes.

Following global trends, the Government of India now estimates poverty using the **National Multidimensional Poverty Index (NMPI)**, based on 12 developmental indicators. This index complements income/consumption measures by directly assessing deprivations.
