# Food Security in India

Since the **Green Revolution** in the early 1970s, India has successfully avoided famine, even during unfavorable weather conditions. India has become self-sufficient in foodgrains over the last 30 years due to the variety of crops grown nationwide.

Ensuring the availability of foodgrains throughout the country, even during adverse times, is managed by a carefully designed government **food security system**. This system has two main components:
*   **(a) buffer stock**
*   **(b) public distribution system**

## What is Buffer Stock?

**Buffer Stock** is the reserve of foodgrains, specifically wheat and rice, purchased and stored by the government through the **Food Corporation of India (FCI)**. The FCI buys wheat and rice from farmers in states that produce a surplus. Farmers are paid a **pre-announced price** for their crops, known as the **Minimum Support Price (MSP)**. The government declares the MSP annually before the sowing season to encourage farmers to increase production.

The purchased foodgrains are stored in granaries. This buffer stock is created to distribute foodgrains in areas facing shortages and among the poorer sections of society at a price lower than the market price. This lower price is called the **Issue Price**. Buffer stock also helps address food shortages during adverse weather or calamities.

## What is the Public Distribution System?

The foodgrains procured by the FCI are distributed through government-regulated shops called **ration shops** (or **Fair Price Shops**) to the poorer sections of society. This network is known as the **Public Distribution System (PDS)**. Ration shops are widespread, found in most localities, villages, towns, and cities across the country (about 5.5 lakh shops).

Fair Price Shops stock foodgrains, sugar, and kerosene for cooking. These items are sold at prices lower than the market price. Families with a **ration card** can buy a fixed quantity of these items each month (e.g., 35 kg of grains, 5 litres of kerosene, 5 kgs of sugar).

There are different types of ration cards:
*   **(a) Antyodaya cards** for the poorest of the poor.
*   **(b) BPL cards** for those below the poverty line.
*   **(c) APL cards** for all others (above poverty line).

The practice of **Rationing** in India began in the 1940s, prompted by the Bengal famine. The system was revived during acute food shortages in the 1960s before the Green Revolution. In the mid-1970s, based on high poverty levels, three major food intervention programmes were launched:
*   **Public Distribution System (PDS)** (strengthened)
*   **Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS)** (started experimentally in 1975)
*   **Food-for-Work (FFW)** (introduced in 1977-78)

Over time, new programmes have been launched or restructured. Many **Poverty Alleviation Programmes (PAPs)**, mostly rural, also include a food component. While some, like PDS and mid-day meals, are solely food security programmes, most PAPs enhance food security by increasing the income of the poor through employment.
