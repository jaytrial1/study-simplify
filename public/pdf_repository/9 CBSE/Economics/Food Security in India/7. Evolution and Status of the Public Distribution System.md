# The National Food Security Act, 2013

This Act aims to provide food and nutritional security at affordable prices, enabling people to live with dignity. Under this Act, 75% of the rural population and 50% of the urban population are identified as eligible households for food security benefits.

# Current Status of the Public Distribution System

The **Public Distribution System (PDS)** is the government's most significant step towards ensuring food security. Initially, PDS coverage was universal, serving both poor and non-poor without distinction. Over the years, the policy has been revised to make it more efficient and targeted.

*   In 1992, the **Revamped Public Distribution System (RPDS)** was introduced in 1,700 backward blocks to extend PDS benefits to remote areas.
*   From June 1997, the **Targeted Public Distribution System (TPDS)** was launched to focus on targeting the 'poor in all areas'. This was the first time a differential price policy was used for poor and non-poor households.
*   In 2000, two special schemes were introduced: **Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY)** for the 'poorest of the poor' and **Annapurna Scheme (APS)** for 'indigent senior citizens'. These schemes were linked to the existing PDS network.

The PDS has been effective in stabilizing prices and making food affordable for consumers. It has helped prevent widespread hunger and famine by moving food from surplus to deficit regions. Prices have been adjusted to favor poor households. The system, including MSP and procurement, has also helped increase foodgrain production and provided income security to farmers in certain areas.

## Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY)

The **Antyodaya Anna Yojana (AAY)** was launched in December 2000. It identified one crore of the poorest families among those covered under TPDS. These families, identified by state rural development departments through BPL surveys, initially received 25 kg of foodgrains per family per month at highly subsidized rates (Rs 2/kg for wheat, Rs 3/kg for rice). This quantity was increased to 35 kg from April 2002. The scheme was further expanded twice, covering 2 crore families in total.

*   Why were the FCI granaries overflowing with foodgrains?
    > **Answer:** FCI granaries were overflowing due to increased procurement of foodgrains at enhanced Minimum Support Prices (MSP), pressure from major foodgrain-producing states, and challenges in the Public Distribution System (PDS) leading to unsold stocks at ration shops.

In 2022, the Central pool stock of wheat and rice was 159 LMT and 104 LMT respectively, significantly more than the minimum buffer norms. The stocks have consistently remained higher than the norms. Distribution under government schemes has helped improve the situation. There is a general view that high buffer stocks are undesirable and can be wasteful, leading to high carrying costs, wastage, and deterioration of grain quality. Some suggest freezing MSP for a few years.
