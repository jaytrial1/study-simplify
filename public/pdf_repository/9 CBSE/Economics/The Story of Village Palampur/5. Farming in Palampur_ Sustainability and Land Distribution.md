This section examines the impact of modern farming on natural resources and how land is distributed among Palampur's farmers.

# Will the Land Sustain?

Land is a natural resource and must be used carefully. Scientific reports show that modern farming methods have overused the natural resource base.

The Green Revolution is linked to soil fertility loss in many areas due to excessive use of chemical fertilisers. Continuous use of groundwater for tubewell irrigation has lowered the water table. Environmental resources like soil fertility and groundwater take years to build up and are hard to restore once damaged. Protecting the environment is crucial for the future of agriculture.

# How is Land Distributed?

Land is essential for farming, but not everyone engaged in agriculture has enough land. In Palampur, about one-third of the 450 families (150 families), mostly Dalits, are landless.

Of the families who own land, 240 cultivate small plots less than 2 hectares. These plots don't provide adequate income for the farmer's family.

Consider Gobind, a farmer in 1960 with 2.25 hectares of mostly unirrigated land. He cultivated it with his three sons, managing to feed the family with a little extra income from a buffalo. After his death, the land was divided among his sons, each getting only 0.75 hectares. Even with improved irrigation and modern methods, they struggle to make a living and need additional work part of the year.

*   Why do so many families of farmers cultivate such small plots of land?
> **Answer:** Many families cultivate small plots because the land is distributed unequally. Over generations, land holdings are divided among heirs, leading to smaller and smaller plot sizes, especially for families who started with limited land.

*   Would you agree that the distribution of cultivated land is unequal in Palampur? Do you find a similar situation for India? Explain.
> **Answer:** Yes, the distribution of cultivated land in Palampur is unequal. 150 families are landless, 240 families cultivate very small plots (less than 2 hectares), while only 60 families cultivate large areas (more than 2 hectares). The graph provided for India (Graph 1.1) also shows a similar situation: a large majority of farmers (around 85%) are small farmers cultivating only a small portion of the total cultivated area (around 45%), while a small minority of medium and large farmers (around 15%) cultivate a large portion of the area (around 55%). This indicates unequal land distribution is common in India.
