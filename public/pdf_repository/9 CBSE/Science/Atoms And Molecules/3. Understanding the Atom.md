# Understanding the Atom

Have you observed a mason building walls, then rooms from these walls, and finally a building from a collection of rooms? What is the fundamental building block of the huge building? It's a brick. What about the building block of an ant-hill? It's a small grain of sand. Similarly, the fundamental building blocks of all matter are **atoms**.

## How big are atoms?

Atoms are incredibly small – smaller than anything we can imagine or compare them to easily. Millions of atoms stacked together would form a layer barely as thick as a sheet of paper.

## Modern Day Symbols of Atoms of Different Elements

Dalton was the first scientist to use symbols for elements, and his symbols represented not just the element but also a definite quantity – one atom of that element. Berzilius proposed using one or two letters from the element's name as symbols.

Initially, element names were often derived from the place they were discovered (e.g., copper from Cyprus) or from colours (e.g., gold from the English word for yellow). Today, the **IUPAC (International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry)** is the international body that approves names, symbols, and units for elements.

Most modern symbols are the first one or two letters of the element's English name. The first letter is always capital (uppercase), and the second is always small (lowercase).
*   hydrogen, H
*   aluminium, Al (not AL)
*   cobalt, Co (not CO)

Some symbols use the first letter and a later letter from the name, like chlorine (Cl) and zinc (Zn). Other symbols come from Latin, German, or Greek names. For example, iron's symbol Fe comes from the Latin *ferrum*, sodium's Na from *natrium*, and potassium's K from *kalium*. Thus, each element has a unique name and symbol.

## Atomic Mass

Dalton's atomic theory introduced the significant concept of **atomic mass**, stating that each element has a characteristic atomic mass. This concept helped explain the law of constant proportions, prompting scientists to measure atomic masses. Measuring the mass of a single atom was difficult, so relative atomic masses were determined based on the laws of chemical combination and the masses of compounds formed.

Consider carbon monoxide (CO), formed from carbon and oxygen. Experiments showed that 3 g of carbon combine with 4 g of oxygen. This means carbon combines with 4/3 times its mass of oxygen. If we defined the atomic mass unit ('u' - unified mass, formerly 'amu') as equal to the mass of one carbon atom, carbon would have an atomic mass of 1.0 u, and oxygen would have 1.33 u.

To get numbers closer to whole numbers, scientists initially used 1/16th of the mass of a naturally occurring oxygen atom as the unit. This was useful because oxygen forms compounds with many elements, and this unit gave whole number masses for most elements. However, in 1961, **carbon-12 isotope** was chosen as the universally accepted standard reference for atomic masses.

One atomic mass unit ('u') is defined as exactly one-twelfth (1/12th) the mass of one atom of carbon-12. Relative atomic masses of all elements are now measured with respect to an atom of carbon-12.

Imagine a fruit seller using a watermelon as a standard. He declares its mass is 12 units (watermelon units). He cuts it into twelve equal pieces and weighs other fruits relative to the mass of one piece (1/12th of the watermelon). He sells fruits by relative fruit mass unit (fmu).

## How Do Atoms Exist?

Atoms of most elements cannot exist independently. Atoms combine to form **molecules** or **ions**. These molecules or ions then aggregate in large numbers to form the matter we can see, feel, or touch.

### Questions

1. Define the atomic mass unit.
    > **Answer:** One atomic mass unit (u) is a mass unit equal to exactly one-twelfth (1/12th) the mass of one atom of carbon-12 isotope.

2. Why is it not possible to see an atom with naked eyes?
    > **Answer:** Atoms are extremely small, with radii typically around $10^{-10}$ meters. The resolving power of the human eye is limited, and objects need to be much larger than this (typically around 100 micrometers or $10^{-4}$ meters) to be visible without magnification. Therefore, individual atoms are far too small to be seen with the naked eye.
