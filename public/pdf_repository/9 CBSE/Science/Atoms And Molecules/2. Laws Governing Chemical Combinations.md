This section details the two fundamental laws established by Lavoisier and Proust that govern how elements combine to form compounds: the Law of Conservation of Mass and the Law of Constant Proportions.

# Laws Governing Chemical Combinations

Following extensive experiments by Lavoisier and Joseph L. Proust, two important laws of chemical combination were established.

## Law of Conservation of Mass

Is there a change in mass when a chemical change (chemical reaction) takes place?

What happens in the reaction flask?
> **Answer:** A chemical reaction takes place, often visible as a change in colour, formation of a precipitate (solid), or potentially gas evolution.

Do you think that a chemical reaction has taken place?
> **Answer:** Yes, the observation of changes (like precipitate formation or colour change) indicates a chemical reaction has occurred.

Why should we put a cork on the mouth of the flask?
> **Answer:** The cork is used to prevent any substances (like gases) from escaping the flask or anything from entering it. This ensures that the total mass of the system (reactants and products) is conserved and can be accurately measured before and after the reaction.

Does the mass of the flask and its contents change?
> **Answer:** According to the Law of Conservation of Mass, the total mass of the flask and its contents should remain the same before and after the chemical reaction.

The **Law of Conservation of Mass** states that mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.

## Law of Constant Proportions

Lavoisier and other scientists observed that many compounds were composed of two or more elements, and each compound always contained the same elements in the same proportions by mass, regardless of its source or method of preparation.

For instance, in water, the ratio of the mass of hydrogen to the mass of oxygen is always *1:8*, no matter where the water comes from. If 9 g of water is decomposed, 1 g of hydrogen and 8 g of oxygen are consistently obtained. Similarly, in ammonia, nitrogen and hydrogen are always present in a *14:3* ratio by mass, regardless of how it is obtained.

This observation led to the **Law of Constant Proportions**, also known as the **Law of Definite Proportions**. This law was stated by Proust as: "In a chemical substance the elements are always present in definite proportions by mass".

The challenge for scientists then was to explain these laws. British chemist John Dalton proposed a fundamental theory about the nature of matter. Dalton revived the philosophical idea of matter's divisibility and adopted the Greek term 'atoms' for the smallest particles of matter. His theory was based on the laws of chemical combination and successfully explained both the Law of Conservation of Mass and the Law of Definite Proportions.

John Dalton was born in England in 1766. He began teaching at a young age and later became a principal. He moved to Manchester in 1793 to teach science. In 1808, he presented his atomic theory, a major milestone in the study of matter.

According to **Dalton's atomic theory**, all matter (elements, compounds, or mixtures) is made of small particles called atoms. The key postulates of this theory are:
*   All matter is made of very tiny particles called **atoms**, which participate in chemical reactions.
*   Atoms are **indivisible** particles that cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.
*   Atoms of a given element are **identical** in mass and chemical properties.
*   Atoms of different elements have **different** masses and chemical properties.
*   Atoms combine in the ratio of **small whole numbers** to form compounds.
*   The **relative number and kinds of atoms** are constant in a given compound.

(Note: Later studies revealed that atoms are composed of even smaller particles, which you will learn about in a subsequent chapter.)

### Questions

1. In a reaction, 5.3 g of sodium carbonate reacted with 6 g of acetic acid. The products were 2.2 g of carbon dioxide, 0.9 g water and 8.2 g of sodium acetate. Show that these observations are in agreement with the law of conservation of mass.
    sodium carbonate + acetic acid $\rightarrow$ sodium acetate + carbon dioxide + water
    > **Answer:**
    > Mass of reactants = mass of sodium carbonate + mass of acetic acid
    > Mass of reactants = 5.3 g + 6 g = 11.3 g
    > Mass of products = mass of sodium acetate + mass of carbon dioxide + mass of water
    > Mass of products = 8.2 g + 2.2 g + 0.9 g = 11.3 g
    > Since the mass of reactants (11.3 g) equals the mass of products (11.3 g), these observations are in agreement with the Law of Conservation of Mass.

2. Hydrogen and oxygen combine in the ratio of 1:8 by mass to form water. What mass of oxygen gas would be required to react completely with 3 g of hydrogen gas?
    > **Answer:** The ratio of hydrogen to oxygen by mass in water is 1:8. This means for every 1 g of hydrogen, 8 g of oxygen are needed.
    > To react completely with 3 g of hydrogen gas, the mass of oxygen required would be 3 times the amount needed for 1 g of hydrogen.
    > Mass of oxygen = 3 g (hydrogen) * 8 (ratio) = 24 g.
    > Thus, 24 g of oxygen gas would be required.

3. Which postulate of Dalton's atomic theory is the result of the law of conservation of mass?
    > **Answer:** The postulate "Atoms are indivisible particles, which cannot be created or destroyed in a chemical reaction" is a direct result of the Law of Conservation of Mass. If atoms cannot be created or destroyed, then the total mass of atoms (and thus matter) remains constant during a reaction.

4. Which postulate of Dalton's atomic theory can explain the law of definite proportions?
    > **Answer:** The postulates "Atoms of different elements have different masses and chemical properties" and "Atoms combine in the ratio of small whole numbers to form compounds" and "The relative number and kinds of atoms are constant in a given compound" together explain the Law of Definite Proportions. Because atoms combine in fixed, simple whole-number ratios, and atoms of each element have a specific, constant mass, any given compound will always have the same elements combined in a fixed proportion by mass.
