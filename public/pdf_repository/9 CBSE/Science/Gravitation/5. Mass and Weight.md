This section clarifies the difference between mass and weight, explaining how weight is a force and how it varies with location.

# Mass and Weight

**Mass** is a measure of the amount of matter in an object. It is also a measure of the object's **inertia** – its resistance to changes in motion. The greater the mass, the greater the inertia. Mass is a fundamental property of an object and remains constant regardless of its location in the universe.

**Weight** is the force with which the Earth attracts an object. This force depends on the object's mass ($m$) and the acceleration due to gravity ($g$) at its location.

From Newton's second law, Force = mass × acceleration.
For weight ($W$), which is the gravitational force, the acceleration is $g$.
So, $W = m \times g$

The SI unit of weight is the same as that of force, which is the **newton (N)**. Weight is a vector quantity; it has both magnitude and direction (always acting vertically downwards towards the center of the Earth).

Since the value of $g$ is approximately constant at a given location on Earth, the weight of an object is directly proportional to its mass ($W \propto m$). This is why weighing machines measure mass by measuring weight.

However, unlike mass, weight is *not* constant. Because the value of $g$ varies slightly from place to place (e.g., poles vs. equator) and significantly from one celestial body to another (e.g., Earth vs. Moon), an object's weight changes depending on its location. Mass, however, stays the same.

### Weight of an Object on the Moon

The weight of an object on the Moon is the force with which the Moon attracts that object. Since the Moon has less mass than the Earth, it exerts a weaker gravitational force.

Let $m$ be the mass of an object.
Weight on the Moon ($W_m$) is given by $W_m = G \frac{M_m \times m}{R_m^2}$, where $M_m$ is the mass of the Moon and $R_m$ is the radius of the Moon.
Weight on the Earth ($W_e$) is given by $W_e = G \frac{M \times m}{R^2}$, where $M$ is the mass of the Earth and $R$ is the radius of the Earth.

Using the values for the masses and radii of the Earth and Moon:

| Celestial Body | Mass (kg)              | Radius (m)            |
| :------------- | :--------------------- | :-------------------- |
| Earth          | $5.98 \times 10^{24}$  | $6.37 \times 10^{6}$  |
| Moon           | $7.36 \times 10^{22}$  | $1.74 \times 10^{6}$  |

Calculating the ratio of $W_m$ to $W_e$:
$W_m = G \frac{7.36 \times 10^{22} \times m}{(1.74 \times 10^6)^2} \approx G \times m \times 2.43 \times 10^{10}$
$W_e = G \frac{5.98 \times 10^{24} \times m}{(6.37 \times 10^6)^2} \approx G \times m \times 1.47 \times 10^{11}$

$\frac{W_m}{W_e} \approx \frac{2.43 \times 10^{10}}{1.47 \times 10^{11}} \approx 0.165 \approx \frac{1}{6}$

This calculation shows that the weight of an object on the Moon is approximately one-sixth of its weight on the Earth.

$W_m = \frac{1}{6} W_e$

### Example: Weight on Earth

Mass of an object = 10 kg. What is its weight on the earth?
Given: $m = 10 \mathrm{~kg}$, $g = 9.8 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-2}$.
$W = m \times g = 10 \mathrm{~kg} \times 9.8 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-2} = 98 \mathrm{~N}$.
The weight of the object is 98 N.

### Example: Weight on Moon

An object weighs 10 N on the surface of the earth. What would be its weight on the surface of the moon?
Given: $W_e = 10 \mathrm{~N}$.
$W_m = \frac{1}{6} W_e = \frac{1}{6} \times 10 \mathrm{~N} \approx 1.67 \mathrm{~N}$.
The weight of the object on the Moon would be approximately 1.67 N.

### Questions

1. What are the differences between the mass of an object and its weight?
> **Answer:** Mass is the amount of matter in an object and is constant everywhere. Weight is the force of gravity acting on an object and varies depending on the strength of the gravitational field (i.e., location). Mass is measured in kilograms (kg), while weight is a force measured in newtons (N).

2. Why is the weight of an object on the moon $\frac{1}{6}{ }^{\text {th }}$ its weight on the earth?
> **Answer:** The weight of an object depends on the mass and radius of the celestial body it is on, as these determine the local acceleration due to gravity ($g$). The Moon has significantly less mass and a smaller radius compared to the Earth, resulting in a much weaker gravitational field. The calculation using the universal law of gravitation shows that the resulting gravitational force (weight) on the Moon is approximately one-sixth of that on the Earth.
