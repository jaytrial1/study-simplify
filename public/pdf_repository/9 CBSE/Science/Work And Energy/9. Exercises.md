This section contains questions and problems related to the concepts of work, energy, and power, allowing students to test their understanding of the chapter's content.

# Exercises

1.  Look at the activities listed below. Reason out whether or not work is done in the light of your understanding of the term 'work'.
    *   Suma is swimming in a pond.
    *   A donkey is carrying a load on its back.
    *   A wind-mill is lifting water from a well.
    *   A green plant is carrying out photosynthesis.
    *   An engine is pulling a train.
    *   Food grains are getting dried in the sun.
    *   A sailboat is moving due to wind energy.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Suma is swimming in a pond:** Yes, work is done. Suma applies force on the water to propel herself, and her body is displaced through a distance.
> *   **A donkey is carrying a load on its back:** No scientific work is done *on the load* by the donkey while walking horizontally at a constant height. The donkey exerts an upward force to support the load, but the displacement is horizontal, so the force and displacement are perpendicular. However, the donkey does work *on itself* to move and overcome friction/air resistance, and work is done on the load if the donkey moves uphill or lifts it. Assuming horizontal motion, no work is done *on the load* by the supporting force.
> *   **A wind-mill is lifting water from a well:** Yes, work is done. The wind's force turns the blades, which powers a pump to lift water. A force is applied to the water (upwards), and the water is displaced vertically.
> *   **A green plant is carrying out photosynthesis:** No scientific work (in the physics sense of force x displacement) is done in the process of photosynthesis itself. It is a chemical process involving energy conversion (light to chemical).
> *   **An engine is pulling a train:** Yes, work is done. The engine exerts a force on the train, and the train is displaced over a distance.
> *   **Food grains are getting dried in the sun:** No scientific work is done *on the food grains* by the sun's energy in the physics sense of force x displacement. The sun's energy causes evaporation (a change of state).
> *   **A sailboat is moving due to wind energy:** Yes, work is done. The wind exerts a force on the sails, and the sailboat is displaced through the water.

2.  An object thrown at a certain angle to the ground moves in a curved path and falls back to the ground. The initial and the final points of the path of the object lie on the same horizontal line. What is the work done by the force of gravity on the object?

> **Answer:**
> The work done by gravity is given by $W_{gravity} = mg \times \Delta h$, where $\Delta h$ is the vertical displacement. Since the initial and final points are on the same horizontal line, the net vertical displacement ($\Delta h$) is zero. Therefore, the work done by the force of gravity on the object for the entire trajectory is zero. Gravity does negative work on the way up and positive work on the way down, and these amounts are equal and opposite when the object returns to the same height.

3.  A battery lights a bulb. Describe the energy changes involved in the process.

> **Answer:**
> In a battery, **chemical energy** is stored. When the battery is connected to a bulb, this chemical energy is converted into **electrical energy**. The electrical energy flows through the circuit to the bulb. In the bulb, the electrical energy is converted primarily into **light energy** and **heat energy**.

4.  Certain force acting on a 20 kg mass changes its velocity from $5 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ to $2 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$. Calculate the work done by the force.

> **Answer:**
> Mass of the object, $m = 20 \mathrm{~kg}$.
> Initial velocity, $u = 5 \mathrm{~m/s}$.
> Final velocity, $v = 2 \mathrm{~m/s}$.
> The work done by the force is equal to the change in kinetic energy of the object (Work-Energy Theorem).
> Initial kinetic energy, $E_{ki} = \frac{1}{2} m u^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 20 \mathrm{~kg} \times (5 \mathrm{~m/s})^2 = 10 \mathrm{~kg} \times 25 \mathrm{~m^2/s^2} = 250 \mathrm{~J}$.
> Final kinetic energy, $E_{kf} = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 20 \mathrm{~kg} \times (2 \mathrm{~m/s})^2 = 10 \mathrm{~kg} \times 4 \mathrm{~m^2/s^2} = 40 \mathrm{~J}$.
> Work done, $W = \Delta E_k = E_{kf} - E_{ki} = 40 \mathrm{~J} - 250 \mathrm{~J} = -210 \mathrm{~J}$.
> The work done by the force is -210 J. The negative sign indicates that the force acted opposite to the direction of motion, causing the object to slow down.

5.  A mass of 10 kg is at a point A on a table. It is moved to a point B. If the line joining A and B is horizontal, what is the work done on the object by the gravitational force? Explain your answer.

> **Answer:**
> The force of gravity acts vertically downwards. The displacement from point A to point B is horizontal. Since the gravitational force and the displacement are perpendicular to each other (at a 90-degree angle), the work done by the gravitational force on the object is zero. Work done = Force $\times$ Displacement $\times \cos(\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the angle between force and displacement. Here $\theta = 90^\circ$, and $\cos(90^\circ) = 0$, so Work done = $mg \times s \times 0 = 0$.

6.  The potential energy of a freely falling object decreases progressively. Does this violate the law of conservation of energy? Why?

> **Answer:**
> No, this does not violate the law of conservation of energy. As a freely falling object loses height, its gravitational potential energy ($mgh$) decreases. However, as it falls, its speed increases, and its kinetic energy ($\frac{1}{2}mv^2$) increases by an equal amount. The decrease in potential energy is converted into an increase in kinetic energy. The sum of the potential energy and kinetic energy (the total mechanical energy) remains constant throughout the fall (assuming no air resistance).

7.  What are the various energy transformations that occur when you are riding a bicycle?

> **Answer:**
> When riding a bicycle:
> *   **Chemical energy** stored in your food is converted into **muscle energy** (a form of chemical/mechanical energy).
> *   Your muscles do work, converting this energy into **mechanical energy** (kinetic energy of your legs and the bicycle parts).
> *   This mechanical energy is transferred through the pedals, chain, and wheels, resulting in the **kinetic energy** of the moving bicycle and yourself.
> *   Some energy is lost as **heat energy** due to friction in moving parts (like the chain, bearings, tires on the road) and air resistance, and also as heat generated by your muscles.

8.  Does the transfer of energy take place when you push a huge rock with all your might and fail to move it? Where is the energy you spend going?

> **Answer:**
> Yes, transfer of energy does take place, but not as work done *on the rock* to displace it. You expend energy from your body (chemical energy in muscles) to exert the force. This energy is converted into **internal energy** within your muscles, causing them to heat up and become fatigued. So, the energy is spent as work done *by your muscles* and converted into heat, but no mechanical work is done on the rock itself because there is no displacement.

9.  A certain household has consumed 250 units of energy during a month. How much energy is this in joules?

> **Answer:**
> One unit of energy is equal to 1 kilowatt-hour (kWh).
> $1 \mathrm{~kWh} = 1 \mathrm{~kW} \times 1 \mathrm{~hour}$
> $1 \mathrm{~kW} = 1000 \mathrm{~W} = 1000 \mathrm{~J/s}$.
> $1 \mathrm{~hour} = 60 \mathrm{~minutes} \times 60 \mathrm{~seconds} = 3600 \mathrm{~s}$.
> So, $1 \mathrm{~kWh} = 1000 \mathrm{~J/s} \times 3600 \mathrm{~s} = 3,600,000 \mathrm{~J} = 3.6 \times 10^6 \mathrm{~J}$.
> The household consumed 250 units.
> Total energy in Joules = 250 units $\times 3.6 \times 10^6 \mathrm{~J/unit}$
> Total energy = $900 \times 10^6 \mathrm{~J} = 9 \times 10^8 \mathrm{~J}$.
> The energy consumed is $9 \times 10^8$ Joules.

10. An object of mass 40 kg is raised to a height of 5 m above the ground. What is its potential energy? If the object is allowed to fall, find its kinetic energy when it is half-way down.

> **Answer:**
> Mass of the object, $m = 40 \mathrm{~kg}$.
> Height raised, $h = 5 \mathrm{~m}$.
> Take $g \approx 9.8 \mathrm{~m/s^2}$ (or $10 \mathrm{~m/s^2}$ if not specified, let's use 9.8).
> Potential energy at height $h$: $E_p = mgh = 40 \mathrm{~kg} \times 9.8 \mathrm{~m/s^2} \times 5 \mathrm{~m} = 1960 \mathrm{~J}$.
>
> Total energy when raised = Potential Energy + Kinetic Energy = $1960 \mathrm{~J} + 0 \mathrm{~J} = 1960 \mathrm{~J}$.
> According to the law of conservation of energy, the total energy remains 1960 J during the fall (ignoring air resistance).
> Half-way down means the height is $h' = 5 \mathrm{~m} / 2 = 2.5 \mathrm{~m}$ above the ground.
> At this height, the potential energy is $E_p' = mgh' = 40 \mathrm{~kg} \times 9.8 \mathrm{~m/s^2} \times 2.5 \mathrm{~m} = 980 \mathrm{~J}$.
> The kinetic energy at this point can be found using the conservation of energy:
> Total Energy = Potential Energy at half-way + Kinetic Energy at half-way
> $1960 \mathrm{~J} = 980 \mathrm{~J} + E_k'$
> $E_k' = 1960 \mathrm{~J} - 980 \mathrm{~J} = 980 \mathrm{~J}$.
> When the object is half-way down, its kinetic energy is 980 J.

11. What is the work done by the force of gravity on a satellite moving round the earth? Justify your answer.

> **Answer:**
> For a satellite moving in a circular orbit around the Earth at a constant speed, the force of gravity is always directed towards the center of the Earth (towards the Earth's radius). The displacement of the satellite at any instant is along the tangent to the circular path, which is perpendicular to the radius and thus perpendicular to the gravitational force. Since the force and displacement are perpendicular, the work done by the force of gravity on the satellite in a circular orbit is zero.

12. Can there be displacement of an object in the absence of any force acting on it? Think. Discuss this question with your friends and teacher.

> **Answer:**
> Yes, there can be displacement of an object in the absence of any *net* force acting on it. According to Newton's first law of motion, an object in motion will continue in motion with a constant velocity (constant speed and direction) if no net force acts on it. If an object is already moving, and the net force becomes zero, it will continue to move and thus be displaced, even though no net force is currently acting to *change* its motion. For example, a hockey puck sliding on frictionless ice after being hit will continue to slide at constant velocity, covering distance (displacement) without a net force.

13. A person holds a bundle of hay over his head for 30 minutes and gets tired. Has he done some work or not? Justify your answer.

> **Answer:**
> In the scientific sense of work (force causing displacement), the person has done **no work** on the bundle of hay while holding it still. The person exerts an upward force to support the bundle, but the bundle is not displaced (it remains at the same height). However, the person gets tired because their muscles are constantly contracting and relaxing at a microscopic level to maintain the force, and this muscular activity requires energy, which is converted into heat within the body. So, energy is expended by the person, but no work is done on the bundle of hay.

14. An electric heater is rated 1500 W. How much energy does it use in 10 hours?

> **Answer:**
> Power of the heater, $P = 1500 \mathrm{~W}$.
> Time duration, $t = 10 \mathrm{~hours}$.
> Convert time to seconds: $t = 10 \mathrm{~hours} \times 3600 \mathrm{~s/hour} = 36000 \mathrm{~s}$.
> Energy consumed = Power $\times$ Time
> Energy = $1500 \mathrm{~W} \times 36000 \mathrm{~s} = 1500 \mathrm{~J/s} \times 36000 \mathrm{~s} = 54,000,000 \mathrm{~J} = 5.4 \times 10^7 \mathrm{~J}$.
> Alternatively, using kilowatt-hours (kWh):
> Power in kW = $1500 \mathrm{~W} / 1000 \mathrm{~W/kW} = 1.5 \mathrm{~kW}$.
> Energy = Power $\times$ Time = $1.5 \mathrm{~kW} \times 10 \mathrm{~hours} = 15 \mathrm{~kWh}$.
> Converting kWh to Joules: $15 \mathrm{~kWh} \times 3.6 \times 10^6 \mathrm{~J/kWh} = 54 \times 10^6 \mathrm{~J} = 5.4 \times 10^7 \mathrm{~J}$.
> The electric heater uses $5.4 \times 10^7$ Joules (or 15 kilowatt-hours) of energy in 10 hours.

15. Illustrate the law of conservation of energy by discussing the energy changes which occur when we draw a pendulum bob to one side and allow it to oscillate. Why does the bob eventually come to rest? What happens to its energy eventually? Is it a violation of the law of conservation of energy?

> **Answer:**
> When a pendulum bob is drawn to one side, work is done against gravity, and it gains maximum **gravitational potential energy** at that highest point. Its kinetic energy is zero. When released, the potential energy begins to convert into **kinetic energy** as it swings downwards. At the lowest point (mean position), the potential energy is minimum (or zero, depending on the reference), and the kinetic energy is maximum. As it swings up to the other side, kinetic energy converts back into potential energy, reaching maximum potential energy and zero kinetic energy at the highest point on the other side. This continuous transformation between potential and kinetic energy illustrates the conservation of mechanical energy (Potential Energy + Kinetic Energy = Constant) in an ideal scenario.
> The bob eventually comes to rest due to dissipative forces like **air resistance** and **friction** at the pivot. These forces do negative work, removing mechanical energy from the pendulum system.
> The lost mechanical energy is converted into **heat energy** (in the air and at the pivot).
> This is **not a violation** of the law of conservation of energy. The total energy of the *universe* (pendulum + air + pivot) is conserved. The mechanical energy of the pendulum system alone decreases, but it is transformed into other forms of energy, primarily heat, which are dispersed into the surroundings.

16. An object of mass, $m$ is moving with a constant velocity, $v$. How much work should be done on the object in order to bring the object to rest?

> **Answer:**
> The object has mass $m$ and is moving with constant velocity $v$. Its initial kinetic energy is $E_{ki} = \frac{1}{2} m v^2$.
> To bring the object to rest, its final velocity must be $v_f = 0$. Its final kinetic energy will be $E_{kf} = \frac{1}{2} m (0)^2 = 0$.
> The work done on the object is equal to the change in its kinetic energy (Work-Energy Theorem).
> Work done, $W = E_{kf} - E_{ki} = 0 - \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = -\frac{1}{2} m v^2$.
> The work required to bring the object to rest is $-\frac{1}{2} m v^2$. The negative sign indicates that the force doing the work must be in the opposite direction to the object's motion.

17. Calculate the work required to be done to stop a car of 1500 kg moving at a velocity of $60 \mathrm{~km/h}$?

> **Answer:**
> Mass of the car, $m = 1500 \mathrm{~kg}$.
> Initial velocity, $v = 60 \mathrm{~km/h}$.
> Convert velocity to m/s: $v = 60 \times \frac{1000 \mathrm{~m}}{3600 \mathrm{~s}} = \frac{60000}{3600} \mathrm{~m/s} = \frac{50}{3} \mathrm{~m/s}$.
> Initial kinetic energy, $E_{ki} = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 1500 \mathrm{~kg} \times (\frac{50}{3} \mathrm{~m/s})^2 = 750 \mathrm{~kg} \times \frac{2500}{9} \mathrm{~m^2/s^2} = \frac{1875000}{9} \mathrm{~J} = \frac{625000}{3} \mathrm{~J} \approx 208333.33 \mathrm{~J}$.
> To stop the car, the final velocity is 0 m/s, so the final kinetic energy is $E_{kf} = 0 \mathrm{~J}$.
> The work required to stop the car is the change in kinetic energy:
> Work done, $W = E_{kf} - E_{ki} = 0 \mathrm{~J} - \frac{625000}{3} \mathrm{~J} = -\frac{625000}{3} \mathrm{~J} \approx -208333.33 \mathrm{~J}$.
> The work required to be done is approximately -208333.33 J. This negative work is done by forces like friction and braking.

19. Soni says that the acceleration in an object could be zero even when several forces are acting on it. Do you agree with her? Why?

> **Answer:**
> Yes, I agree with Soni. Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. According to Newton's second law, acceleration is caused by the net force acting on an object ($a = F_{net}/m$). If several forces are acting on an object, but they are balanced such that their vector sum (the net force, $F_{net}$) is zero, then the acceleration of the object will be zero. In this case, the object will either remain at rest or continue to move with a constant velocity. For example, a book resting on a table has gravity pulling it down and the table pushing it up; these forces are balanced, resulting in zero net force and zero acceleration.

20. Find the energy in joules consumed in 10 hours by four devices of power 500 W each.

> **Answer:**
> Power of one device = 500 W.
> Total power of four devices = $4 \times 500 \mathrm{~W} = 2000 \mathrm{~W} = 2 \mathrm{~kW}$.
> Time duration, $t = 10 \mathrm{~hours}$.
> Energy consumed = Total Power $\times$ Time
> Energy = $2000 \mathrm{~W} \times 10 \mathrm{~hours}$.
> Convert time to seconds: $t = 10 \mathrm{~hours} \times 3600 \mathrm{~s/hour} = 36000 \mathrm{~s}$.
> Energy = $2000 \mathrm{~J/s} \times 36000 \mathrm{~s} = 72,000,000 \mathrm{~J} = 7.2 \times 10^7 \mathrm{~J}$.
> Alternatively, using kilowatt-hours:
> Energy = $2 \mathrm{~kW} \times 10 \mathrm{~hours} = 20 \mathrm{~kWh}$.
> Convert kWh to Joules: $20 \mathrm{~kWh} \times 3.6 \times 10^6 \mathrm{~J/kWh} = 72 \times 10^6 \mathrm{~J} = 7.2 \times 10^7 \mathrm{~J}$.
> The energy consumed is $7.2 \times 10^7$ Joules (or 20 kilowatt-hours).

21. A freely falling object eventually stops on reaching the ground. What happens to its kinetic energy?

> **Answer:**
> When a freely falling object hits the ground and stops, its kinetic energy (which was maximum just before impact) is rapidly converted into other forms of energy. This energy is primarily transformed into **heat energy** (due to friction and deformation during impact), **sound energy** (the impact sound), and potentially **energy causing deformation** of the object or the ground. The kinetic energy is not destroyed but transferred and transformed.
