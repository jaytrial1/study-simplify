This section defines energy as the capacity to do work. It discusses various sources and forms of energy and introduces the concept of kinetic energy as the energy of motion.

# Energy

Life requires energy, and the demand for it is constantly growing. The Sun is our primary natural source of energy, and many other sources are derived from it. Energy can also come from atomic nuclei, the Earth's interior, and tides.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Other sources of energy:** Wind, flowing water (hydro energy), biomass, geothermal energy, nuclear energy, fossil fuels (coal, petroleum, natural gas), tidal energy, wave energy.
> *   **Sources due to the Sun:**
>     *   Solar energy (direct sunlight).
>     *   Biomass (plants store solar energy through photosynthesis).
>     *   Fossil fuels (formed from ancient plants and animals which got energy from the sun).
>     *   Wind energy (caused by uneven heating of the atmosphere by the sun).
>     *   Hydro energy (water cycle driven by solar heating causing evaporation).
> *   **Sources not due to the Sun:**
>     *   Nuclear energy (from atomic nuclei).
>     *   Geothermal energy (from Earth's internal heat).
>     *   Tidal energy (from gravitational pull of the moon and sun).

The word **energy** is commonly used, but in science, it has a precise meaning. Consider these examples:
*   A fast-moving cricket ball hitting a stationary wicket throws it away.
*   An object raised to a height can do work (e.g., falling).
*   A raised hammer falling on a nail drives it into wood.
*   Winding a toy car spring allows it to move.
*   Pressing a balloon can change its shape or even make it explode.

In these examples, objects gain the **capability of doing work** through different means. An object with the capacity to do work is said to possess **energy**. When an object does work, it loses energy, and the object on which work is done gains energy.

An object with energy can exert a force on another object. This transfers energy. The second object may then move and do work. Thus, possessing energy gives an object the capacity to do work.

The energy possessed by an object is measured by its capacity to do work. The unit of energy is the same as work: the **joule (J)**. 1 J is the energy needed to do 1 joule of work. A larger unit, the **kilojoule (kJ)**, is sometimes used: 1 kJ = 1000 J.

### Forms of ENERGY

Energy exists in many forms, including:
*   **Mechanical energy** (which is the sum of potential energy and kinetic energy)
*   **Heat energy**
*   **Chemical energy**
*   **Electrical energy**
*   **Light energy**

A moving object can do work. An object moving faster can do more work than an identical object moving slower. Examples include a moving bullet, blowing wind, a rotating wheel, or a speeding stone. How does a bullet pierce a target? How does wind move windmill blades? Objects in motion possess energy called **kinetic energy**.

Examples of objects with kinetic energy include a falling coconut, a speeding car, a rolling stone, a flying aircraft, flowing water, blowing wind, and a running athlete. Kinetic energy is the energy an object possesses due to its motion. The kinetic energy of an object increases as its speed increases.

The kinetic energy of a body moving with a certain velocity is equal to the work done on it to make it acquire that velocity.

Let's derive an equation for kinetic energy. Consider an object of mass, $m$ moving with a uniform velocity, $u$. Let a constant force, $F$ act on it in the direction of its displacement $s$, causing its velocity to change from $u$ to $v$. Let $a$ be the acceleration produced.
From the equation of motion relating initial velocity ($u$), final velocity ($v$), acceleration ($a$), and displacement ($s$):
$v^2 - u^2 = 2as$
Rearranging for $s$:
$s = \frac{v^2 - u^2}{2a}$ (Eq. 10.2)

From Newton's second law, $F = ma$.
Substituting $F=ma$ and the expression for $s$ into the work done equation $W = Fs$:
$W = ma \times \left(\frac{v^2 - u^2}{2a}\right)$
$W = \frac{1}{2} m (v^2 - u^2)$ (Eq. 10.3)

This equation shows that the work done on the object is equal to the change in its kinetic energy ($\frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mu^2$).

If the object starts from rest, its initial velocity $u=0$. In this case, the work done is:
$W = \frac{1}{2} m (v^2 - 0^2)$
$W = \frac{1}{2} m v^2$ (Eq. 10.4)

This work done is stored in the object as kinetic energy. Thus, the kinetic energy ($E_k$) possessed by an object of mass, $m$ moving with a uniform velocity, $v$ is:
$E_k = \frac{1}{2} m v^2$ (Eq. 10.5)

**Example 10.3** An object of mass 15 kg is moving with a uniform velocity of 4 $\mathrm{m} \mathrm{s}^{-1}$. What is the kinetic energy possessed by the object?

**Solution:**
Mass of the object, $m=15 \mathrm{~kg}$.
Velocity of the object, $v=4 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$.
From Eq. (10.5),
$E_k = \frac{1}{2} m v^2$
$E_k = \frac{1}{2} \times 15 \mathrm{~kg} \times (4 \mathrm{~m/s})^2$
$E_k = \frac{1}{2} \times 15 \mathrm{~kg} \times 16 \mathrm{~m^2/s^2}$
$E_k = 15 \mathrm{~kg} \times 8 \mathrm{~m^2/s^2} = 120 \mathrm{~kg \ m^2/s^2} = 120 \mathrm{~J}$.
The kinetic energy of the object is 120 J.

**Example 10.4** What is the work to be done to increase the velocity of a car from $30 \mathrm{~km} \mathrm{~h}^{-1}$ to $60 \mathrm{~km} \mathrm{~h}^{-1}$ if the mass of the car is 1500 kg?

**Solution:**
Mass of the car, $m=1500 \mathrm{~kg}$.
Initial velocity, $u = 30 \mathrm{~km/h}$. Convert to m/s:
$u = 30 \times \frac{1000 \mathrm{~m}}{3600 \mathrm{~s}} = \frac{30000}{3600} \mathrm{~m/s} = \frac{300}{36} \mathrm{~m/s} = \frac{25}{3} \mathrm{~m/s}$.
Final velocity, $v = 60 \mathrm{~km/h}$. Convert to m/s:
$v = 60 \times \frac{1000 \mathrm{~m}}{3600 \mathrm{~s}} = \frac{60000}{3600} \mathrm{~m/s} = \frac{600}{36} \mathrm{~m/s} = \frac{50}{3} \mathrm{~m/s}$.

Initial kinetic energy, $E_{ki} = \frac{1}{2} m u^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 1500 \mathrm{~kg} \times (\frac{25}{3} \mathrm{~m/s})^2$
$E_{ki} = 750 \mathrm{~kg} \times \frac{625}{9} \mathrm{~m^2/s^2} = \frac{468750}{9} \mathrm{~J} = \frac{156250}{3} \mathrm{~J}$.

Final kinetic energy, $E_{kf} = \frac{1}{2} m v^2 = \frac{1}{2} \times 1500 \mathrm{~kg} \times (\frac{50}{3} \mathrm{~m/s})^2$
$E_{kf} = 750 \mathrm{~kg} \times \frac{2500}{9} \mathrm{~m^2/s^2} = \frac{1875000}{9} \mathrm{~J} = \frac{625000}{3} \mathrm{~J}$.

The work done is equal to the change in kinetic energy:
$W = E_{kf} - E_{ki} = \frac{625000}{3} \mathrm{~J} - \frac{156250}{3} \mathrm{~J} = \frac{468750}{3} \mathrm{~J} = 156250 \mathrm{~J}$.
The work done is 156250 J.

3. The kinetic energy of an object of mass, $m$ moving with a velocity of $5 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ is 25 J . What will be its kinetic energy when its velocity is doubled? What will be its kinetic energy when its velocity is increased three times?

> **Answer:**
> 1.  The kinetic energy of an object is the energy it possesses due to its motion.
> 2.  The expression for the kinetic energy of an object of mass $m$ moving with velocity $v$ is $E_k = \frac{1}{2} m v^2$.
> 3.  Given $E_k = 25 \mathrm{~J}$ when $v = 5 \mathrm{~m/s}$. Using $E_k = \frac{1}{2} m v^2$, we have $25 = \frac{1}{2} m (5)^2 = \frac{1}{2} m (25)$. This means $\frac{1}{2} m = \frac{25}{25} = 1$. So, $m = 2 \mathrm{~kg}$.
>     *   When velocity is doubled, $v' = 2 \times 5 \mathrm{~m/s} = 10 \mathrm{~m/s}$.
>         New kinetic energy, $E_k' = \frac{1}{2} m (v')^2 = \frac{1}{2} (2 \mathrm{~kg}) (10 \mathrm{~m/s})^2 = 1 \mathrm{~kg} \times 100 \mathrm{~m^2/s^2} = 100 \mathrm{~J}$.
>         The kinetic energy will be 100 J.
>     *   When velocity is increased three times, $v'' = 3 \times 5 \mathrm{~m/s} = 15 \mathrm{~m/s}$.
>         New kinetic energy, $E_k'' = \frac{1}{2} m (v'')^2 = \frac{1}{2} (2 \mathrm{~kg}) (15 \mathrm{~m/s})^2 = 1 \mathrm{~kg} \times 225 \mathrm{~m^2/s^2} = 225 \mathrm{~J}$.
>         The kinetic energy will be 225 J.
