This section explains the scientific definition of work, contrasting it with the everyday use of the term. It establishes the conditions required for work to be done in physics.

# Work

What is work? There is a difference in how we use the term 'work' in day-to-day life and in science.

### Not Much 'work' in SPite of WORKING HARD!

Consider Kamali preparing for exams. She studies, reads, draws diagrams, organizes thoughts, collects papers, attends classes, discusses problems, and performs experiments. She expends a lot of energy. In common language, she is 'working hard'. However, scientifically, this 'hard work' might involve very little 'work'.

If you push a huge rock with great effort but it doesn't move, you get exhausted. Scientifically, you have done no work on the rock because there was no displacement.

If you stand still for minutes with a heavy load on your head, you get tired and spend energy. But in the scientific sense, no work is done on the load because there is no displacement of the load.

Climbing stairs to reach a building's second floor or climbing a tall tree involves a lot of work according to the scientific definition.

In daily life, any useful physical or mental effort is often considered work. Activities like playing, talking, humming, watching a movie, or attending a function may not be considered work. The definition of 'work' depends on how it is used. In science, the term work is defined differently.

> *   **Climbing steps or a tree:** Scientific work is done. (i) Work is being done on your body (or the object you are moving). (ii) Your body (or the object) is being displaced vertically against gravity. (iii) You are doing the work.

### SCIENTIFIC CONCEPTION OF WORK

From a scientific perspective, work is done when a force causes displacement. Consider these situations:

*   Pushing a pebble: You exert a force, and the pebble moves a distance. Force is applied, and the pebble is displaced. Work is done.
*   Pulling a trolley: A girl pulls a trolley, and it moves a distance. She exerts a force, and the trolley is displaced. Work is done.
*   Lifting a book: You apply a force to lift a book, and it moves up. Force is applied, and the book is displaced. Work is done.

These examples show that two conditions are needed for work to be done:
1.  A **force** must act on an object.
2.  The object must be **displaced**.

If either condition is not met, no scientific work is done.

Consider a bullock pulling a cart that moves. There is a force on the cart, and it moves. In this situation, work is done by the bullock on the cart.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Example:** Kicking a football.
>     *   Is work being done? Yes.
>     *   Force acting on the object? The force of your kick.
>     *   Object on which work is done? The football.
>     *   What happens to the object? It moves (changes velocity).
> *   **Example:** Pushing a swing.
>     *   Is work being done? Yes.
>     *   Force acting on the object? The force of your push.
>     *   Object on which work is done? The swing.
>     *   What happens to the object? It moves (swings).
> *   **Example:** A car driving on a flat road at constant speed.
>     *   Is work being done by the engine? Yes, against friction and air resistance.
>     *   Force acting on the object? Engine's force (and friction/air resistance).
>     *   Object on which work is done? The car.
>     *   What happens to the object? It moves. (Note: If considering net force at constant velocity, net work is zero, but engine does positive work against resistive forces).

## Activity

- Think of situations when the object is not displaced in spite of a force acting on it.
- Also think of situations when an object gets displaced in the absence of a force acting on it.
- List all the situations that you can think of for each.
- Discuss with your friends whether work is done in these situations.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Situations with force, but no displacement (No work done):**
>     *   Pushing a wall.
>     *   Holding a heavy book still.
>     *   A tug-of-war where neither side moves.
>     *   A tree standing in the wind (wind exerts force, but the tree doesn't displace).
> *   **Situations with displacement, potentially no *net* force (Work might have been done previously, or other forces balance):**
>     *   An object moving at a constant velocity in space (no friction, no air resistance - requires no *net* force to *continue* moving, but work was done to *start* its motion).
>     *   A hockey puck sliding across frictionless ice after being hit (again, work was done by the stick to displace it initially).
>     *   A satellite orbiting the Earth (gravity exerts a force, but the displacement is perpendicular to the force, resulting in zero work by gravity in a circular orbit).
