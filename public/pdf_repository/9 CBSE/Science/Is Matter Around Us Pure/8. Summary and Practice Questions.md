This section provides a summary of the key concepts learned in the chapter and includes practice questions to test understanding.

# What you have learnt

*   A mixture contains more than one substance (element and/ or compound) mixed in any proportion.
*   Mixtures can be separated into pure substances using appropriate separation techniques.
*   A solution is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. The major component of a solution is called the solvent, and the minor, the solute.
*   The concentration of a solution is the amount of solute present per unit volume or per unit mass of the solution.
*   Materials that are insoluble in a solvent and have particles that are visible to naked eyes, form a suspension. A suspension is a heterogeneous mixture.
*   Colloids are heterogeneous mixtures in which the particle size is too small to be seen with the naked eye, but is big enough to scatter light. Colloids are useful in industry and daily life. The particles are called the dispersed phase and the medium in which they are distributed is called the dispersion medium.
*   Pure substances can be elements or compounds. An element is a form of matter that cannot be broken down by chemical reactions into simpler substances. A compound is a substance composed of two or more different types of elements, chemically combined in a fixed proportion.
*   Properties of a compound are different from its constituent elements, whereas a mixture shows the properties of its constituting elements or compounds.

2.  Write the steps you would use for making tea. Use the words solution, solvent, solute, dissolve, soluble, insoluble, filtrate and residue.

> **Answer to Q2:**
> 1. Take water as the **solvent** in a pot.
> 2. Heat the water.
> 3. Add tea leaves (which are largely **insoluble** in water in terms of forming a true solution) and sugar (the **solute**, which is **soluble**).
> 4. Stir to help the sugar **dissolve**, forming a sugar **solution** in the water.
> 5. Add milk (which forms a colloid).
> 6. Boil the mixture.
> 7. Pour the mixture through a strainer or filter paper. The liquid that passes through is the **filtrate** (the tea). The tea leaves and other solid particles left behind on the strainer/filter paper are the **residue**.

4.  Explain the following giving examples.
    (a) Saturated solution
    (b) Pure substance
    (c) Colloid
    (d) Suspension

> **Answer to Q4:**
> (a) **Saturated solution:** A solution that has dissolved the maximum amount of solute possible at a specific temperature. No more solute can dissolve in it at that temperature. Example: Adding salt to water until no more salt dissolves, even after stirring.
> (b) **Pure substance:** Matter that consists of a single type of particle with uniform chemical nature. It has a fixed composition. Examples: Water (H₂O), Iron (Fe), Sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁).
> (c) **Colloid:** A heterogeneous mixture where tiny particles of one substance (dispersed phase) are evenly distributed throughout another substance (dispersion medium). The particles are too small to see with the naked eye but can scatter light (Tyndall effect). Examples: Milk, fog, smoke.
> (d) **Suspension:** A heterogeneous mixture where solid particles are dispersed in a liquid or gas but do not dissolve. The particles are large enough to be seen with the naked eye and will settle out over time. Examples: Muddy water, sand in water, chalk powder in water.

5.  Classify each of the following as a homogeneous or heterogeneous mixture.
    soda water, wood, air, soil, vinegar, filtered tea.

> **Answer to Q5:**

> *   filtered tea: **Homogeneous mixture** (After filtering out tea leaves, the dissolved substances form a uniform solution)

6.  How would you confirm that a colourless liquid given to you is pure water?

> **Answer to Q6:** You can confirm if a colourless liquid is pure water by checking its physical properties, specifically its boiling point and melting point. Pure water boils at exactly 100°C (at standard atmospheric pressure) and freezes at exactly 0°C. If the liquid boils and freezes precisely at these temperatures, and these temperatures remain constant during the phase change, it is likely pure water. A mixture would typically boil and freeze over a range of temperatures.

    (d) Coal
    (e) Soda water

> **Answer to Q8:** Solutions are homogeneous mixtures.

> (c) Air: **Solution** (Homogeneous mixture of gases)

> (e) Soda water: **Solution** (Carbon dioxide dissolved in water - homogeneous)

9.  Which of the following will show "Tyndall effect"?
    (a) Salt solution
    (b) Milk
    (c) Copper sulphate solution
    (d) Starch solution

> **Answer to Q9:** Tyndall effect is shown by colloids and suspensions, but not by true solutions.
> (a) Salt solution: **No** (True solution)
> (b) Milk: **Yes** (Colloidal solution)
> (c) Copper sulphate solution: **No** (True solution)
> (d) Starch solution: **Yes** (Often forms a colloidal solution, depending on preparation)
> The mixtures that will likely show the Tyndall effect are: (b) Milk and (d) Starch solution.

11. Which of the following are chemical changes?
    (a) Growth of a plant
    (b) Rusting of iron
    (c) Mixing of iron filings and sand
    (d) Cooking of food
    (e) Digestion of food
    (f) Freezing of water
    (g) Burning of a candle

> **Answer to Q11:** Chemical changes involve the formation of new substances.
> (a) Growth of a plant: **Chemical change** (Photosynthesis and other metabolic processes create new plant materials)
> (b) Rusting of iron: **Chemical change** (Iron reacts to form iron oxide)
> (c) Mixing of iron filings and sand: **Physical change** (Substances are mixed but remain chemically unchanged)
> (d) Cooking of food: **Chemical change** (Heat causes chemical reactions that change the composition and properties of food)
> (e) Digestion of food: **Chemical change** (Enzymes break down complex food molecules into simpler ones)
> (f) Freezing of water: **Physical change** (Water changes state from liquid to solid, but is still H₂O)
> (g) Burning of a candle: **Chemical change** (Wax reacts with oxygen to form new substances like CO₂, H₂O, etc.) - *Note: As discussed earlier, melting is physical, but the combustion itself is chemical.*
> Chemical changes from the list are: (a) Growth of a plant, (b) Rusting of iron, (d) Cooking of food, (e) Digestion of food, (g) Burning of a candle.
