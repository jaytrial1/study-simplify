This section explains how plants obtain essential nutrients and how adding manure and fertilizers can improve soil fertility and crop yield.

# Crop Production Management

Farming practices vary greatly in India depending on farm size, financial resources, and access to technology. Financial condition often dictates the inputs and technologies a farmer can use. Higher inputs generally correlate with higher yields. Production practices can range from 'no cost' to 'high cost'.

## Nutrient Management

Plants, like animals, need nutrients for growth, development, and well-being. These nutrients come from air, water, and soil. Air provides carbon and oxygen. Water provides hydrogen and oxygen. Soil provides thirteen other essential nutrients.

Nutrients required in large quantities are *macro-nutrients*. Those needed in small quantities are *micro-nutrients* (Table 12.1).

| Table 12.1: Nutrients supplied by air, water and soil |  |
| :--: | :--: |
| Source | Nutrients |
| Air | carbon, oxygen |
| Water | hydrogen, oxygen |
| Soil | (i) Macronutrients: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulphur <br> (ii) Micronutrients: iron, manganese, boron, zinc, copper, molybdenum, chlorine |

Deficiency of these nutrients negatively affects plant processes like reproduction, growth, and disease resistance. Soil fertility can be improved by adding manure and fertilizers to supply these nutrients and increase yield.

## Questions

1. What are macro-nutrients and why are they called macronutrients?

> **Answer:** Macro-nutrients are essential nutrients required by plants in relatively large quantities for their growth and development. They are called macro-nutrients because 'macro' means large. Examples include nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium.

2. How do plants get nutrients?

> **Answer:** Plants get nutrients from three main sources: air (carbon and oxygen), water (hydrogen and oxygen), and soil (thirteen other essential nutrients, including macro-nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulphur, and micro-nutrients like iron, manganese, boron, zinc, copper, molybdenum, chlorine).

## Manure

Manure contains large amounts of organic matter and small amounts of nutrients. It is made by decomposing animal excreta and plant waste. Manure improves soil fertility by adding nutrients and organic matter, which enhances soil structure. In sandy soils, it increases water holding capacity. In clayey soils, it improves drainage and prevents water logging.

Using manure recycles biological waste, protecting the environment from excessive fertilizer use. Manure types based on material used:
*   **Compost and vermi-compost:** Decomposition of farm waste (livestock excreta, vegetable waste, etc.) in pits (composting). *Vermi-compost* uses earthworms to speed up decomposition.
*   **Green manure:** Growing plants like sun hemp or guar and then ploughing them into the soil before sowing the main crop. This enriches the soil with nitrogen and phosphorus.

## Fertilizers

Fertilizers are commercially produced plant nutrients, primarily supplying nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. They promote good vegetative growth (leaves, branches, flowers) and healthy plants, contributing to higher yields in high-cost farming.

Fertilizers must be applied carefully (proper dose, time, precautions) for full utilisation. Excessive irrigation can wash away fertilizers, leading to water pollution.

Continuous fertilizer use can harm soil fertility by depleting organic matter and harming soil micro-organisms. Balancing the short-term benefits of fertilizers with the long-term benefits of manure for soil fertility is important for optimum yields.

## Question

1. Compare the use of manure and fertilizers in maintaining soil fertility.

> **Answer:** Manure adds large quantities of organic matter to the soil, improving soil structure, water holding capacity, and drainage, while also supplying small amounts of nutrients. Its benefits are long-term for soil health and it is environmentally friendly as it recycles waste. Fertilizers are commercially produced and provide specific nutrients (like N, P, K) in concentrated amounts, leading to rapid vegetative growth and high yields in the short term. However, continuous use can harm soil micro-organisms, reduce organic matter, and cause environmental pollution if not managed properly.
