1.  An object experiences a net zero external unbalanced force. Is it possible for the object to be travelling with a non-zero velocity? If yes, state the conditions that must be placed on the magnitude and direction of the velocity.
    > **Answer:** Yes, it is possible for an object to be travelling with a non-zero velocity even if it experiences a net zero external unbalanced force. According to Newton's first law, an object in motion will continue in motion with uniform velocity in a straight line unless acted upon by an unbalanced force. The condition is that the velocity must be constant in both magnitude and direction.

2.  When a carpet is beaten with a stick, dust comes out of it, Explain.
    > **Answer:** This happens due to inertia. The carpet is set into motion by the stick, but the dust particles on the carpet tend to remain in their state of rest due to inertia. As the carpet moves away, the dust particles are left behind and fall out.

3.  Why is it advised to tie any luggage kept on the roof of a bus with a rope?
    > **Answer:** Luggage on the roof of a bus should be tied with a rope to prevent it from moving or falling off due to inertia when the bus starts, stops, or turns. When the bus accelerates from rest, the luggage tends to stay at rest. When the bus brakes, the luggage tends to continue moving forward. When the bus turns, the luggage tends to continue moving in a straight line. Tying the luggage provides the necessary force to change its state of motion along with the bus.

4.  A batsman hits a cricket ball which then rolls on a level ground. After covering a short distance, the ball comes to rest. The ball slows to a stop because
    (a) the batsman did not hit the ball hard enough.
    (b) velocity is proportional to the force exerted on the ball.
    (c) there is a force on the ball opposing the motion.
    (d) there is no unbalanced force on the ball, so the ball would want to come to rest.
    > **Answer:** (c) there is a force on the ball opposing the motion. This opposing force is primarily friction between the ball and the ground, and air resistance. These forces cause an unbalanced force opposite to the direction of motion, slowing the ball down.

5.  A truck starts from rest and rolls down a hill with a constant acceleration. It travels a distance of 400 m in 20 s. Find its acceleration. Find the force acting on it if its mass is 7 tonnes (Hint: 1 tonne $= 1000 \mathrm{~kg}$.)
    > **Answer:**
    > Given: Initial velocity $u = 0 \mathrm{~m/s}$ (starts from rest), distance $s = 400 \mathrm{~m}$, time $t = 20 \mathrm{~s}$, mass $m = 7 \text{ tonnes} = 7 \times 1000 \mathrm{~kg} = 7000 \mathrm{~kg}$.
    >
    > (a) Find acceleration ($a$):
    > Using the kinematic equation $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$:
    > $400 \mathrm{~m} = (0 \mathrm{~m/s})(20 \mathrm{~s}) + \frac{1}{2}a(20 \mathrm{~s})^2$
    > $400 = 0 + \frac{1}{2}a(400)$
    > $400 = 200a$
    > $a = \frac{400}{200} = 2 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$
    > The acceleration is $2 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$.
    >
    > (b) Find the force ($F$) acting on it:
    > Using Newton's second law, $F = ma$:
    > $F = (7000 \mathrm{~kg}) \times (2 \mathrm{~m/s}^2)$
    > $F = 14000 \mathrm{~N}$
    > The force acting on the truck is 14000 N.

6.  A stone of 1 kg is thrown with a velocity of $20 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ across the frozen surface of a lake and comes to rest after travelling a distance of 50 m. What is the force of friction between the stone and the ice?
    > **Answer:**
    > Given: Mass $m = 1 \mathrm{~kg}$, initial velocity $u = 20 \mathrm{~m/s}$, final velocity $v = 0 \mathrm{~m/s}$ (comes to rest), distance $s = 50 \mathrm{~m}$.
    >
    > First, find the acceleration ($a$) using the kinematic equation $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$:
    > $(0 \mathrm{~m/s})^2 = (20 \mathrm{~m/s})^2 + 2a(50 \mathrm{~m})$
    > $0 = 400 + 100a$
    > $100a = -400$
    > $a = \frac{-400}{100} = -4 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$
    > The negative sign indicates deceleration.
    >
    > The force of friction ($F_f$) is the unbalanced force causing this deceleration. Using Newton's second law, $F = ma$:
    > $F_f = (1 \mathrm{~kg}) \times (-4 \mathrm{~m/s}^2)$
    > $F_f = -4 \mathrm{~N}$
    > The magnitude of the force of friction is 4 N. The negative sign indicates it acts opposite to the direction of motion.

7.  An 8000 kg engine pulls a train of 5 wagons, each of 2000 kg, along a horizontal track. If the engine exerts a force of 40000 N and the track offers a friction force of 5000 N, then calculate:
    (a) the net accelerating force and
    (b) the acceleration of the train.
    > **Answer:**
    > Given: Mass of engine $m_e = 8000 \mathrm{~kg}$. Mass of each wagon $m_w = 2000 \mathrm{~kg}$. Number of wagons = 5. Total mass of train (wagons) $m_{train} = 5 \times 2000 \mathrm{~kg} = 10000 \mathrm{~kg}$. Total mass of engine + train $M = m_e + m_{train} = 8000 \mathrm{~kg} + 10000 \mathrm{~kg} = 18000 \mathrm{~kg}$. Force exerted by engine $F_{engine} = 40000 \mathrm{~N}$. Friction force $F_{friction} = 5000 \mathrm{~N}$.
    >
    > (a) The net accelerating force ($F_{net}$) is the difference between the force exerted by the engine and the friction force:
    > $F_{net} = F_{engine} - F_{friction}$
    > $F_{net} = 40000 \mathrm{~N} - 5000 \mathrm{~N} = 35000 \mathrm{~N}$
    > The net accelerating force is 35000 N.
    >
    > (b) The acceleration ($a$) of the train can be found using Newton's second law, $F_{net} = Ma$, where $M$ is the total mass being accelerated (engine + train):
    > $35000 \mathrm{~N} = (18000 \mathrm{~kg}) \times a$
    > $a = \frac{35000 \mathrm{~N}}{18000 \mathrm{~kg}} = \frac{35}{18} \mathrm{~m/s}^2 \approx 1.94 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$
    > The acceleration of the train is approximately $1.94 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$.

8.  An automobile vehicle has a mass of 1500 kg. What must be the force between the vehicle and road if the vehicle is to be stopped with a negative acceleration of $1.7 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-2}$?
    > **Answer:**
    > Given: Mass $m = 1500 \mathrm{~kg}$. Acceleration $a = -1.7 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$ (negative acceleration means deceleration).
    >
    > Using Newton's second law, $F = ma$:
    > $F = (1500 \mathrm{~kg}) \times (-1.7 \mathrm{~m/s}^2)$
    > $F = -2550 \mathrm{~N}$
    > The force between the vehicle and the road (provided by the brakes and friction) must be -2550 N. The negative sign indicates the force is opposite to the direction of motion. The magnitude of the force is 2550 N.

9.  What is the momentum of an object of mass $m$, moving with a velocity $v$?
    (a) $(mv)^2$
    (b) $mv^2$
    (c) $1/2 mv^2$
    (d) $mv$
    > **Answer:** (d) $mv$. Momentum is defined as the product of mass and velocity.

10. Using a horizontal force of 200 N, we intend to move a wooden cabinet across a floor at a constant velocity. What is the friction force that will be exerted on the cabinet?
    > **Answer:** When an object moves at a constant velocity, its acceleration is zero. According to Newton's second law ($F_{net} = ma$), if $a=0$, then $F_{net}$ must be zero. The net force is the applied force minus the friction force (assuming they are in opposite directions).
    > $F_{net} = F_{applied} - F_{friction} = 0$
    > $F_{applied} = F_{friction}$
    > Given the applied force is 200 N, the friction force must be equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to balance it, resulting in a net force of zero and thus constant velocity.
    > The friction force exerted on the cabinet is 200 N.

11. According to the third law of motion when we push on an object, the object pushes back on us with an equal and opposite force. If the object is a massive truck parked along the roadside, it will probably not move. A student justifies this by answering that the two opposite and equal forces cancel each other. Comment on this logic and explain why the truck does not move.
    > **Answer:** The student's logic is incorrect because the action and reaction forces act on *different* objects, not on the same object. The force you exert on the truck acts on the truck, and the force the truck exerts on you acts on you. They do not cancel each other out because they are applied to different bodies.
    > The reason the massive truck does not move is because the force you exert on it is not large enough to overcome the static friction force between the truck's tires and the road. The static friction force opposes your push, and as long as your push is less than or equal to the maximum static friction force, the net force on the truck remains zero, and it stays at rest.

12. A hockey ball of mass 200 g travelling at $10 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ is struck by a hockey stick so as to return it along its original path with a velocity at $5 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$. Calculate the magnitude of change of momentum occurred in the motion of the hockey ball by the force applied by the hockey stick.
    > **Answer:**
    > Given: Mass $m = 200 \mathrm{~g} = 0.2 \mathrm{~kg}$. Initial velocity $u = 10 \mathrm{~m/s}$. Final velocity $v = -5 \mathrm{~m/s}$ (since it returns along the original path, its direction is opposite, hence the negative sign).
    >
    > Initial momentum $p_1 = mu = (0.2 \mathrm{~kg}) \times (10 \mathrm{~m/s}) = 2 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    > Final momentum $p_2 = mv = (0.2 \mathrm{~kg}) \times (-5 \mathrm{~m/s}) = -1 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    >
    > Change in momentum $\Delta p = p_2 - p_1 = (-1 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}) - (2 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}) = -3 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    > The magnitude of the change of momentum is $|-3 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}| = 3 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.

13. A bullet of mass 10 g travelling horizontally with a velocity of $150 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ strikes a stationary wooden block and comes to rest in 0.03 s. Calculate the distance of penetration of the bullet into the block. Also calculate the magnitude of the force exerted by the wooden block on the bullet.
    > **Answer:**
    > Given: Mass $m = 10 \mathrm{~g} = 0.01 \mathrm{~kg}$. Initial velocity $u = 150 \mathrm{~m/s}$. Final velocity $v = 0 \mathrm{~m/s}$ (comes to rest). Time $t = 0.03 \mathrm{~s}$.
    >
    > First, calculate the acceleration ($a$) of the bullet within the block using $v = u + at$:
    > $0 \mathrm{~m/s} = 150 \mathrm{~m/s} + a(0.03 \mathrm{~s})$
    > $a(0.03) = -150$
    > $a = \frac{-150}{0.03} = -5000 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$
    >
    > Now, calculate the distance of penetration ($s$) using $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$ or $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$:
    > Using $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$:
    > $s = (150 \mathrm{~m/s})(0.03 \mathrm{~s}) + \frac{1}{2}(-5000 \mathrm{~m/s}^2)(0.03 \mathrm{~s})^2$
    > $s = 4.5 \mathrm{~m} + \frac{1}{2}(-5000)(0.0009) \mathrm{~m}$
    > $s = 4.5 \mathrm{~m} - (2500)(0.0009) \mathrm{~m}$
    > $s = 4.5 \mathrm{~m} - 2.25 \mathrm{~m}$
    > $s = 2.25 \mathrm{~m}$
    >
    > Using $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$:
    > $(0 \mathrm{~m/s})^2 = (150 \mathrm{~m/s})^2 + 2(-5000 \mathrm{~m/s}^2)s$
    > $0 = 22500 - 10000s$
    > $10000s = 22500$
    > $s = \frac{22500}{10000} = 2.25 \mathrm{~m}$
    > The distance of penetration is 2.25 m.
    >
    > Now, calculate the magnitude of the force ($F$) exerted by the block on the bullet using $F = ma$:
    > $F = (0.01 \mathrm{~kg}) \times (-5000 \mathrm{~m/s}^2)$
    > $F = -50 \mathrm{~N}$
    > The magnitude of the force exerted by the wooden block on the bullet is 50 N. The negative sign indicates it opposes the bullet's motion.

14. An object of mass 1 kg travelling in a straight line with a velocity of $10 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ collides with, and sticks to, a stationary wooden block of mass 5 kg. Then they both move off together in the same straight line. Calculate the total momentum just before the impact and just after the impact. Also, calculate the velocity of the combined object.
    > **Answer:**
    > Given: Mass of object $m_1 = 1 \mathrm{~kg}$. Initial velocity of object $u_1 = 10 \mathrm{~m/s}$. Mass of block $m_2 = 5 \mathrm{~kg}$. Initial velocity of block $u_2 = 0 \mathrm{~m/s}$ (stationary).
    >
    > Total momentum just before impact:
    > $P_{before} = p_1 + p_2 = m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2$
    > $P_{before} = (1 \mathrm{~kg})(10 \mathrm{~m/s}) + (5 \mathrm{~kg})(0 \mathrm{~m/s})$
    > $P_{before} = 10 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s} + 0 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s} = 10 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    >
    > After impact, the object and block stick together, forming a combined mass $M = m_1 + m_2 = 1 \mathrm{~kg} + 5 \mathrm{~kg} = 6 \mathrm{~kg}$. Let their combined velocity be $v$.
    >
    > According to the law of conservation of momentum, the total momentum just before impact is equal to the total momentum just after impact, provided no external force acts on the system during collision (which is assumed here).
    > $P_{after} = P_{before} = 10 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    >
    > Total momentum just after impact is also $P_{after} = Mv$:
    > $10 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s} = (6 \mathrm{~kg}) \times v$
    > $v = \frac{10 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}}{6 \mathrm{~kg}} = \frac{10}{6} \mathrm{~m/s} = \frac{5}{3} \mathrm{~m/s} \approx 1.67 \mathrm{~m/s}$.
    >
    > Total momentum just before impact = $10 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    > Total momentum just after impact = $10 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    > The velocity of the combined object is approximately $1.67 \mathrm{~m/s}$.

15. An object of mass 100 kg is accelerated uniformly from a velocity of $5 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ to $8 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$ in 6 s. Calculate the initial and final momentum of the object. Also, find the magnitude of the force exerted on the object.
    > **Answer:**
    > Given: Mass $m = 100 \mathrm{~kg}$. Initial velocity $u = 5 \mathrm{~m/s}$. Final velocity $v = 8 \mathrm{~m/s}$. Time $t = 6 \mathrm{~s}$.
    >
    > Initial momentum $p_1 = mu = (100 \mathrm{~kg}) \times (5 \mathrm{~m/s}) = 500 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    > Final momentum $p_2 = mv = (100 \mathrm{~kg}) \times (8 \mathrm{~m/s}) = 800 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    >
    > The magnitude of the force exerted on the object can be found using Newton's second law in terms of change in momentum: $F = \frac{\Delta p}{t} = \frac{p_2 - p_1}{t}$.
    > $F = \frac{800 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s} - 500 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}}{6 \mathrm{~s}} = \frac{300 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}}{6 \mathrm{~s}} = 50 \mathrm{~N}$.
    >
    > Alternatively, first find acceleration $a = \frac{v-u}{t} = \frac{8 \mathrm{~m/s} - 5 \mathrm{~m/s}}{6 \mathrm{~s}} = \frac{3 \mathrm{~m/s}}{6 \mathrm{~s}} = 0.5 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$.
    > Then $F = ma = (100 \mathrm{~kg}) \times (0.5 \mathrm{~m/s}^2) = 50 \mathrm{~N}$.
    >
    > Initial momentum = $500 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    > Final momentum = $800 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    > Magnitude of the force = $50 \mathrm{~N}$.

16. Akhtar, Kiran and Rahul were riding in a motorcar that was moving with a high velocity on an expressway when an insect hit the windshield and got stuck on the windscreen. Akhtar and Kiran started pondering over the situation. Kiran suggested that the insect suffered a greater change in momentum as compared to the change in momentum of the motorcar (because the change in the velocity of the insect was much more than that of the motorcar). Akhtar said that since the motorcar was moving with a larger velocity, it exerted a larger force on the insect. And as a result the insect died. Rahul while putting an entirely new explanation said that both the motorcar and the insect experienced the same force and a change in their momentum. Comment on these suggestions.
    > **Answer:** Let's analyze each suggestion based on Newton's laws:
    > *   **Akhtar's suggestion:** Akhtar is incorrect in saying the motorcar exerted a larger force on the insect. According to Newton's Third Law, the force exerted by the motorcar on the insect is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the force exerted by the insect on the motorcar.
    > *   **Kiran's suggestion:** Kiran is incorrect about the change in momentum. According to the law of conservation of momentum (which applies to the system of the car and the insect during the collision), the total momentum of the system remains constant. This means the change in momentum of the car is equal in magnitude and opposite in direction to the change in momentum of the insect. So, the magnitudes of the change in momentum are equal for both the insect and the motorcar. The large change in the insect's velocity is due to its small mass ($ \Delta v_{insect} = -\Delta v_{car} \times \frac{m_{car}}{m_{insect}} $).
    > *   **Rahul's suggestion:** Rahul is correct. According to Newton's Third Law, the force exerted by the motorcar on the insect is equal in magnitude to the force exerted by the insect on the motorcar. According to Newton's Second Law ($F \Delta t = \Delta p$), since the force and the time of impact are the same for both the insect and the motorcar, the change in momentum ($ \Delta p $) will also be the same in magnitude for both, but in opposite directions. The insect dies because its small mass means the equal force causes a very large acceleration and thus a very large change in velocity, resulting in severe impact.

17. How much momentum will a dumb-bell of mass 10 kg transfer to the floor if it falls from a height of 80 cm? Take its downward acceleration to be $10 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-2}$.
    > **Answer:**
    > Given: Mass $m = 10 \mathrm{~kg}$. Height $h = 80 \mathrm{~cm} = 0.8 \mathrm{~m}$. Initial velocity $u = 0 \mathrm{~m/s}$ (assuming it starts from rest). Downward acceleration $a = 10 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$ (due to gravity).
    >
    > We need to find the velocity ($v$) of the dumb-bell just before it hits the floor. Using the kinematic equation $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$:
    > $v^2 = (0 \mathrm{~m/s})^2 + 2(10 \mathrm{~m/s}^2)(0.8 \mathrm{~m})$
    > $v^2 = 0 + 16$
    > $v = \sqrt{16} = 4 \mathrm{~m/s}$ (Taking the positive root as it's falling downwards).
    >
    > The momentum of the dumb-bell just before hitting the floor is $p = mv = (10 \mathrm{~kg}) \times (4 \mathrm{~m/s}) = 40 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    >
    > When the dumb-bell hits the floor and comes to rest, its final momentum is 0. The change in momentum of the dumb-bell is $\Delta p = p_{final} - p_{initial} = 0 - 40 = -40 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
    >
    > According to Newton's third law and the concept of momentum transfer (related to impulse), the change in momentum of the dumb-bell is equal in magnitude to the momentum transferred to the floor (and Earth) during the impact. The momentum transferred to the floor is in the downward direction.
    >
    > The momentum transferred to the floor is $40 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$ (downward).

A1. The following is the distance-time table of an object in motion:
Time in seconds | Distance in metres
| :-- | --: |
| 0 | 0 |
| 1 | 1 |
| 2 | 8 |
| 3 | 27 |
| 4 | 64 |
| 5 | 125 |
| 6 | 216 |
| 7 | 343 |

(a) What conclusion can you draw about the acceleration? Is it constant, increasing, decreasing, or zero?
> **Answer:** Let's look at the distance covered in each second:
> - 0s to 1s: $1 - 0 = 1 \mathrm{~m}$
> - 1s to 2s: $8 - 1 = 7 \mathrm{~m}$
> - 2s to 3s: $27 - 8 = 19 \mathrm{~m}$
> - 3s to 4s: $64 - 27 = 37 \mathrm{~m}$
> - 4s to 5s: $125 - 64 = 61 \mathrm{~m}$
> - 5s to 6s: $216 - 125 = 91 \mathrm{~m}$
> - 6s to 7s: $343 - 216 = 127 \mathrm{~m}$
>
> The distance covered in each successive second is increasing significantly (1, 7, 19, 37, 61, 91, 127...). This indicates that the velocity of the object is increasing over time. Since the velocity is increasing, the object is accelerating. Furthermore, the *increase* in distance covered each second is also increasing (7-1=6, 19-7=12, 37-19=18, 61-37=24, 91-61=30, 127-91=36...). This pattern (6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36...) shows a constant increase in the distance gain, which suggests that the acceleration is increasing. The distance is proportional to $t^3$ (e.g., at t=2, distance is $2^3=8$; at t=3, distance is $3^3=27$), which means velocity is proportional to $t^2$ and acceleration is proportional to $t$. Therefore, the acceleration is increasing.

(b) What do you infer about the forces acting on the object?
> **Answer:** Since the object is accelerating, there must be a net unbalanced force acting on it according to Newton's second law ($F_{net} = ma$). Since the acceleration is increasing, the net unbalanced force acting on the object must also be increasing over time.

A2. Two persons manage to push a motorcar of mass 1200 kg at a uniform velocity along a level road. The same motorcar can be pushed by three persons to produce an acceleration of $0.2 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-2}$. With what force does each person push the motorcar? (Assume that all persons push the motorcar with the same muscular effort.)
> **Answer:**
> Given: Mass of motorcar $m = 1200 \mathrm{~kg}$.
>
> Scenario 1: Two persons push at uniform velocity.
> Uniform velocity means acceleration $a_1 = 0 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$. According to Newton's second law, the net force is $F_{net,1} = ma_1 = (1200 \mathrm{~kg})(0 \mathrm{~m/s}^2) = 0 \mathrm{~N}$.
> When pushing at uniform velocity, the applied force from the two persons must be balancing the opposing forces like friction. Let $F_p$ be the force exerted by one person and $F_f$ be the total friction force.
> $F_{net,1} = 2 F_p - F_f = 0$, so $F_f = 2 F_p$.
>
> Scenario 2: Three persons push to produce an acceleration of $0.2 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$.
> Acceleration $a_2 = 0.2 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$. The net force is $F_{net,2} = ma_2 = (1200 \mathrm{~kg})(0.2 \mathrm{~m/s}^2) = 240 \mathrm{~N}$.
> With three persons pushing, the applied force is $3 F_p$. The friction force $F_f$ is assumed to be the same as in Scenario 1 (friction usually depends on the normal force and surface properties, not the number of pushers, although it can be slightly different for static vs kinetic friction; however, the problem implies $F_f$ is a constant opposing force).
> $F_{net,2} = 3 F_p - F_f = 240 \mathrm{~N}$.
>
> Substitute $F_f = 2 F_p$ from Scenario 1 into the equation for Scenario 2:
> $3 F_p - (2 F_p) = 240 \mathrm{~N}$
> $F_p = 240 \mathrm{~N}$.
>
> The force with which each person pushes the motorcar is 240 N. (Note: We can also find $F_f = 2 \times 240 = 480 \mathrm{~N}$).

A3. A hammer of mass 500 g, moving at $50 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-1}$, strikes a nail. The nail stops the hammer in a very short time of 0.01 s. What is the force of the nail on the hammer?
> **Answer:**
> Given: Mass of hammer $m = 500 \mathrm{~g} = 0.5 \mathrm{~kg}$. Initial velocity $u = 50 \mathrm{~m/s}$. Final velocity $v = 0 \mathrm{~m/s}$ (stops). Time $t = 0.01 \mathrm{~s}$.
>
> We can calculate the force using Newton's second law in the form $F = \frac{m(v-u)}{t}$:
> $F = \frac{(0.5 \mathrm{~kg})(0 \mathrm{~m/s} - 50 \mathrm{~m/s})}{0.01 \mathrm{~s}}$
> $F = \frac{(0.5)(-50)}{0.01} \mathrm{~N}$
> $F = \frac{-25}{0.01} \mathrm{~N}$
> $F = -2500 \mathrm{~N}$.
> The force of the nail on the hammer is -2500 N. The negative sign indicates the force is in the opposite direction to the hammer's initial motion. The magnitude of the force is 2500 N.

A4. A motorcar of mass 1200 kg is moving along a straight line with a uniform velocity of $90 \mathrm{~km} / \mathrm{h}$. Its velocity is slowed down to $18 \mathrm{~km} / \mathrm{h}$ in 4 s by an unbalanced external force. Calculate the acceleration and change in momentum. Also calculate the magnitude of the force required.
> **Answer:**
> Given: Mass $m = 1200 \mathrm{~kg}$. Initial velocity $u = 90 \mathrm{~km/h}$. Final velocity $v = 18 \mathrm{~km/h}$. Time $t = 4 \mathrm{~s}$.
>
> First, convert velocities from km/h to m/s:
> $u = 90 \mathrm{~km/h} = 90 \times \frac{1000 \mathrm{~m}}{3600 \mathrm{~s}} = 90 \times \frac{5}{18} \mathrm{~m/s} = 5 \times 5 \mathrm{~m/s} = 25 \mathrm{~m/s}$.
> $v = 18 \mathrm{~km/h} = 18 \times \frac{1000 \mathrm{~m}}{3600 \mathrm{~s}} = 18 \times \frac{5}{18} \mathrm{~m/s} = 5 \mathrm{~m/s}$.
>
> (a) Calculate the acceleration ($a$):
> $a = \frac{v-u}{t} = \frac{5 \mathrm{~m/s} - 25 \mathrm{~m/s}}{4 \mathrm{~s}} = \frac{-20 \mathrm{~m/s}}{4 \mathrm{~s}} = -5 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$.
> The acceleration is $-5 \mathrm{~m/s}^2$ (deceleration).
>
> (b) Calculate the change in momentum ($\Delta p$):
> Initial momentum $p_1 = mu = (1200 \mathrm{~kg})(25 \mathrm{~m/s}) = 30000 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
> Final momentum $p_2 = mv = (1200 \mathrm{~kg})(5 \mathrm{~m/s}) = 6000 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
> $\Delta p = p_2 - p_1 = 6000 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s} - 30000 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s} = -24000 \mathrm{~kg \ m/s}$.
