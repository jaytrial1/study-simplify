This section details the specific factors that influence India's climate, including its latitudinal extent, the presence of the Himalayas, and its unique pressure and wind systems.

# Factors Affecting India's Climate

India's climate is affected by several factors:

## Latitude

The Tropic of Cancer passes through the middle of India. Almost half the country, south of this Tropic, is in the tropical area. The remaining area to the north is in the sub-tropics. Therefore, India's climate exhibits characteristics of both tropical and subtropical climates.

## Altitude

India has mountains (Himalayas) in the north with an average height of about 6,000 metres, and a vast coastal area with a maximum elevation of about 30 metres. The Himalayas are crucial as they prevent cold winds from Central Asia from entering the subcontinent, resulting in milder winters in India compared to Central Asia.

## Pressure and Winds

India's climate and weather are governed by:
*   Pressure and surface winds
*   Upper air circulation
*   Western cyclonic disturbances and tropical cyclones

India is in the region of **north easterly winds**. These originate from the subtropical high-pressure belt of the northern hemisphere, blow south, get deflected to the right due to the **Coriolis force**, and move towards the equatorial low-pressure area.

**Coriolis force:** An apparent force caused by the Earth's rotation. It deflects winds to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere (also known as 'Ferrel's Law').

Normally, north easterly winds carry little moisture as they blow over land, suggesting India should be arid. However, this is not the case due to unique pressure and wind conditions.

During winter, a high-pressure area exists north of the Himalayas, causing cold, dry winds to blow south towards oceanic low-pressure areas.

In summer, a low-pressure area develops over interior Asia and northwestern India. This causes a complete reversal of wind direction. Air moves from the high-pressure area over the southern Indian Ocean (south-easterly direction), crosses the equator, turns right (due to Coriolis force), and moves towards the low-pressure areas over India. These are the **Southwest Monsoon winds**. Blowing over warm oceans, they gather moisture and bring widespread rainfall to India.

The upper air circulation is dominated by a **westerly flow**, including the **jet stream**.

**Jet stream:** A narrow belt of high-altitude (above 12,000 m) westerly winds in the troposphere. Their speed varies from about 110 km/h in summer to about 184 km/h in winter. Subtropical westerly jet streams are located around 27°-30° North latitude.

Over India, the subtropical westerly jet stream blows south of the Himalayas throughout the year, except in summer. These jet streams bring **western cyclonic disturbances** to the north and northwestern parts of the country. In summer, with the apparent northward movement of the sun, the subtropical westerly jet stream moves north of the Himalayas. A tropical easterly jet stream blows over peninsular India (around 14° N) during summer.

## Western Cyclonic Disturbances

These are winter weather phenomena originating over the Mediterranean Sea and western Asia, moving into India with the westerly flow. They influence the weather of northern and northwestern India, causing needed winter rains ('mahawat') and snowfall in mountains. Tropical cyclones occur during the monsoon and in October-November, affecting coastal regions, originating as part of the easterly flow.
