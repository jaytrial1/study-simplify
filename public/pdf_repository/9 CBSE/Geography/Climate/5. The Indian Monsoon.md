# The Indian Monsoon

India's climate is strongly influenced by monsoon winds. Sailors noticed this seasonal wind reversal long ago, using it for trade voyages. The Arabs named this system 'monsoon'.

Monsoons are experienced in tropical areas, roughly between 20° N and 20° S. Key factors for understanding the monsoon mechanism include:

*   **Differential Heating:** The unequal heating and cooling of land and water create low pressure on the Indian landmass in summer while surrounding seas have high pressure.
*   **ITCZ Shift:** The shift of the **Inter Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)** over the Ganga plain in summer. This equatorial trough (normally around 5° N) is also called the monsoon trough.
*   **High Pressure over Madagascar:** The presence of a high-pressure area east of Madagascar (around 20° S) over the Indian Ocean. Its intensity and position affect the Indian Monsoon.
*   **Tibetan Plateau Heating:** Intense heating of the Tibetan plateau in summer creates strong vertical air currents and low pressure at about 9 km above sea level.
*   **Jet Stream Movement:** The movement of the westerly jet stream to the north of the Himalayas and the presence of the tropical easterly jet stream over peninsular India during summer.

## Inter Tropical Convergence Zone

The **Inter Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)** is a broad trough of low pressure near the equator where the northeast and southeast trade winds converge. Its position shifts north or south with the apparent movement of the sun.

## Southern Oscillation and El Nino

Changes in pressure conditions over the southern oceans also affect monsoons. Normally, the tropical eastern South Pacific has high pressure while the eastern Indian Ocean has low pressure. Periodically, this reverses (eastern Pacific has lower pressure than eastern Indian Ocean). This change is the **Southern Oscillation (SO)**. The pressure difference between Tahiti (Pacific) and Darwin (Australia, Indian Ocean) is used to predict monsoon intensity; a negative difference suggests below-average and late monsoons.

A phenomenon linked to SO is **El Nino**.

**El Nino:** A warm ocean current that periodically replaces the cold Peruvian current along the coast of Peru (every 2-5 years). The name means 'the child' in Spanish, referring to its appearance around Christmas. El Nino increases sea-surface temperatures and weakens trade winds in the region.

The changes in pressure conditions are connected to El Nino, hence the term **ENSO (El Nino Southern Oscillations)**.
