This section describes the four main seasons experienced in India, detailing the weather conditions and characteristics of each.

# The Seasons

India's monsoon climate is marked by distinct seasons. Weather conditions change significantly from one season to the next, especially in the interior. Coastal areas have less temperature variation but different rainfall patterns. India experiences four main seasons:

1.  The Cold Weather Season (Winter)
2.  The Hot Weather Season (Summer)
3.  The Advancing Monsoon (The Rainy Season)
4.  The Retreating Monsoon (The Transition Season)

## The Cold Weather Season (Winter)

This season is from mid-November to February in northern India. December and January are the coldest months.
*   Temperature decreases from south to north. Average temperature in Chennai is 24°-25°C, while in the northern plains it's 10°-15°C.
*   Days are warm, nights are cold. Frost is common in the north. Higher Himalayan slopes experience snowfall.
*   **Northeast trade winds** prevail, blowing from land to sea, making it mostly dry.
*   The Tamil Nadu coast receives some rainfall from these winds as they blow from sea to land there.
*   A feeble high-pressure area develops in the north, with light winds. Weather is typically clear, with low temperatures, low humidity, and weak winds.
*   A key feature is the inflow of **western cyclonic disturbances** from the Mediterranean Sea and western Asia. These low-pressure systems bring needed winter rains ('**mahawat**') to the plains and snowfall to the mountains. Mahawat is important for 'rabi' crops.
*   The peninsular region has no well-defined cold season due to the moderating influence of the sea, resulting in little seasonal temperature change.

## The Hot Weather Season (Summer)

From March to May, it's the hot weather season due to the apparent northward movement of the sun and the shifting heat belt.
*   Highest temperatures are recorded in March (around 38°C on the Deccan plateau), April (around 42°C in Gujarat and Madhya Pradesh), and May (around 45°C in northwestern India).
*   Peninsular India has lower temperatures due to oceanic influence.
*   Rising temperatures and falling air pressure occur in northern India. By late May, an elongated low-pressure area develops from the Thar Desert to Patna and Chotanagpur plateau.
*   A striking feature is the '**loo**': strong, hot, dry, gusty winds blowing during the day over north and northwestern India, sometimes continuing into the evening. Direct exposure can be fatal.
*   Dust storms are common in May in northern India, bringing temporary relief by lowering temperature and sometimes causing light rain and cool breezes.
*   This season also sees localised thunderstorms with violent winds, heavy rain, and hail. In West Bengal, they are called '**Kaal Baisakhi**'.
*   Towards the end of summer, **pre-monsoon showers** are common, especially in Kerala and Karnataka. These help ripen mangoes and are called '**mango showers**'.

## Advancing Monsoon (The Rainy Season)

By early June, the low-pressure over the northern plains intensifies, attracting south-east trade winds from the southern oceans. These cross the equator, turn right (Coriolis force), and enter India as the **south-west monsoon**.
*   These winds blow over warm oceans, bringing abundant moisture. They are strong, blowing at about 30 km/h.
*   Except for the extreme northwest, monsoon winds cover India in about a month.
*   The arrival of the southwest monsoon completely changes the weather.
*   Early in the season, the windward side of the Western Ghats gets very heavy rainfall (over 250 cm). The Deccan Plateau and parts of Madhya Pradesh receive some rain despite being in the rain shadow.
*   Northeastern India receives the maximum rainfall. Mawsynram in the Khasi Hills has the highest average rainfall in the world.
*   Rainfall decreases from east to west in the Ganga valley. Rajasthan and parts of Gujarat receive scanty rainfall.
*   A phenomenon of the monsoon is '**breaks**' in rainfall – alternating wet and dry spells. Rains occur only for a few days at a time, interspersed with rainless intervals.
*   Breaks are linked to the movement of the monsoon trough. When the trough's axis is over the plains, they get good rainfall. When it shifts closer to the Himalayas, plains have dry spells, and mountain catchment areas get heavy rain, causing floods in the plains.
*   Tropical depressions, forming at the head of the Bay of Bengal and crossing onto the mainland, also influence the amount and duration of monsoon rains. They follow the axis of the monsoon trough.
*   The monsoon is known for its uncertainties: intensity, frequency, and duration of wet/dry spells vary. This can cause floods in one area and droughts in another, often disrupting farming schedules.
