# The Peninsular Plateau

This section describes the Peninsular Plateau, its formation, divisions (Central Highlands and Deccan Plateau), and prominent features like the Ghats and hills.

The Peninsular plateau is a tableland composed of old crystalline, *igneous*, and *metamorphic* rocks. It was formed when the Gondwana land broke and drifted, making it one of the oldest landmasses. The plateau is characterized by broad and shallow valleys and rounded hills.

It has two main divisions:

1.  **The Central Highlands:** The part of the Peninsular plateau north of the Narmada river, covering a large area of the Malwa plateau. The Vindhyan range borders it on the south, and the Aravalis on the northwest. Its westward extension gradually merges with the sandy and rocky desert of Rajasthan. Rivers draining this region (Chambal, Sind, Betwa, Ken) flow from southwest to northeast, indicating the slope. The Central Highlands are wider in the west and narrower in the east. The eastern extensions are locally known as the Bundelkhand and Baghelkhand. The Chotanagpur plateau marks the further eastward extension, drained by the Damodar river.

2.  **The Deccan Plateau:** A triangular landmass south of the Narmada river. The Satpura range forms its broad base in the north, while the Mahadev, Kaimur hills, and Maikal range form its eastern extensions. The Deccan Plateau is higher in the west and slopes gently eastwards. An extension is also seen in the northeast, known locally as the Meghalaya, Karbi-Anglong Plateau, and North Cachar Hills, separated from the Chotanagpur Plateau by a fault. Prominent hill ranges from west to east in this northeastern extension are the Garo, Khasi, and Jaintia Hills.

The **Western Ghats** and the **Eastern Ghats** mark the western and eastern edges of the Deccan Plateau, respectively.

*   **Western Ghats:** Lie parallel to the western coast. They are continuous and can only be crossed through passes. They are higher than the Eastern Ghats, with an average elevation of 900-1600 metres. The height increases from north to south. They cause *orographic rain* by forcing rain-bearing winds to rise. Highest peaks include Anai Mudi (2,695 metres) and Doda Betta (2,637 metres). They are known by different local names.
*   **Eastern Ghats:** Stretch from the Mahanadi Valley to the Nilgiris in the south. They are discontinuous, irregular, and dissected by rivers flowing into the Bay of Bengal. Their average elevation is around 600 metres. Mahendragiri (1,501 metres) is the highest peak. Shevroy Hills and Javadi Hills are located southeast of the Eastern Ghats.

### Key Concepts & Examples

*   **Passes in Western Ghats:** Thal Ghat, Bhor Ghat, Pal Ghat are important passes.
*   **Hill Stations:** Udagamandalam (Ooty) and Kodaikanal are famous hill stations located in the Eastern Ghats region (specifically the Nilgiri and Palani hills).

A distinct feature of the peninsular plateau is the **Deccan Trap**, a black soil area of volcanic origin. The rocks here are igneous and have eroded over time to form black soil.

The **Aravali Hills** lie on the western and northwestern margins of the peninsular plateau. These are highly eroded and appear as broken hills, extending from Gujarat to Delhi in a southwest-northeast direction.
