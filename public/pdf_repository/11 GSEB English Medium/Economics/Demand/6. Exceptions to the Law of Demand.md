# Exceptions to the Law of Demand

This section discusses situations where the Law of Demand does not hold true, meaning demand may increase as price rises or decrease as price falls.

Exceptions occur when a change in price causes demand to change in the same direction, contrary to the Law of Demand. Some examples include:

*   **Prestigious Goods:** Expensive items consumed by the wealthy as status symbols (e.g., luxury cars, high-end jewelry) may see their demand increase with price. A lower price might reduce their prestige value, causing demand to fall among this group.
*   **Extremely Low-Priced Goods:** For goods that are very cheap and form a tiny part of a consumer's budget (e.g., pins), a price change might not significantly affect demand. Consumers buy them based on need, and even a price fall may not lead to much increase in demand if they already have enough.
*   **Giffen Goods:** These are a specific type of inferior good, named after Robert Giffen. For these goods, when the price falls, the negative income effect (reduced demand due to higher real income) outweighs the substitution effect, leading to a decrease in demand. They are typically necessities consumed by low-income groups (e.g., coarse grains). If the price of a Giffen good falls, the consumer has more real income and may switch to a superior good, reducing demand for the Giffen good.
*   **Special Preferences of People:** Sometimes, strong consumer loyalty or habit towards a particular brand or product can make demand less responsive to price changes. Consumers may continue buying it even if the price increases slightly.
