# Price Elasticity of Demand

This section focuses on price elasticity, explaining how to measure the responsiveness of demand specifically to changes in the good's own price.

The Law of Demand tells us that price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, but it doesn't say by how much. Price elasticity of demand measures the magnitude of this response.

## Meaning of Price Elasticity of Demand

Price elasticity of demand shows the proportionate change in demand resulting from a proportionate change in price. It is calculated as:

$$
\text{Price elasticity of demand } (\varepsilon_{p}) = \frac{\text{Proportionate change in demand}}{\text{Proportionate change in price}}
$$

Or, using percentages:

$$
\varepsilon_{p} = \frac{\text{Percentage change in demand}}{\text{Percentage change in price}}
$$

For example, if a 1% fall in price leads to a 5% rise in demand, the price elasticity is $|5\% / -1\%| = |5|$.

Note: Price elasticity of demand is a pure number without units.

According to Marshall, price elasticity depends on how much demand rises when price falls and how much demand falls when price rises.

## Degrees of Price Elasticity of Demand

The responsiveness of demand to price changes can be categorized into five degrees:

1.  **Perfectly Elastic Demand ($\varepsilon_{p}=\infty$):** Demand changes infinitely in response to a negligible (or zero) change in price.
    $$
    \varepsilon_{p} = \frac{\text{Infinite change in demand}}{\text{Negligible change in price}} = \infty
    $$

2.  **Perfectly Inelastic Demand ($\varepsilon_{p}=0$):** Demand does not change at all, regardless of the change in price.
    $$
    \varepsilon_{p} = \frac{\text{0\% change in demand}}{\text{Percentage change in price}} = 0
    $$

3.  **Unitary Elastic Demand ($\varepsilon_{p}=1$):** The percentage change in demand is exactly equal to the percentage change in price.
    $$
    \varepsilon_{p} = \frac{\text{Percentage change in demand}}{\text{Percentage change in price}} = 1
    $$

4.  **Relatively Elastic Demand ($\varepsilon_{p}>1$):** The percentage change in demand is proportionately greater than the percentage change in price.
    $$
    \varepsilon_{p} = \frac{\text{Larger percentage change in demand}}{\text{Smaller percentage change in price}} > 1
    $$
    Example: 30% fall in demand for a 10% rise in price gives elasticity of $|-30\% / +10\%| = |3|$, which is > 1.

5.  **Relatively Inelastic Demand ($\varepsilon_{p}<1$):** The percentage change in demand is proportionately lesser than the percentage change in price.
    $$
    \varepsilon_{p} = \frac{\text{Smaller percentage change in demand}}{\text{Larger percentage change in price}} < 1
    $$
    Example: 5% fall in demand for a 20% rise in price gives elasticity of $|-5\% / +20\%| = |-1/4| = |0.25|$, which is < 1.
