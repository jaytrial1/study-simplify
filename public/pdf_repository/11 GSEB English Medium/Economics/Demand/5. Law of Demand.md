# Law of Demand

This section introduces the Law of Demand, its assumptions, and the reasons behind the inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded.

The Law of Demand describes the inverse relationship between the price of a good and the quantity demanded, assuming all other factors influencing demand remain constant. In this law, price is considered the cause and demand the effect.

Presented by Professor Alfred Marshall, the law states: "When other factors influencing demand remain unchanged, if price of a good falls, its demand expands and if price of a good rises, its demand contracts."

## Assumptions of Law of Demand

The inverse relationship described by the Law of Demand holds true under certain conditions:
*   Tastes and preferences of the consumer remain unchanged.
*   Income of the consumer remains unchanged.
*   Prices of substitute and complementary goods remain unchanged.
*   Consumers do not anticipate changes in future prices.
*   Size of the population remains the same.

## Reasons for the Inverse Relationship

The inverse relationship between price and demand is primarily due to two effects:

*   **Income Effect:** When the price of a good falls, the consumer's *real income* (purchasing power) increases, even if their monetary income stays the same. With higher real income, the consumer can afford to buy more of the good, leading to increased demand. This effect is positive for normal goods but negative for inferior goods (where rising real income leads to less demand for the inferior good).
*   **Substitution Effect:** When the price of a good falls, it becomes relatively cheaper compared to its substitutes. Consumers tend to switch from the relatively more expensive substitutes to the cheaper good, thus increasing the demand for the good whose price has fallen.
