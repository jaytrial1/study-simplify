This section describes the three primary methods used to calculate national income: the production method, the income method, and the expenditure method, detailing the components and considerations for each.

# Measurement Methods of National Income

National income can be calculated using three main methods, each corresponding to a different phase of the circular flow:

## Production Method

Developed from Marshall's definition, this method calculates national income by summing the monetary value of all final goods and services produced in the agriculture, industries, and service sectors during a year. It involves identifying the output in each sector, multiplying it by market prices to get the money value, and summing these values across the economy.

**Important Considerations for the Production Method:**
*   **Classification of Economy:** The economy is classified into various sectors (agriculture, industry, services, etc.).
*   **Selection of Goods/Services:** Only the value of *final* goods or services is counted. Intermediate goods are excluded.
*   **Service of House-Wife:** Household work by a housewife is typically not included as its monetary value is not measured in the market.
*   **Self-Consumption:** Goods produced for self-consumption are generally not included, as their market value is not realized, though exceptions like food grains consumed by farmers in India may be considered.
*   **Defence and Police:** Services of defence and police are not typically included as they don't have a market price, although their contribution is vital.
*   **Imputed Rent:** The estimated rent value of owner-occupied houses (imputed rent) is included in national income calculation.
*   **Avoiding Double Counting:** This is crucial. Counting the value of a commodity more than once inflates national income.
    *   **Remedy 1: Count only Finished Goods:** Calculate only the value of the final product, not the intermediate goods used in its production. For example, count the value of a machine, not the value of the iron used to make it separately.
    *   **Remedy 2: Value Added Method:** Sum the value added at each stage of production. Value added is the increase in monetary value as a good moves from one stage to the next.

    | Stages of Production | Sales Income (€) | Factor Cost (€) | Increasing Value (Value Added) (€) |
    | :------------------- | :--------------- | :-------------- | :------------------------------- |
    | Cotton               | 100              | 00              | 100                              |
    | Yarn                 | 200              | 100             | 100                              |
    | Cloth                | 280              | 200             | 80                               |
    | **Total**            | **580**          | **300**         | **280**                          |

    *In this example, summing sales income (580) would be double counting. Summing value added (100+100+80=280) correctly reflects the total new value created.*
*   **Indirect Tax and Subsidy:** Indirect taxes are deducted from market value, and government subsidies are added to arrive at national product at factor cost.
*   **Resale:** The value of goods resold (e.g., a used car or house) is not counted, as their value was included in the national product when they were originally produced.
*   **Deduct Depreciation:** Depreciation of capital goods is deducted.
*   **Export Value:** The value of exports is added.
*   **Illegal Goods:** The value of smuggled or illegal goods is not included.

## Income Method

Based on Pigou's definition, this method sums up all the incomes earned by the factors of production within a country during a year. It includes rent, interest, wages, and profit. To get the national income, the net income from foreign countries (income earned by citizens abroad minus income paid to foreigners domestically) is added.

**Important Considerations for the Income Method:**
*   **Factor Incomes:**
    *   **Rent:** Includes rent from land/buildings, imputed rent for owner-occupied houses, and royalties (e.g., for copyrights, patents).
    *   **Interest:** Interest earned on capital is included. However, interest paid by the government is typically *not* included as it's often seen as a transfer payment funded by taxes, not income from productive activity.
    *   **Wage:** Wages and salaries paid to laborers.
    *   **Profit:** Income earned by entrepreneurs, including corporate profits (distributed as dividends, retained as reserved profit, or paid as taxes).
*   **Incomes Not Considered:** Transfer payments like gifts, rewards, prizes, tips, theft income, unemployment allowance, government assistance to elders, lottery winnings, etc., are not included because they are transfers of existing income, not earnings from current production.
*   **Subsidy:** Government subsidies are deducted (as they lower the price received by producers but are not earned income).
*   **Net Foreign Income:** Added (Exports - Imports of factor income, not goods/services).
*   **Commission/Brokerage:** Income from commission or brokerage on the sale of consumable goods is included as it relates to a service provided in the flow of goods.
*   **Productive Income:** Only incomes generated from the flow of production of goods or services that increase the monetary value in the economy are considered.
*   **Second-hand Goods:** Income from the sale of second-hand goods is not included (value was counted when new).

## Expenditure Method

Developed from Fisher's definition, this method measures national income by summing up the total monetary expenditure incurred on final goods and services by individuals, families, firms, and the government during a year. The total expenditure on final goods and services should equal the GDP.

**Important Considerations for the Expenditure Method:**
*   **Four Components of Monetary Expenditure:**
    *   **Consumption Expenditure (C):** Spending by households and firms on consumable goods and services (durable goods like TV, non-durable goods like food, and services like education).
    *   **Investment Expenditure (I):** Spending by firms on capital goods like factory buildings, plant, machinery, and equipment.
    *   **Government Expenditure (G):** Spending by the central, state, and local governments on consumption (e.g., salaries) and investment (e.g., infrastructure).
    *   **Net Export Expenditure (X-M):** The difference between expenditure by foreigners on a country's goods (exports, X) and expenditure by domestic residents on foreign goods (imports, M). Net exports (X-M) are included.
    Total National Income = C + I + G + (X - M)
*   **Expenditure Not Considered:** Expenditure on second-hand goods, transfer payments (pension, unemployment allowances, financial assistance), purchase of old shares, and expenditure on intermediate goods are not included. Some expenditures that are purely money transfers without corresponding production of new goods/services (like subsidies paid by the government) are also excluded from the expenditure side calculation of GDP/National Income.
*   **Difficulty in Calculation:** Obtaining accurate official data on individual and firm expenditures can be challenging. Tracking the flow of money through multiple transactions makes it difficult to distinguish final expenditure and avoid double counting.

Q: Hence the question arises, 'what is the actual expenditure ?' ₹ 30,000 or ₹ 30,000 + ₹ 3000 = ₹ 33,000 ? Thus, the problem of double counting arises even in the expenditure method of calculating national income.

> **Answer:** In national income accounting using the expenditure method, we aim to count the expenditure on *final* goods and services. In the example given, Arav's payment of ₹ 30,000 to Milap is income for Milap, not final expenditure on goods/services by Arav. Milap's payment of ₹ 3000 to Khushbu is income for Khushbu. The difficulty lies in tracing how this income is ultimately spent on final goods and services by Milap and Khushbu. Counting both ₹ 30,000 (as Arav's expenditure on a service - though the text frames it as salary) and ₹ 3,000 (as Milap's expenditure on a service) would lead to double counting if these amounts represent intermediate payments or income transfers rather than direct spending on final consumption or investment goods. The goal is to track the final spending on newly produced items in the economy, which is hard to capture comprehensively from individual transaction data.

A country can theoretically use any of these three methods. If the data is accurate, all methods should yield the same national income figure. However, each method has limitations, making them complementary. For instance, the production method might be suitable for developing countries like India with large informal sectors, while the income and expenditure methods might be more suitable for developed economies like the USA or Russia with better record-keeping. Calculating national income using one method can help verify the results obtained from another.
