This section details the economic conditions and policies in India under British rule, highlighting exploitation and the development of certain infrastructure for colonial benefit.

# Indian Economy During British Rule

Foreign rulers, including the East India Company and later the British government, were drawn to India by its wealth, scriptures, and cohesive social structure. India was famous for trade in commodities like cotton, muslin, indigo, spices, and various metals.

During this period, foreign rulers also engaged in conflicts among themselves to establish dominance using armed force. Once the British gained control, India's resources were exploited, poverty increased, and its ancient wealth diminished. However, certain developments also occurred:

## Railway

The British established the railway infrastructure in India. The first train ran between Boribandar (Mumbai) and Thane on April 16, 1853. By 1947, India had a rail network of 53,000 km, benefiting a large population.

## Roadways

The British also built a significant road network. The Public Works Department (PWD) was created to develop basic utilities. By the end of the 19th century, there were 2,78,420 km of roads, increasing to 4,47,105 km by 1943, with about 32% being paved ('pukka') and 68% unpaved ('kachcha').

## Banks

Private sector banks started in India from 1770. By 1946, there were over 700 banks. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI), the central bank, was established in 1935.

## Social Structure

Certain harmful social practices were abolished during British rule, such as female infanticide and *sati pratha*.

## Agriculture

Indian agriculture suffered under high revenues, the **Zamindari system** (landlordship), and other British policies. Exploitation was widespread, leading to increased poverty in this sector. For example, after the Industrial Revolution in Britain, Indian farmers were pressured to grow more indigo for the British textile industry. If farmers took loans for indigo cultivation and faced losses, the British government did not waive the loans, causing farmers to accumulate debt.

## Land Revenues

The East India Company initially gained rights to collect land revenues from Indian kings and landowners. Later, the British government collected revenues from farmers through Zamindars. Farmers unable to pay were punished with land confiscation and fines. Revenues could be as high as half of the farmer's produce.

## High Rates of Taxes

According to Dadabhai Naoroji's calculations in 1876, the wealthy paid about 8% of national income as taxes, while the poor Indians were forced to pay 15%.

## High Rates of Excise and Customs

High excise duties were levied on commodities like matches, sugar, steel, and silver. Despite salt being readily available, the British declared Indian production on shores illegal, monopolized salt trade, imported salt with high customs duties, making it expensive for poor Indians. This led to Gandhiji's **Salt Satyagraha** and Dandi March.

High duties (up to 15%) were imposed on cotton cloth exports from India, hindering the Indian cotton industry. In contrast, cotton cloth imported from Manchester, England, faced much lower tariffs (about 2.5%). The British aimed to export India's raw cotton to England and sell their manufactured cloth in India. This exploited India's raw materials to benefit British industry at the cost of Indian industry.

## Industrial Policy

During the period of industrial revolution (1750-1830), the East India Company and later the British government implemented policies that eroded Indian investor confidence. Industrial development was **lopsided**, focusing only on some light and consumer goods industries. No attention was given to machine-manufacturing, heavy engineering, heavy chemical, or other basic and key industries. The policy was designed to extract raw materials from India and sell British manufactured goods back to India.

## Economic Exploitation

To benefit their own economy, the British adopted policies that exploited Indian resources and promoted British industries. Indian agriculture was also exploited through high land revenues.

## Exploitation of Artisans

The East India Company exploited artisans by buying goods at 15% to 40% lower prices and selling them globally for high profits. Under direct British rule, cheap goods manufactured in England were sold in India, ruining India's small-scale industries.

## Investment Pattern

British investments in India were selective, prioritizing areas that benefited Britain. Railways and roads were built to facilitate the movement of raw materials and goods for their industry. Investment in education focused on training personnel for British administration, while technical education for Indians was discouraged. Colleges for medicine and civil engineering were established to meet their own needs (e.g., civil engineers for public works in India and Britain), but studies in science and technology were not promoted for Indians.

## Payment Burden

India's income was used to make various payments to Britain, including large salaries for British personnel. These payments, known as "**Home Charges**", covered costs for administration, army maintenance, war expenses, pensions for retired officers, and other expenditures related to maintaining the colony.

Home charges consisted of:

*   Interest payments on debts incurred for maintaining the Indian colony.
*   Interest on railway investments.
*   Civil and military charges.

By the time the British left in 1947, Indian agriculture, industry, and trade were devastated. A once prosperous country had become impoverished.
