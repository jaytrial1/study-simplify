This section explores the historical perspective of economic thought, beginning with ancient Indian philosophy and the ideas of Kautilya, and then transitioning to the development of economics as a distinct science in the West, particularly influenced by the Industrial Revolution and the work of Adam Smith.

# Economics in Indian and Western Thought

The history of Indian culture, over five thousand years old, offers insights into human life through its philosophy. The four **'purusharthas'** (purposes of a human being) in Indian philosophy are:
*   **'dharma'** (righteousness, duty)
*   **'arth'** (purpose, benefit, wealth)
*   **'kama'** (desire)
*   **'moksha'** (liberation)

Activities for **'arth'** (benefits) form the subject matter of economics. Any activity aiming for benefit is an **economic activity**. Creating wealth for living and material pleasures is considered one of the four goals or duties.

Around 2500 years ago, **Kautilya (Chanakya)** discussed the purpose of state and societal economic activity in his book **'Arthashashtra'**. According to Kautilya, **'arth'** is wealth, and the land with human settlement is also **'arth'**. Thus, economics is the science explaining the purpose and utility of wealth creation on earth.

# Development of Economics as a Science in the West

Greek philosopher **Aristotle** shared his views on economics in his book **'Oeconomica'**.

With the **Industrial Revolution**, knowledge, like industrial labour, became specialized. Social sciences developed as specialized branches, applying scientific tools rather than purely descriptive logic. Studying purposeful human activity became an independent science.

**Adam Smith**, pondering wealth creation, wrote **"An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of Wealth of Nations"** (popularly **'Wealth of Nations'**) in 1776. He is considered a pioneer in developing economics as a specialized field in the West. The Industrial Revolution brought new wealth creation methods through machines and mass production investments.

Adam Smith introduced economics as a social science, studying human efforts scientifically. Since his time, economics has been studied independently, not just as part of general philosophy, though he touched upon human welfare.
