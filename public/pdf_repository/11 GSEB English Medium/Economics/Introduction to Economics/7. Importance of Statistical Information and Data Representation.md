This section emphasizes the crucial role of statistical information in economics. It explains how data supports theories, reveals trends, facilitates comparisons, and allows for precise presentation. It also details important considerations when collecting and presenting data graphically, such as reliable sources, identifying variables, and selecting appropriate scales.

# Importance of Statistical Information

The importance of statistical information in economics:

*   **Statistical Information Supports or Confirms a Principle:** Statistical data from real life helps test the validity of economic theories and principles across different times and places. For example, data on rainfall and agricultural production can confirm the theoretical relationship between them. Data on price and demand can confirm the law of demand.
*   **Gives an Idea about the Changing Trends of Economic Parameters:** Economic data reveals the direction and magnitude of change in parameters like firm sales, national employment, or production trends in different sectors. This helps individuals make decisions and governments formulate policies.
*   **To Make Comparative Study Easy:** Statistical data allows for easy comparison of parameters over different time periods, across regions, or between nations (e.g., comparing India's growth rate over decades or comparing its parameters with other countries).
*   **To Make Precise Presentation of Facts:** Statistical data and graphs (like those showing inflation or production) can present facts more clearly and understandably, even for lay persons, compared to descriptions in words.

# Some Requisite Aspects in Collecting Statistical Information and Presenting it in Diagram/Graph/Chart

Presenting statistical information in diagrams/charts/graphs helps explain principles, trends, and relationships simply and attractively. Considerations for constructing diagrams:

*   **Sources of Information/Data:** Data sources must be reliable and universally acceptable for accuracy. Examples include 'Economic Survey of India', Central Statistical Organization data, and 'World Development Report' for international comparisons.
*   **Clarity Regarding Dependent and Independent Variable:** In cause-effect relationships, the cause is the **independent variable** (usually on the X-axis), and the effect is the **dependent variable** (usually on the Y-axis).
    *   *Example:* Rainfall (independent, X-axis) and agricultural production (dependent, Y-axis). Time periods (independent, X-axis) and production (dependent, Y-axis).
    *   *Note:* In the law of demand, price (independent) is conventionally on the Y-axis, and demand (dependent) on the X-axis.
*   **Selecting Appropriate Scales:** Proper scales on both axes are needed for clear, appropriately sized graphs. Scales convert large actual data into manageable measures (e.g., 1 cm = 10,000 units).

# Presenting Quantitative/Numerical Information in Diagrams/Graphs

Numerical data can be represented using various graphs and diagrams, including linear and non-linear relations like the demand curve.

Other types of charts/graphs/diagrams frequently used:
1.  Bar diagram
2.  Grouped bar Diagram
3.  Pie Diagram

**Bar Diagram:** Represents grouped data with rectangular bars whose length is proportional to the values.

*   **Example:** Production of wheat in India.
