This section delves into different ways economists define and measure the cost of production, including real cost, opportunity cost, and monetary cost.

# Various Concepts of Cost

## Real Cost

The concept of *real cost* was introduced by classical economists, initially focusing on land and labor. Modern economics extends this concept to capitalists and entrepreneurs as well.

According to Marshall, the psychological and physical burden borne by laborers, capitalists, and entrepreneurs involved in production is called real cost. This includes not only the money spent by producers but also the *fatigue, boredom, tension, stress* faced by laborers, and the *anxiety, insecurity, and indecisiveness* faced by capitalists who sacrifice their savings and capital. Real cost cannot be expressed in monetary terms, so it is also called *non-monetary cost*. Prof. Marshall notes that factors of production bear this real cost and are compensated with wages, interest, and profit to attract them.

### Problems in Measuring Real Cost

Real cost involves subjective elements like fatigue, boredom, pain, sacrifice, and anxiety, which are difficult to measure. Furthermore, negative social impacts like the adverse health effects of pollution from factories are also a social cost but are not easily measured.

## Opportunity Cost

The concept of *opportunity cost* was presented by Austrian economists and later elaborated by Marshall. This concept is based on the characteristic of factors of production having *alternative uses*. When a factor is used for one purpose, its other potential uses are forgone. The *best alternative* that is given up is the opportunity cost.

For example, a piece of land can be used to produce either wheat or rice. If producing wheat yields ₹2 lakh and producing rice yields ₹3.5 lakh, a rational farmer will choose to produce rice. The income of ₹2 lakh that was forgone by not producing wheat is the opportunity cost of earning ₹3.5 lakh from rice production.

### Problems in Measuring of Opportunity Cost

1.  **Factors with One Use:** If a factor of production has only one use, its opportunity cost cannot be determined. For instance, land usable only for growing grass has no alternative use to measure opportunity cost against. Similarly, for an unemployed person with no alternative work, opportunity cost cannot be calculated.
2.  **Factors having Specific Use:** For factors with highly specific uses (e.g., computer experts, nuclear scientists), their returns are determined more by their demand than by alternative uses, making the opportunity cost concept less applicable.

## Monetary Cost

The concept of *monetary cost* is crucial for economic analysis, production decisions, and price determination because real cost and opportunity cost have limitations in measurement. The cost of production calculated in terms of money is known as monetary cost. Producers aiming to maximize profit try to produce at low monetary cost and maximize income by selling the product.

For example, if a factory spends ₹50,000 to produce 1000 pens, the monetary cost of producing 1,000 pens is ₹50,000.
