# Total Revenue, Average Revenue and Marginal Revenue under Imperfectly Competitive Market

An *imperfectly competitive market* is a situation where perfect competition is absent. Examples include monopoly, duopoly, oligopoly, and monopolistic competition. In these markets, especially in monopoly and monopolistic competition, the seller typically has to *reduce the price* to sell more units. This means the firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve.

To increase sales, the firm must lower its price. As a result, total revenue increases, but usually at a decreasing rate (after a certain point). Crucially, there is a difference between Average Revenue and Marginal Revenue. Because the price decreases to sell more, the Average Revenue curve slopes downwards from left to right. Since the firm must lower the price on *all* units to sell an additional unit, Marginal Revenue decreases more rapidly than Average Revenue.

Therefore, the Marginal Revenue curve lies *below* the Average Revenue curve in imperfectly competitive markets.

*The graph displays the Average Revenue (AR) curve and the Marginal Revenue (MR) curve for a firm in an imperfectly competitive market. The X-axis represents Output (Unit) Sale, and the Y-axis represents Revenue. Both the AR and MR curves are downward-sloping straight lines. The MR curve is positioned below the AR curve, indicating that Marginal Revenue decreases faster than Average Revenue as output increases.*

In the diagram, the Average Revenue curve slopes downwards from left to right, showing that to sell more, the price must be reduced. Because the price is reduced for all units to increase sales, Marginal Revenue decreases more rapidly compared to Average Revenue, and thus the Marginal Revenue curve lies below the Average Revenue curve. The reason for the rapid decrease in Marginal Revenue is that when the price is reduced to increase sales, this lower price applies not just to the additional unit but also to all earlier units that could have been sold at a higher price.
