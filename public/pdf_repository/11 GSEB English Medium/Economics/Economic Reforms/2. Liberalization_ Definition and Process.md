This section defines economic liberalization in the Indian context and describes the specific policy changes and regulatory shifts that were part of this process after 1991.

# Liberalization
**Liberalization** means increasing the freedom of private enterprise by allowing greater play of market forces and reducing state interference. It involves relaxing policy restrictions imposed on economic activity.

Increasing the role of the private sector and market-oriented processes in economic planning, in place of state-regulated economic processes, is called **liberalization of the Indian economy**. In the process of liberalization:

-   There is a gradual decline in the controls imposed by the state on economic functioning.
-   The role of market forces of demand and supply increases in economic decision-making.
-   The private sector is systematically allowed to enter investment areas previously reserved for the public sector.
-   Protection granted by the state to domestic industries against foreign competition is systematically reduced. This means the discrimination between domestic and foreign enterprises by the state is gradually narrowed down. For example, restrictions on cheaper or better quality imports, previously in place to enable sales of domestic goods, are reduced.

## Process of Liberalization in India
Liberalization in India was implemented gradually. Initially, investment rules were simplified for domestic producers and investors, and subsequently for foreign investors. Similarly, the consumer goods sector was first opened up for investment by foreign companies, followed by the service sector, and then the financial sector. Thus, it was a strategic liberalization process.

Bringing about these economic changes, also called **economic reforms**, required systematic changes in policy regulations. Some important regulatory changes enacted by the Indian legislative body included:

-   The **MRTP Act** (Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969), which prevented enterprises from growing very large and establishing monopolies, was replaced by the **Competition Act, 2002**. This new Act aimed at reducing unhealthy competition among enterprises.
-   **FERA** (Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, 1973), which regulated foreign exchange earnings and transactions, was replaced by **FEMA** (Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999). The word 'regulatory' was replaced by 'Management', indicating a shift in approach.
-   Major changes were made in the industrial policy. One noteworthy change was opening up areas previously reserved exclusively for public sector investment to the private sector. Now, only three sectors remain reserved for the public sector: atomic energy, some minerals related to atomic energy, and railways. Another significant change was raising the investment limit defining small scale units, allowing them to adopt modernization with higher investment.
-   The procedure for foreign investment became more investor-friendly, with the introduction of an 'automatic licensing route' for investment by foreign companies in many sectors in India.
-   Besides relaxations in industrial policy, relaxations in export-import rules were announced. Foreign exchange was allowed to be converted at market rates instead of only at official rates. Expenditure on subsidies was also targeted for reduction as part of fiscal policy changes.
