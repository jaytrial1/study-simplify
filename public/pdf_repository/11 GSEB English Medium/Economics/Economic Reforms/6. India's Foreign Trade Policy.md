This section discusses India's foreign trade policy, outlining the challenges it faced before and after the 1991 reforms and how the policy evolved to address these challenges and align with global trade practices.

# India's Foreign Trade Policy
This policy regulates India's trade with other countries.
India's foreign trade policy always faced the following challenges:

-   The need to control certain imports to protect domestic production from competition by foreign goods.
-   Ensuring sufficient imports of technology, machines, spare parts, and resources to help increase domestic production and facilitate import substitution.
-   Controlling imports of goods unessential for India's development to save scarce foreign exchange.
-   Encouraging exports to earn foreign exchange needed to pay for necessary imports.
-   Many Indian goods struggled to compete against the quality of foreign goods, making increasing exports a challenge for trade policy.
-   Promoting the export of goods produced by the small scale sector.
-   The challenges of foreign trade are distinct from domestic trade as foreign trade involves foreign exchange.

India's foreign trade policy was formulated and amended over time to deal with these challenges in the best possible manner.

## Stages of India's Foreign Trade
Let us understand India's foreign trade policy in two sections:
1.  Foreign trade policy before globalization (1991)
2.  Foreign trade policy after globalization (1991)

### India's Foreign Trade Policy before Globalization (1991)
In the initial years after independence, India focused on setting up its administrative, economic, and state functions in the national interest. After experiencing a long period of foreign rule that began with a trading company, India was cautious about many imported goods. Simultaneously, due to poverty and scarcity of foreign exchange, India was compelled to regulate imports. Exports were also limited.

India adopted a mixed economic system after independence and pursued strategic development through planning. It began establishing basic large-scale industries, which required importing costly machinery, technology, and spare parts. This led to a scarcity of foreign exchange, and restrictions were imposed on imports of consumer goods.

Hence, in the initial years, the foreign trade policy emphasized measures to protect domestic industry against foreign competition, imposed various import restrictions, and introduced export promotion measures. Later, alongside traditional export items like agricultural produce, handicrafts, gems, and jewellery, exports of non-traditional industrial goods were also promoted.

Exchange rates were officially determined, and the rupee was devalued to increase exports and reduce imports. While devaluation made imports costly, imports did not significantly decline as the items were necessary for India's industrialization. Import bills increased, resulting in a deficit in India's **Balance of Payments**. A policy of **import substitution** was also adopted. Import substitution is a policy of replacing imports with domestically produced goods, thereby reducing imports.

### India's Foreign Trade Policy after Globalization (1991)
After 1991, economic policies in India were reformed to enhance trade and investments. The foreign trade policy shifted from a restrictive, inward-looking approach to an outward-looking one. The Indian rupee was allowed to be converted into foreign currencies at market rates, moving away from earlier official conversion. Import-export licensing was simplified; strict licensing now exists only for Crude oil, edible oils, and chemical fertilizers.

With the promotion of FDI and privatization, foreign companies could now sell a variety of goods in India. After globalization, India's trade with non-traditional trade partners or new countries increased. The new trade policy aimed at increasing India's percentage share in world trade. India became a member of the **World Trade Organization (WTO)** in 1995, and trade policy changes were made according to WTO rules, such as changes in import-export rules for agricultural goods and trade-related investment measures.
