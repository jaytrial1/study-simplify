This section provides an assessment of the economic reforms initiated in India since 1991, discussing both the positive (favorable) and negative (unfavorable) effects observed over the subsequent years.

# Evaluation of Economic Reforms
From 1951, India adopted a socialist pattern of planning and, aiming for an egalitarian society, implemented narrow, regulated, and inward-looking economic policies. After 1991, India transformed these policies to be liberal, market-oriented, and outward-looking to promote trade and development.

This transformation is known as the process of **economic reforms**. An evaluation of these reforms after almost 25 years can be done by examining their favorable and unfavorable effects.

## Favorable Effects of Economic Reforms
The economic policy reforms in the form of liberalization, privatization, and globalization increased the significance of the market forces of demand and supply. This led to the determination of prices, wages, and interest rates becoming more market-oriented, realistic, and less regulated. Additionally, with reduced regulations, decisions regarding production, investment, and distribution also became market-oriented. The strict difference between domestic and foreign investments narrowed down. Several favorable effects for India were observed:

-   Consumers gained easier access to a variety of goods of international quality at reasonable prices.
-   India's foreign exchange reserves increased.
-   India's exports increased.
-   Along with increased FDI, the risk associated with certain investments and the state's debt burden for importing costly technology reduced.
-   Large-scale investments increased in the private sector, which in turn boosted production and employment.
-   Factors of production became more mobile within the nation and between nations.
-   In the era of excessive regulations, corruption, bureaucratic hurdles, delays in decision-making, and administrative inflexibility were common features. These were found to have gradually reduced after the reforms.
-   Certain sectors significant for growth but previously neglected due to capital scarcity and government regulations received a boost from private sector investment initiatives (e.g., natural gas pipelines, modernization of railways).
-   Shortages of goods and services became less common; instead, variety increased.
-   Social and cultural ties with other nations improved.

## Unfavorable Effects of Economic Reforms
The new economic policies also raised some social concerns in India, including:

-   Small and cottage industries struggled to withstand competition from multinational companies.
-   Globalization started concurrently with privatization. Before the Indian private sector became fully efficient, Indian companies faced competition from foreign companies, leading to setbacks for some.
-   Subsidies were reduced in many sectors, making services in these sectors more expensive.
-   Exchange rate determination (determining the value of the Indian currency against foreign currencies) was left to the market, leading to greater fluctuations. Many companies suffered due to these fluctuations.
-   Some foreign companies began selling their goods at unusually low prices in India. This practice, called **'dumping'**, caused setbacks for many Indian companies selling similar goods as they could not produce and sell at such low prices.
-   Many policies of the World Trade Organization imposed strict quality measures, posing difficulties for exports from countries like India, particularly for agricultural goods.
-   Infrastructural facilities like electricity and roads proved insufficient to cope with the speed of privatization and globalization.
-   Inequalities of economic power increased.
-   The production and sale of lifestyle goods increased at the expense of necessities.
-   Some people believe that the social-cultural foundations of India are threatened because of globalization.
