This section outlines the main economic thoughts of Mahatma Gandhi, presenting his alternative approach to Western capitalist and socialist models, focusing on Sarvodaya, labour, machinery, decentralization, simplicity, non-possession, and trusteeship.

# Gandhiji's Core Economic Philosophy

Gandhiji believed that neither capitalist nor socialist economic approaches could fully solve India's economic problems. He proposed alternative economic ideas countering Western capitalist production, excessive machinery use, and materialistic philosophy.

## Sarvodaya

Gandhiji envisioned a non-violent society where the helpless, poor, and needy are uplifted. This idea of social development is called **'Sarvodaya'**, meaning **'upliftment of all'**. Based on the Bhagwad Gita's teaching that the same soul dwells in all beings, Sarvodaya is founded on mutual cooperation and love. He suggested that this form of socialism could be achieved by forsaking desires that lead to greed.

For successful Sarvodaya, he emphasized:
*   Renunciation
*   Service
*   Reducing dependence on machinery
*   Protection of labour
*   Decentralization of power
*   Prevention of exploitation

He disliked the concentration of wealth in the hands of a few due to machinery and industry. For him, socialism meant equality and the absence of differences between people.

## Importance of Labour

Gandhiji promoted **equitable remuneration** for different types of labour. He saw labour as the only living factor of production, entitling anyone who labours to a dignified life. He believed the state was obligated to create employment opportunities for all.

While acknowledging the importance of mental labour, Gandhiji asserted that everyone should engage in some form of **physical labour**. He observed a decline in the dignity of physical labour in modern times and stressed that dedicated physical effort helps overcome greed, provides employment, and leads to freedom from poverty.

## Use of Machinery

Gandhiji was often perceived as being against machines, but he was not opposed to their **appropriate use**. He opposed the reckless use of machines that replaced human labour and caused unemployment.

His views on machinery can be summarized:
*   He recommended simple machines that poor producers could own and use.
*   Machines that facilitate labour should be used; those that replace labour widely should not.
*   He did not oppose machines used for the welfare of the poor.
*   Machinery that reduces physical labour, saves time, and funds could be widely employed.
*   He cautioned against machines that enhance profits for a few individuals or lead to the concentration of economic power, becoming a tool for exploitation.
*   He asserted that machines are for human beings, not the reverse, and dependence on machines should not become excessive.

## Decentralized Economy

Gandhiji favored a **decentralized economic system** for India to distribute economic power widely, not just among a few. He believed that the development of villages should be the central focus of economic development, achievable through decentralized planning.

He proposed decentralized economic planning as an alternative to capitalist planning and a solution to the problems of centralized economies. He promoted the use of **'khadi'** and spinning khadi as a household industry using the **'charkha'** (spinning wheel). This concept was rooted in the idea of **'Gram Swaraj'** (village self-reliance).

## Simplicity and Non-Possession

Gandhiji believed that economic problems stem from greed and the relentless pursuit of material possessions, often disregarding ethics. He advocated **non-possession** and consumption based on **needs, not greed**. He advised limiting wants and striving for contentment. His personal principle was **'simple living and high thinking'**.

He identified reasons for restlessness:
*   Continuously increasing wants
*   Use of complex machines
*   Unequal distribution systems

Gandhiji stated that welfare increases when wants are limited, and true happiness lies in simple living. Production and distribution should be based on people's needs. He insisted on the idea of **'everyone's livelihood without anyone's exploitation'**.

## Doctrine of Trusteeship

Trusteeship is a key principle inspired by the Bhagwad Gita and the Ishopanishad. The Ishopanishad states, "All that is created on the earth is because of God and hence we must relish it after surrendering it."

According to Gandhiji, if a person has accumulated wealth beyond what is necessary for a dignified living, that excess wealth belongs to society. The wealthy and capitalists should voluntarily act as **trustees** of this excess wealth. They retain stewardship and use their talent to increase wealth, not for themselves, but for the nation and society, without exploitation.

Key aspects of the doctrine of trusteeship:

### Convincing the Rich (Change of Heart)
*   Based on truth and non-violence, trusteeship relies on voluntary action and a **'technique of change of heart'**.
*   Confiscating wealth or imposing high taxes were not considered appropriate methods for redistribution.
*   Trusteeship meant being responsible for one's own life and that of others.
*   Excess wealth, accumulated using societal resources, should be used for the poor's benefit through a trust managed by the wealthy themselves.
*   Society should inherit excess wealth, not the individual.

### Duty Instead of Right
*   The rich should feel responsible for using excess wealth for society's benefit, viewing it as a duty rather than just a right.

### Awareness, Referendum and Social Change
*   Trusteeship is a source of radical social change, brought about voluntarily, not by force.
*   Gandhiji did not support very high taxes or forceful confiscation but suggested building public opinion for social change.

### Importance of the Interest of the Whole Society
*   Gandhiji believed in working for the greater good, benefiting a large number of people over a few individuals.
*   He emphasized large-scale production of goods necessary for the masses. The interest of the larger society is more important than that of individuals.

### Compensation to the Trustee
*   Gandhiji believed the state should compensate trustees for their service.

### Opposition to Nationalization
*   Gandhiji opposed nationalizing production resources and abolishing private property rights to achieve economic equality. He felt this would decrease morals and turn people against the state.
*   He would allow capitalists and landlords to retain ownership but convince them to become trustees for the good of society.

### Appointing the Successor
*   The legal owners should be the official trustees, acting as representatives of society. Society implicitly owns the resources.
*   The current trustee can name a successor, who will also be a representative, not an owner.
*   The state or society cannot seize trust property. Succession should follow a legal procedure.
*   While the owner names the successor, the state must monitor the trust's functioning.

### State Control
*   If owners do not voluntarily form a trust, the state can, in certain cases, direct the use of private wealth with minimal force.
*   This applies only to some forms of wealth, evaluated based on accumulation methods.
*   The state can take control of certain private property types after providing adequate compensation.

Gandhiji identified three forms of ownership in this context: (1) Private sector ownership, (2) Formation of a trust, (3) Public sector ownership.

Gandhiji also shared thoughts on the 'swadeshi movement', cooperation, the importance of small, cottage, and village industries, equality, and the dignity of women. His ideas are rooted in welfare and humanism, offering potential solutions to modern problems like poverty, unemployment, inequality, and economic power concentration.
