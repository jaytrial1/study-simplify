# Methods of Creating Agency

The relationship of principal and agent can be established through several methods, each with distinct legal implications.

*   **Q:** Explain different method of creation at Agency.
*   **Q:** What are the different modes in which an agency may be created?

> **Answer:** An agency may be created by:
> 1.  **Express Agreement:** Authority is explicitly given, either orally or in writing (e.g., Power of Attorney).
> 2.  **Implied Agreement:** Arises from the conduct, situation, or relationship of the parties.
> 3.  **Estoppel or Holding Out:** Occurs when a person's words or conduct lead another to believe that a certain person is their agent, preventing them from denying the agency later.
> 4.  **Necessity:** Arises in emergencies where an agent acts without authority to protect the principal's interests from loss, provided certain conditions are met.
> 5.  **Ratification:** The principal confirms and approves an unauthorized act done on their behalf by an agent, making it binding as if it were previously authorized.

## Agency by Express Agreement

Typically, an agency contract is formed by an *express agreement*, where the principal explicitly grants authority to the agent. This agreement can be oral or written. A common written form is a *Power of Attorney* on stamped paper, which can be general, special, or particular.

## Agency by Implied Agreement

An agency agreement can be *implied* from the conduct, situation, or relationship of the parties (Sec. 187).

**Example:** An elder brother managed property with the mother's active association and sold suit land. A younger brother, aware of the transaction and living elsewhere, allowed the purchaser possession for 7 years. He later executed a document with other brothers affirming the elder brother's acts. This was held to be a case of implied agency, making the sale valid.

## Agency by Estoppel or Holding Out

*   **Q:** Short note: Agency by estoppel (Holding out).

> **Answer:** Agency by estoppel, or holding out, arises when a person, through their conduct, words (spoken or written), or actions, willfully leads another person to believe that a certain individual is their agent. In such a scenario, the person who created this belief is *estopped* (prevented) from later denying the existence of the agency. This type of agency is created by implication of law, binding the principal to acts done by the "agent" within the scope of the induced belief, even if no actual authority was given. (Sec. 237)

*Estoppel* means a person is prevented from denying a previously made statement. Where a person's conduct or words lead another to believe someone is their agent, they are estopped from denying it later, thus creating agency by implication of law.

Section 237 states: "When an agent has, without authority, done acts or incurred obligations to third persons on behalf of his principal, the principal is bound by such acts or obligations if he has by his words or conduct induced such third persons to believe that such acts and obligations were within the scope of the agent's authority."

## Agency of Necessity

*   **Q:** Short note: Agency of Necessity.

> **Answer:** Agency of necessity occurs when an agent acts without prior authority to protect their principal from loss, especially in emergency situations. This arises under specific circumstances: (a) the agent could not communicate with the principal, (b) the agent took all reasonable steps to protect the principal's interests, (c) there was a genuine and actual necessity, and (d) the agent acted in good faith. A common example is a wife pledging her husband's credit for necessaries if she is not properly provided for or has been deserted.

A contract of agency can arise due to urgent circumstances. An agent gains authority to act to protect the principal from loss, even without prior authorization. This is called 'agency of necessity' and typically arises if:
*   The agent could not communicate with the principal.
*   The agent took all reasonable steps to protect the principal's interest.
*   There was a genuine and actual necessity.
*   The agent acted *bonafide* (in good faith).

**Example:** A consigns provisions to B at Calcutta with instructions to send them immediately to Cuttack. If the provisions will spoil on the journey to Cuttack, B may sell them at Calcutta under agency of necessity.

### Husband and Wife

A wife can be an agent of necessity, having the power to pledge her husband's credit for life's necessaries if:
*   She is not properly provided for by him, or
*   She has been deserted by the husband.

This is based on the principle that a husband is bound to maintain his wife. A wife living with her husband has implied authority to buy household necessities.

## Agency by Ratification

*   **Q:** Explain what is meant by 'Agency by Ratification'. State the requisites of a valid ratification.
*   **Q:** Explain: Agency by Ratification.
*   **Q:** What is ratification? Discuss the essential requisites of a Valid ratification.
*   **Q:** What is Ratification? Discuss its essential elements.

> **Answer:** *Agency by Ratification* means that when a person (the agent) performs an act on behalf of another (the principal) without prior knowledge or authority, the principal has the option to either confirm (ratify) or disown that unauthorized act. If the principal chooses to ratify, the act becomes as legally binding as if it had been authorized from the beginning. This concept is often summarized as "ratification tantamounts to prior authority" or "ratification relates back" to the date the act was performed.
>
> **Requisites of a Valid Ratification:**
> 1.  **Agent must act as agent:** The person must have purported to act as an agent for an identifiable principal at the time of the contract.
> 2.  **Principal must be in existence:** The principal must exist at the time the contract was made.
> 3.  **Principal must have contractual capacity:** The principal must have been competent to contract both at the time of the original act and at the time of ratification.
> 4.  **Full knowledge of facts:** Ratification must be made with complete knowledge of all material facts; defective knowledge invalidates it.
> 5.  **Lawful and valid act:** The act to be ratified must be legal and not void or illegal.
> 6.  **Reasonable time:** Ratification must occur within a reasonable time.
> 7.  **Whole act to be ratified:** The principal must ratify the entire transaction; they cannot ratify a part and reject the rest.
> 8.  **Communication:** Ratification must be communicated to the party intended to be bound.
> 9.  **Principal's power to do the act:** The act must be one that the principal himself had the power to perform.
> 10. **No third-party damages:** Ratification should not subject a third person to damages or terminate their rights or interests.

### Meaning

When an agent exceeds their authority or acts without authority, the principal can choose to disown the act (making it not binding) or confirm and *ratify* it. If ratified, the same consequences follow as if the act had been previously authorized. This is expressed as "ratification tantamounts to prior authority" or "ratification relates back" to the date of the act. Essentially, ratification is the affirmation of an agent's unauthorized acts.

### Effect of Ratification

"Where acts are done by one person on behalf of another, but without his knowledge or authority, he may elect to ratify or to disown such acts. If he ratifies them, the same effect will follow as if they had been performed by his authority." (Sec. 196)

Ratification can be *express* or *implied* (Sec. 197).

**Example:** A, without B's authority, lends B's money to C. Afterwards B accepts interest on the money from C. B's conduct implies a ratification of the loan.

The underlying rule is *Omnis ratihabitio retrotrahitur at priori mandato aequiparatur*, meaning every ratification relates back to and is equivalent to a previous command or authority.

### Requisites of Valid Ratification

For a ratification to be valid, it must meet the following conditions (Sections 196 to 200):
1.  **The agent must expressly contract as agent:** The person must purport to act as an agent for an identifiable principal who is in contemplation at the time of the contract. An act done by someone for themselves cannot be ratified.
2.  **The principal must be in existence at the time of the contract:** For instance, a company cannot ratify contracts made by promoters before its incorporation.
3.  **The principal must have contractual capacity both at the time of the contract and at the time of ratification:** Since ratification is equivalent to prior authority, the principal must have been competent to authorize the transaction from its inception.
4.  **Ratification must be with full knowledge of facts:** "No valid ratification can be made by a person whose knowledge of facts of the case is materially defective." (Sec. 198)

    **Example:** A has authority from B to buy a particular car model at market rate. A buys at a higher rate, but B accepts. Later, B discovers the car belonged to A. The ratification is not binding on B.
5.  **The act to be ratified must be lawful and not void or illegal:** There can be no ratification of an illegal or void act. The act must be valid in itself.
6.  **Ratification must be done within a reasonable time.**
7.  **The whole act to be ratified:** "A person ratifying any unauthorised act done on his behalf ratifies the whole of the transaction of which such act formed a part." (Sec. 199) A principal cannot ratify only a part of the transaction and reject the rest.

    **Example:** A, an agent, buys 100 cows and 10 horses for his principal B in one transaction. B cannot ratify the purchase of 100 cows while rejecting the purchase of 10 horses.
8.  **Ratification must be communicated:** For effectiveness, communication of ratification to the party sought to be bound by the agent's act is essential.
9.  **Ratification can be of acts which the principal had the power to do:** An act that the principal himself is incapable of doing cannot be ratified (e.g., a minor's act).
10. **Ratification should not put a third party to damages:** "An act done by one person on behalf of another without such other person's authority, which, if done with authority, would have the effect of subjecting a third person to damages, or of terminating any right or interest of a third person, cannot, by ratification, be made to have such effect." (Sec. 200)

    **Example:** A holds a lease from B, terminable on three months' notice. C, an unauthorized person, gives notice of termination to A. This notice cannot be ratified by B to be binding on A.
