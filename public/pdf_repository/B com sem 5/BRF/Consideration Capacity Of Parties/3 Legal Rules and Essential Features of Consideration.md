3_Legal Rules and Essential Features of Consideration

This section outlines the fundamental legal rules governing consideration, detailing what constitutes valid consideration and providing examples of each principle.

# Legal Rules and Essential Features of Consideration

The following are the essential elements of consideration:

*   To constitute consideration, there must be an act, abstinence, or promise on the part of the promisee or some other person at the desire of the promisor.
*   A consideration to be valid must be "good" or "valuable" in the eyes of law.
*   It should not be "sham."
*   Consideration need not be "adequate" – adequacy being a matter purely for contracting parties to decide and to agree upon, but it must be "real."
*   Consideration is what moves from the promisee, whether it be an advantage to the promisor or a detriment suffered by the promisee.
*   Consideration is something different from motive.

## It Must Move at the Desire of the Promisor

Consideration must proceed at the request of the promisor. The act done or loss suffered by the promisee must have been done or suffered at the desire of the promisor. Acts done voluntarily or gratuitously or at the request of a third person do not constitute good consideration in the eyes of law.

**Examples:**
*   (i) A saves B from drowning in the river without being asked to do so. A cannot demand payment for his services. It is a gratuitous act.
*   (ii) B constructed a market at the desire of the Collector of the district. D promised to pay B a commission on articles sold through D's agency in the market. This was in consideration of money expended by B on the construction of the market. Held, the agreement was void being without consideration as B had not constructed the market at the desire of D. (*Durga Prasad v. Baldev* (1880) 3 All. 221.)

## It May Move from the Promisee or Any Other Person

Consideration may move from the promisee or, if the promisee has no objection, then from any other person. Though consideration for an agreement may proceed from a third party, it does not follow that the third party can sue on the consideration. Under English Law, consideration must move from the promisee. In contrast, under Indian Law, consideration can move from any person.

**Example:** A, a daughter, in consideration of some property received from her mother, entered into an agreement with C, her uncle, to pay him an annuity. Later on, she refused to pay on the ground that her uncle, a promisee, had not given consideration. It was held that consideration had moved from her mother, and as such, she was liable to pay. (*Chinnayya v. Ramaya* (1886) 14 Cal. 64.)

**Q: State the legal rules as to 'consideration.'**
**Q: Explain its essential elements.**
> **Answer:** The essential legal rules as to consideration are:
> 1.  It must move at the desire of the promisor: The act or abstinence must be done at the specific request of the promisor, not voluntarily or at the request of a third party.
> 2.  It may move from the promisee or any other person: Unlike English law, Indian law allows consideration to be provided by someone other than the promisee.
> 3.  It may be past, present, or future: Consideration can relate to an act already done, being done concurrently, or promised to be done in the future.
> 4.  It need not be adequate but must be real: The value of consideration is not scrutinized by law, but it must not be illusory, physically impossible, legally impossible, or relate to an already existing public duty.
> 5.  It must not be illegal, immoral, or opposed to public policy: The consideration must be lawful.
