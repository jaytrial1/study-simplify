4_Privity of Contract and Its Exceptions

This section clarifies the doctrine of "privity of contract," explaining who can sue on a contract under English and Indian law, and detailing the exceptions to this general rule in India.

# Privity of Contract and Its Exceptions

## Can a Person Who is Not a Party to a Contract Sue Upon It?

### English Law

Normally, the rule is that consideration must move from the promisee, and only a party to a contract can sue. In other words, a stranger to a consideration cannot sue.

*   In *Dutton v. Poole* (1668), a father was about to cut timber to provide a marriage portion for his daughter. His son intervened and agreed to pay £1,000 to the daughter if the father did not cut the timber. In an action by the daughter on the son's promise, the Court held that if the persons from whom the promise was made are related by ties of blood, natural love, and affection, it might be presumed that meritorious consideration moved from the person suing. This was sometimes called the doctrine of "Constructive Consideration."
*   However, this doctrine has since been disclaimed. In *Tweddle v. Atkinson* (1861), an agreement was made between the respective fathers of a bride and her intended husband, that the bride's father should pay £200 to the husband in consideration of the bride's father-in-law similarly paying £100. A suit was instituted by the husband against the executors of his father-in-law's estate. The Court negatived the doctrine of constructive consideration and held the promise to be unenforceable.

**Thus, under English law:**
(a) A stranger to a consideration cannot sue.
    *   **Illustration:** Suppose A, a doctor, agrees to treat B, but as A will not accept payment, B promises C (A's son) that he will pay him Rs. 500/-. C cannot maintain a suit on the promise because he is a stranger to the consideration, and the fact of C being A's son will not alter the position.
(b) A stranger to a contract also cannot sue.
    *   **Illustration:** If a doctor agrees to treat B and makes a promise to A that he will pay C a sum of Rs. 1000/-. Can 'C' sue? In this illustration, the promise is made to A, so C is a stranger to the consideration as well as to the contract. A stranger to a contract can never sue.

**Q: Can a stranger sue on contract?**
**Q: Explain the meaning of 'Privity of contract'.**
> **Answer:** The doctrine of "privity of contract" states that a contract is a private relationship between the parties who make it, and therefore, the rights and obligations under such a contract are strictly confined to them. This means that a person who is not a party to the contract generally cannot sue upon it, even if the contract is for their benefit. Under English law, a stranger to the consideration and a stranger to the contract cannot sue.

### Indian Law

The law in India is practically the same as English law regarding privity of contract, meaning a stranger to a contract cannot generally sue. However, under Indian law, consideration may move from the "promisee or any other person." Therefore, it is clear that under Indian law, consideration may move from any person.

**Example:** An old lady, by a deed of gift, made over certain properties to her daughter D, under the direction that she should pay her aunt, P (sister of old lady), a certain sum of money. The same day, D entered into an agreement with P to pay her the agreed amount. D later on, refused to pay the amount on the plea that no consideration had moved from P to D. Held, P was entitled to maintain suit as consideration had moved from the old lady, sister of P, to the daughter D. (*Chinnyya v. Ramaya* 18824 Mad 137.)

Thus, in light of the clear definition given by the Indian Contract Act, consideration might move from any person.

## Exceptions to the Rule: 'A Stranger to a Contract Cannot Sue in India'

Under Indian law, the following are the exceptions to the rule that a stranger to a contract cannot sue:

1.  **Beneficiaries in the case of trust:** An agreement to create a trust can be enforced by the beneficiary, even if they were not a party to the contract between the settlor and the trustees.
    *   **Example:** A agrees to transfer certain properties to B to be held by B in trust for the benefit of P. P can enforce the agreement, even though he is not a party to the agreement. (*M. K. Rapai V. John*, AIR 1965 Ker 203.)
2.  **Marriage settlement, partition, or other family arrangements:** A party, although a stranger to the contract, can sue to enforce a benefit where it is due to them under a marriage settlement, partition, or other family settlement.
    *   **Example:** R's father entered into an agreement for her marriage with J; R was a minor at the time of the agreement. Held, R, after attaining majority, could sue J for damages for breach of promise of marriage. (*Rose Femandes v. Joseph Gonslaves* 192448 Bom 673.)
3.  **Acknowledgment or Estoppel:** Where the promisor, by their conduct, acknowledges or otherwise constitutes themselves as an agent of the third party, a binding obligation is thereby incurred towards them.
    *   **Example:** D receives some money from T to be paid over to P. D admits receipt to P. P can recover the amount from D, who shall be regarded as the agent of P.
4.  **Assignment of Contract:** Where an assignment takes place through operation of law, the assignee can sue on the contract. Where a person is adjudged insolvent, their properties vest in the Official Assignee, who may sue upon the contract entered into by them.
5.  **Contracts entered into through an agent:** The principal can enforce the contracts entered into by an agent provided the agent acts within the scope of their authority and in the name of the principal during the course of business.
6.  **A charge on immovable property:** A charge created on a specific immovable property in favour of a certain person can be enforced by such a person.

**Conclusion on Privity:**
*   A stranger to a consideration cannot sue in English law but can under Indian law.
*   A stranger to a contract, both under English and Indian law, cannot, as a rule, sue upon the contract. But both systems recognize certain exceptions to the rule.

**Q: 'A Stranger to a contract cannot sue in India' - Explain. State the exception to this rule.**
> **Answer:** The statement means that generally, only the parties directly involved in a contract have the right to enforce it or be bound by its obligations. A third party, even if the contract benefits them, cannot sue on it. This is known as the doctrine of privity of contract.
>
> **Exceptions to this rule in India are:**
> 1.  Beneficiaries in the case of a trust.
> 2.  Marriage settlements, partition, or other family arrangements where a benefit is due to a stranger.
> 3.  Acknowledgment or Estoppel, where the promisor acknowledges themselves as an agent for the third party.
> 4.  Assignment of a contract through operation of law (e.g., insolvency).
> 5.  Contracts entered into through an agent where the principal can enforce the contract.
> 6.  A charge created on immovable property in favor of a specific person.
