8_Promises to Charities

This section discusses the enforceability of promises made towards charitable causes, examining case law that illustrates when such pledges may become binding under Indian law.

# Promises to Charities

A promise to make a contribution to charity is generally not enforceable because it is without consideration. A note executed by the promisor for arrears of contribution to charity is no more than a repetition of a voluntary promise and is not enforceable. (*Jamuna V. Ram*, 169 I C 369).

However, in a Calcutta decision of *Kedarnath V. Gorie Mohamad* (1886 14 Cal 64), the defendant promised to pay Rs. 1,000/- towards the construction of the Town Hall, and the Trustees on such promise engaged contractors to build the Hall. The defendant subsequently refused to pay and hence the suit. It was held that ordinarily, subscriptions to a charitable object were not recoverable, but if the promisors knew the purpose of the charity and also knew that on the strength of their promise, obligations may be undertaken to third parties, the promise is enforceable.

It may be noted that this decision is contrary to English case law. For example, in another case (*Abdul Aziz V: Masum Ali* (1914) 36 All. 268.), where a Muslim subscribed Rs. 500/- to rebuild a mosque, it was decided in that case the promise was without consideration and that the subscriber was not liable.

It may now be taken as settled law in India that "a promise to pay a subscription becomes enforceable as soon as any definite steps have been taken in furtherance of the object and on the faith of the promised subscription."
*   **Example:** K promised to pay Rs. 10,000 to the society for the starting of a college. Definite steps were taken on the faith of that promise for the starting of the college. Held, the promise is now enforceable. (*N. S. Society V. Kunjukrishna Pillai*, A.I.R. (1965) Ker. 265.)

**Q: Short note: Promise to charities.**
> **Answer:** Generally, a promise to contribute to charity is not enforceable as it lacks consideration. However, Indian law, particularly through cases like *Kedarnath V. Gorie Mohamad*, has established an exception: if the promisee (e.g., a charity or its trustees) undertakes definite steps or incurs obligations to third parties based on the promisor's pledge, knowing that such actions would be taken, the promise becomes enforceable. This principle holds that if the charity acts upon the promise by incurring a liability, the promise is then supported by implied consideration (the detriment suffered by the charity) and becomes binding.
