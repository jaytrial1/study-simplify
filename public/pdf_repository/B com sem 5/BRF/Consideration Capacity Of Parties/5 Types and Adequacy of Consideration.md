5_Types and Adequacy of Consideration

This section categorizes consideration into past, present, and future forms, explaining their validity, particularly contrasting English and Indian law on past consideration, and emphasizing that consideration must be real, even if not adequate.

# Types and Adequacy of Consideration

## Types of Consideration: Past, Present, or Future

Consideration may be classified into three types: (i) past, (ii) present, and (iii) future. Section 2(d) of the Indian Contract Act uses the words "...has done or abstained from doing (i.e., past consideration) or does or abstains from doing (i.e., present consideration) or promises to do or abstain from doing (i.e., future consideration) something..."

Consideration may be both positive and negative (i.e., 'an act to do or not to do'). Forbearance to sue, as seen earlier, will also be good consideration.

(a) **Past consideration:** When the consideration of one party was given before the date of the promise, it is said to be past.
    *   **Example:** A finds B's purse and returns it (without expecting any reward). B promises to pay him some money. The consideration by B is past consideration.
    *   **English Law Compared:** Under English law, past consideration is generally no consideration, and a contract based on past consideration is void.
    *   **Indian Law:** In Indian law, past consideration is good consideration because the definition of consideration as per Section 2(d) itself includes the words "has done or abstained from doing."

(b) **Present or executed consideration:** Consideration which moves simultaneously with the promise is called present consideration or executed consideration.
    *   **Example:** B buys an article from a shop and pays the price immediately. The consideration moving from B is present or executed consideration.

(c) **Future or executory consideration:** When the consideration is to move at a future date, it is called future or executory consideration. In a contract, consideration may be executory on both sides.
    *   **Example:** A promises to pay money at a future date for goods to be delivered by B at a future date, which is a valid contract. Here, the consideration of both A and B is executory.

**Q: 'Past consideration is a good consideration in India'. - Explain and state the English law on the subject.**
> **Answer:** In India, past consideration is considered valid. This is explicitly supported by Section 2(d) of the Indian Contract Act, which includes the phrase "has done or abstained from doing," referring to actions completed in the past at the promisor's desire. This means a promise made now for an act that was voluntarily performed in the past for the promisor's benefit can be legally enforceable. In contrast, under English law, past consideration is generally not considered valid consideration, and a contract based solely on it would be void, as consideration must be given at the time of or after the promise, not before.

## Adequacy and Reality of Consideration

### It Need Not Be Adequate

Consideration, whether in the sense of benefit or detriment, need not be adequate to the promise. Law requires the presence of consideration but does not inquire about its adequacy, unless it is so grossly shocking as to serve as presumptive evidence of want of real consent. The courts do not exist to repair bad bargains. So long as consideration exists, the matter ends. Courts are not concerned as to its adequacy provided it is of some value. Its adequacy is for the parties to consider at the time of making the agreement, not for the court when it is sought to be enforced. [Section 25 (Explanation 2)]

*   **Example:** P agrees to sell a horse worth Rs. 1,000 for Rs. 10. P's consent to the agreement was freely given. The agreement is a contract notwithstanding the inadequacy of the consideration.

### It Must Be Real and Not Illusory

The consideration, to be valid, must be 'real' and 'valuable' and must not be 'illusory' or 'sham'.

**Instances of Considerations Not Real:**
The following are considered not to be real considerations:

(i) **Physical impossibility:**
    *   **Example:** A promise to find lost treasure by magic. The consideration is said to be impossible.
(ii) **Legal impossibilities:**
    *   **Example:** A owes Rs. 500 to B. He promises to pay Rs. 100 to C, the nephew of B, who in turn promises to discharge A from the debt. This is legally impossible because C cannot give a legal discharge for a debt due to B.
(iii) **Already existing public duty:** Where the promisee is already under an existing public duty, an express promise to perform that duty will not amount to consideration.
    *   **Example:** A promises to pay B, a witness in a trial, a certain sum of money. B had already received a subpoena to appear before the court at trial. The consideration is not real. (*Collins V. Godefroy* (1815) I. B. and Ad. 950.)
(iv) **'Illusory' or "sham consideration":**
    *   **Example:** Two of the crew of a ship deserted it halfway through a voyage. The Captain thereby promised to divide their salary among the rest of the crew if they brought the vessel home. They could not recover the amount as the consideration was 'illusory'. They were already under an obligation to bring the vessel home. (*Stilk v. Myrick* (1809) 2 Camp. 317.)

**Instances of Good Consideration:**
However, it may be noted that where a person does something more than what he was bound to do, this will be good consideration for the promise. Thus, forbearance to sue, compromise of disputed claims, and compositions with creditors are illustrations of good consideration for a contract.

### It Must Not Be Illegal, Immoral, or Opposed to Public Policy

The consideration given for an agreement must be lawful. Where the consideration for a contract is illegal, immoral, or against public policy, the courts do not allow an action on such a contract (Section 23).

**Q: 'Consideration need not be adequate but it must be real'-Discuss.**
> **Answer:** This statement highlights two crucial aspects of valid consideration. "Consideration need not be adequate" means that the law does not require the value of the consideration exchanged between parties to be equal or proportionate. Parties are free to determine the value of their bargain, and courts will generally not interfere merely because the consideration seems small or insufficient. The purpose of consideration is to show that a bargain exists, not to ensure fairness of value.
>
> However, "it must be real" means that the consideration must have some actual value in the eyes of the law and not be illusory, impossible, or a mere pretense. It cannot be based on physical or legal impossibility, or on the performance of an existing duty (unless something extra is done). A "sham" or "illusory" consideration, like promising to perform an act one is already legally obligated to do, is not considered real and will invalidate the contract. Thus, while the amount of consideration is irrelevant, its genuine existence and lawful nature are essential.
