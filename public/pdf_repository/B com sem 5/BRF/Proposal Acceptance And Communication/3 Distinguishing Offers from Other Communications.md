3_Distinguishing Offers from Other Communications

This section clarifies the difference between a legally binding offer and other forms of communication that may seem like offers but do not create legal rights or liabilities.

# Differentiating an Offer from Other Statements

Not every expression of willingness to enter into a contract constitutes a legal offer. It might merely be a preliminary step. It is crucial to distinguish a true offer from:
*   A mere declaration of intention.
*   An invitation to make an offer.
*   An auction sale.

## A Mere Declaration of Intention

A simple declaration of intent to do something does not create any rights or liabilities. Casual conversations are also not considered offers.

**Case Example:** *Harvey v. Facie (1893) A.C. 552*
The plaintiffs telegraphed the defendants asking, "Will you sell us Bumper Hall Pen? Telegraph lowest cash price." The defendants replied, "Lowest price for Bumper Hall Pen £900." The plaintiffs then immediately sent another telegram: "We agree to buy Bumper Hall Pen for 900 asked by you." The defendants refused to sell. The Court ruled that merely stating the lowest price a vendor would sell for does not imply a contract to sell at that price to the inquirer.

**Case Example:** *Weeks v. Tayblad (1604) 74 E.R. 982*
The defendant casually stated he would give £100 to anyone marrying his daughter with his consent. The plaintiff married the daughter with consent and sued for the amount. The court held that such general words spoken to excite suitors were not a legal offer capable of creating a legal relationship.

## An Invitation to Make an Offer

An offer must be distinguished from an *invitation to make an offer* (often called an *invitation to treat*). This is not an offer itself, but rather an indication of willingness to negotiate or receive offers.
*   *Example:* A bookseller's catalogue advertising prices is not an "offer" but an "invitation to an offer."
*   Quotations, advertisements, and circulars typically do not constitute offers; they are invitations to the public to make an offer.

**Case Example:** *Fisher v Bell (1960) 3 All. E. R. 731*
A shop exhibited a knife with a marked price in its show-window. The question was whether this display constituted an "offer for sale." Lord Parker C. J. stated that displaying an article with a price in a shop window is merely an *invitation to treat*, not an offer for sale whose acceptance would form a contract.

### Unanswered Questions

*   **Q:** How does an 'offer' differ from (i) a mere declaration of intention (ii) an invitation to offer.

> **Answer:**
> (i) An offer differs from a mere declaration of intention because an offer is made with the specific intent to obtain assent and create a legal relationship, whereas a declaration of intention merely states a future plan or desire without any legal commitment.
> (ii) An offer differs from an invitation to offer in that an offer, once accepted, forms a binding contract. An invitation to offer, however, is a preliminary step that invites others to make an offer, which the party issuing the invitation is then free to accept or reject.

## Auction Sale

In an auction sale, articles displayed are shown with the intention that bidders will make offers for them. Therefore, a *bid* in an auction is an offer. This bid can be withdrawn at any time before the auctioneer accepts it, typically signified by the fall of the hammer.
