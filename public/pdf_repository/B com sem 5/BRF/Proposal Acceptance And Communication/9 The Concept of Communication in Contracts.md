9_The Concept of Communication in Contracts

This section defines communication in the context of contract law, emphasizing that willingness or assent must be brought to the notice of the other party. It also discusses modern means of communication for offers, acceptances, and revocations.

# Communication in Contract Law

As per Sections 2(a) and 2(b), a promisor must "signify" their assent. This "signification" and its mode are referred to as *communication* (Section 3). The phrase "signifies to another" implies that willingness or assent must be brought to the other party's notice.

As Lord Lindley noted, "A state of mind not communicated cannot be regarded in dealing between man and man." Section 3 outlines how communication of proposals, acceptances, and revocations is deemed to be made:
"The communication of proposals, the acceptance of proposals and the revocation of proposals and acceptances, respectively, are deemed to be made by any act or omission of the party proposing, accepting or revoking, by which he intends to communicate such proposals, acceptance or revocation, or which has the effect of communicating it."

Modern means of communication such as post, telegram, telephone, telex, and radio can be used for communicating offers, acceptances, or revocations.

## Contracts Through Post

When parties are face-to-face, a contract forms instantly upon unqualified acceptance. When using the post, the letter must be *actually posted*. Handing a letter to a peon is not enough; the peon must physically post it.
*   *Example:* G applied for shares in a Company. The Company's letter of acceptance was posted but did not reach him. Held, G was liable as a shareholder, as acceptance was complete upon posting. (Household Fire Insurance Co. v. Grant 4 Ex. D 216.)

## Contracts by Telephone or Telex

For instantaneous communication methods like telephone or telex, the rules differ. Communication is considered instantaneous.
*   There is no communication of acceptance if there's a sudden interruption in the line (e.g., telephone goes 'dead'). In such a case, no contract forms. (Entories Ltd. V. Miles Far East Corp. (1955) 1 All, E.R. 493)
*   Similarly, for telex or telephone conversations, the contract is not complete until the acceptance is clearly heard and understood by the offerer. (Kanayalal v. Dineshchandra (1958) M.P. 234.)
*   In telephonic conversations, the contract is made at the place where the acceptance is received. (Bhagwandas v. Girdharlal, AIR 1966. S.C. 543.)
*   *Example:* An English Company offered to a Dutch Company via Telex. The contract was formed in England because the acceptance became effective there, as Telex communication is analogous to telephone (instantaneous) and complete upon receipt by the offerer.
