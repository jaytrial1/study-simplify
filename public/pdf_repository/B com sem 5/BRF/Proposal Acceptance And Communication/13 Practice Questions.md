13_Practice Questions

This section provides a series of practice questions covering the concepts of proposal, acceptance, and communication in contract law, followed by their answers based on the chapter's content.

# Practice Questions

1.  Define offer. What are the essentials of a valid offer?
    > **Answer:** An offer (or proposal) is when one person signifies to another their willingness to do or abstain from doing something, with a view to obtaining the other's assent. Essentials of a valid offer include: capability of creating legal relationships, definiteness and certainty, communication to the offeree, being conditional (if specified), being general or specific, made with a view to obtaining assent, and not imposing the burden of acceptance.

2.  Distinguish between 'offer' and 'invitation to offer'.
    > **Answer:** An 'offer' is a definite proposal that, if accepted, immediately creates a binding contract. An 'invitation to offer' (or invitation to treat) is merely an expression of willingness to negotiate or receive offers, not intended to be binding upon receipt of a positive response. Examples of invitations to offer include advertisements, price lists, and goods displayed in shop windows.

3.  "An offer is made when, and not until it is communicated to the offeree"; Explain with illustrations.
    > **Answer:** This statement means that for an offer to be legally effective, the offeree must be aware of it. Without communication, the offeree cannot accept, and thus no contract can form. For instance, if A sends a letter offering to sell his house to B, the offer is only complete when B actually receives and reads that letter. Similarly, if a reward is offered for a lost item, a person who finds and returns the item without knowledge of the reward cannot claim it, as the offer was not communicated to them.

4.  How and when may an offer be revoked?
    > **Answer:** An offer may be revoked by the proposer communicating a notice of revocation to the offeree. It can be revoked at any time before its acceptance is complete as against the proposer, but not afterwards. Other ways an offer lapses include by the expiry of a stipulated or reasonable time, by the offeree's failure to fulfill a condition precedent, by the death or insanity of the proposer (if known to the offeree before acceptance), by a counter-offer, or by outright rejection.

5.  Define Proposal. In which circumstances proposal can be revoked?
    > **Answer:** A proposal is when one person signifies to another their willingness to do or abstain from doing anything, with a view to obtaining the other's assent. A proposal can be revoked by communication of notice of revocation, by lapse of time, by failure of the acceptor to fulfill a condition precedent, by the death or insanity of the proposer (if known to the acceptor before acceptance), by a counter-offer, or by rejection.

6.  Short notes:
    1)  Proposal.
        > **Answer:** A proposal, also known as an offer, is the initial step in contract formation where one person expresses willingness to do or abstain from doing something to another, with the intention of securing that person's agreement. It must be definite, communicated, and capable of creating legal relations.
    2)  Standing offer.
        > **Answer:** A standing offer is a continuing offer for the supply of goods or services over a specified period at a certain rate. Its acceptance does not create an immediate contract for the entire period, but rather, each individual order placed under it forms a distinct contract. It can be revoked at any time before a specific order is placed.
    3)  Lapse of an offer.
        > **Answer:** An offer lapses, or terminates, under several circumstances, making it no longer open for acceptance. These include revocation by the proposer, expiry of a stipulated or reasonable time, failure of the offeree to fulfill a condition precedent, death or insanity of the proposer (if known), a counter-offer by the offeree, or outright rejection by the offeree.

7.  Define the term "Acceptance". What are the essentials of a valid acceptance?
    > **Answer:** Acceptance is the signification of assent by the person to whom a proposal is made, turning the proposal into a promise. The essentials of a valid acceptance are: it must be absolute and unqualified, expressed in a usual or prescribed manner, made by the party to whom the offer was addressed, communicated to the offerer, made within a reasonable time, and not in ignorance of the offer.

8.  "Acceptance is to an offer what a lighted match is to a train of gun-powder. It produces something which cannot be recalled or undone." Comment.
    > **Answer:** This simile highlights the transformative and irrevocable nature of acceptance in contract law. Just as a lighted match ignites gunpowder, creating an irreversible explosion, acceptance transforms a mere offer into a binding contract. Before acceptance, an offer can lapse or be revoked, but once accepted, the agreement becomes a promise that creates legal obligations, making it too late for either party to unilaterally withdraw without consequence.

9.  "A mere mental acceptance not evidenced by words or conduct, is in the eyes of law no acceptance." Explain.
    > **Answer:** This principle emphasizes that acceptance must be outwardly manifested. For an acceptance to be legally valid, it's not enough for the offeree to simply decide in their mind to accept the offer. There must be some external act – either spoken words, written communication, or clear conduct – that signifies their assent and brings it to the knowledge of the offerer. Without such outward manifestation, there is no communication of acceptance, and thus, no contract is formed.

10. "Acceptance must be an absolute and an unqualified acceptance of all the terms of the offer." What is the effect of qualified acceptance of an offer? Explain and illustrate.
    > **Answer:** This rule means that the offeree must agree to all the terms of the offer exactly as they are presented, without any changes, additions, or conditions. A 'qualified acceptance' is one that introduces new terms or modifies the original terms. The effect of a qualified acceptance is that it does not form a contract; instead, it operates as a counter-offer. This counter-offer then terminates the original offer, and it is up to the original offerer to accept or reject this new counter-offer. For example, if A offers to sell a car for Rs. 50,000, and B replies, "I accept, but only for Rs. 45,000," B has made a qualified acceptance, which is a counter-offer, and A's original offer is now terminated.

11. "An acceptance to be effective must be communicated to the offerer." Are there any exceptions to this rule?
    > **Answer:** Yes, while communication is generally essential, there are exceptions. Communication of acceptance may not be necessary in cases where:
    *   The offer implies that performance of a condition is sufficient acceptance (e.g., in general offers where action constitutes acceptance, like in *Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.*).
    *   The offerer has expressly or impliedly waived the requirement of formal communication.
    *   Acceptance is made to an authorized agent of the offerer.
    *   In the case of acceptance by post, communication is complete as against the proposer as soon as the letter is posted, even if it hasn't reached the offerer yet.

12. Discuss in brief:
    1)  "Performance of the conditions of a proposal is an acceptance of the proposal."
        > **Answer:** This statement means that if a proposal sets out certain conditions, and the offeree performs those conditions, such performance itself constitutes acceptance of the proposal, even without explicit verbal or written communication of acceptance. This is common in general offers where the offerer invites performance as the mode of acceptance.
    2)  Acceptance cannot be presumed from mere silence of the offerer.
        > **Answer:** This statement is partially incorrect. Acceptance cannot be presumed from the mere silence *of the offeree*. The general rule is that silence does not constitute acceptance, even if the offerer states it will. However, there are exceptions where silence, coupled with benefiting from goods/services or consistent past dealings, can imply acceptance.
    3)  It is essential to communicate the acceptance of the proposal to the proposer. Performance of the conditions of a proposal, or the acceptance of any consideration cannot be considered to be acceptance of the proposal.
        > **Answer:** This statement contains a contradiction. While it is essential to communicate acceptance, the second part is incorrect. As per Section 8 of the Indian Contract Act, "Performance of the conditions of a proposal or the acceptance of any consideration for a reciprocal promise which may be offered with a proposal, is an acceptance of the proposal." Thus, performance or acceptance of consideration *can* indeed be considered acceptance by conduct.
    4)  Meaning of Standing Offer.
        > **Answer:** A standing offer is a continuous offer to supply goods or services at a specified rate over a definite period. It does not become a binding contract until a specific order is placed under it; each order creates a separate contract.

13. Write short notes on:
    1)  Acceptance.
        > **Answer:** Acceptance is the offeree's unequivocal agreement to the terms of an offer, signifying their assent and converting the proposal into a legally binding promise. It can be expressed orally, in writing, or through conduct, and must be absolute, unqualified, and communicated to the offerer within a reasonable time.
    2)  Effect of silence on acceptance.
        > **Answer:** Generally, silence by the offeree does not amount to acceptance, even if the offerer stipulates so. However, silence can imply acceptance if the offeree derives a benefit from goods or services they had a reasonable opportunity to reject, or if prior dealings between the parties have established that silence signifies assent.

14. State whether the following statements are true or false. Give brief reasons:
    1)  Acceptance should be absolute and qualified.
        > **Answer:** False. Acceptance should be *absolute and unqualified*. A qualified acceptance is a counter-offer, not a valid acceptance.
    2)  There is no difference between acceptance of an offer and a counter-offer.
        > **Answer:** False. Acceptance creates a binding contract by agreeing to all terms of the original offer. A counter-offer rejects the original offer and proposes new terms, thus not forming a contract but rather a new offer.
    3)  Mental acceptance is no acceptance at all.
        > **Answer:** True. Legal acceptance requires an outward manifestation (words or conduct) of assent, not just a mental decision. It must be communicated to the offerer.

15. (1) Ramesh inserts an advertisement, "Our land bearing survey no. 5 at Thaltej is to be sold. Minimum price Rs. 1,50,000." In response Paresh writes that he agrees to buy the land at Rs. 1,50,000. Is there a contract?
    > **Answer:** No, there is no contract yet. Ramesh's advertisement is generally considered an 'invitation to offer', not a definite offer to sell. It merely expresses an intention to sell at a minimum price and invites interested parties to make offers. Paresh's response is the actual offer, which Ramesh is free to accept or reject.

    (2) G sends his servant L in search of his missing nephew. Thereafter, he announces a reward of Rs. 500 for information concerning his nephew. Without the knowledge about the reward L traces the nephew. Can L claim the reward?
    > **Answer:** No, L cannot claim the reward. For acceptance to be valid, the offeree must be aware of the offer. Since L traced the nephew without knowledge of the reward, there was no acceptance of the offer by him, and thus no contract for the reward was formed.

16. State the rules relating to offer, acceptance and revocation when made by letters transmitted through post, bringing out the difference if any, between the rules under the Indian law and the rules under the English law.
    > **Answer:**
    > *   **Offer:** In both Indian and English law, an offer is complete when it comes to the knowledge of the offeree.
    > *   **Acceptance:**
    >     *   **Indian Law:** Communication of acceptance is complete *as against the proposer* when the letter is posted, and complete *as against the acceptor* when the letter comes to the knowledge of the proposer.
    >     *   **English Law:** Acceptance is complete as soon as the letter is posted (the "postal rule").
    > *   **Revocation of Offer:** In both laws, an offer can be revoked anytime before its acceptance is complete against the proposer.
    > *   **Revocation of Acceptance:**
    >     *   **Indian Law:** Acceptance can be revoked if the revocation reaches the offerer before or at the same time as the acceptance letter.
    >     *   **English Law:** Acceptance cannot be revoked once it is posted; it is final.

17. How and when may an offer and acceptance through post be revoked? Does the Indian law differ from the English law on the subject?
    > **Answer:**
    > *   **Revocation of Offer:** An offer sent by post can be revoked by a notice of revocation that reaches the offeree before the offeree's letter of acceptance is posted. This rule is generally consistent between Indian and English law.
    > *   **Revocation of Acceptance:**
    >     *   **Indian Law:** An acceptance sent by post *can* be revoked. The revocation is complete against the acceptor if the message of revocation (e.g., by telegram or faster means) reaches the offerer *before* or *at the same time* as the original letter of acceptance.
    >     *   **English Law:** An acceptance sent by post *cannot* be revoked once it has been posted. The postal rule dictates that acceptance is complete and binding on posting, regardless of whether the letter reaches or if a faster revocation is sent. Indian law thus differs significantly by allowing revocation of acceptance if it reaches earlier.

18. Discuss: The rules for valid acceptance.
    > **Answer:** The rules for valid acceptance are: it must be absolute and unqualified, agreeing to all terms of the offer; it must be expressed in a usual or prescribed manner; it must be made by the specific party to whom the offer was made (unless it's a general offer); it must be communicated to the offerer (though this can be by conduct or implied in some cases); it must be made within a reasonable timeframe; and the acceptor must be aware of the offer when accepting it.

19. True or False:
    (i) Communication is essential in offer but not in acceptance.
        > **Answer:** False. Communication is essential for both offer and acceptance to be legally effective. An offer is complete when communicated to the offeree, and acceptance is complete when communicated to the offerer (or when put in transmission, depending on the party).
    (ii) The proposal comes to an end, in case of death or insanity of proposer prior to acceptance of proposal.
        > **Answer:** True, provided the fact of death or insanity comes to the knowledge of the acceptor before acceptance. If the acceptor is unaware, the acceptance may still be valid in some cases under Indian law.

22. Whether following statements are true or false. Explain with reasons.
    (1) The proposal comes to an end in case of death or insanity of proposer prior to an acceptance of proposal.
        > **Answer:** True. The proposal terminates if the proposer dies or becomes insane, provided the offeree becomes aware of this fact before they accept the proposal.
    (2) It is not necessary that acceptance of an offer should be absolute and conditional.
        > **Answer:** False. It is necessary that acceptance of an offer should be *absolute and unqualified*. A conditional acceptance is not a valid acceptance; it is a counter-offer.
    (3) An offer may be conditional.
        > **Answer:** True. A proposer is at liberty to make an offer subject to certain conditions, provided these conditions are properly communicated to the offeree.
    (4) Communication of an offer is compulsory.
        > **Answer:** True. An offer is only complete and effective when it comes to the knowledge of the person to whom it is made.
    (5) Acceptance of an offer must be communicated.
        > **Answer:** True. A mere mental acceptance is not sufficient; it must be communicated to the offerer through words or conduct, unless the offerer has waived this requirement or performance itself constitutes acceptance.
    (6) Acceptance of an offer should be absolute and unconditional.
        > **Answer:** True. For a valid contract, acceptance must mirror the offer exactly, without any changes or new conditions.
    (7) An offer may be general or specific.
        > **Answer:** True. An offer can be made to a specific person or group (specific offer) or to the public at large (general offer).
    (8) Acceptance should be absolute unqualified.
        > **Answer:** True. This reiterates the core principle that acceptance must be a mirror image of the offer.
    (9) Only mental acceptance is not a legal acceptance.
        > **Answer:** True. Legal acceptance requires outward manifestation and communication, not just internal assent.
    (10) Execution of conditions is an acceptance of proposal.
        > **Answer:** True. As per Section 8, performing the conditions of a proposal can constitute acceptance, especially in cases of general offers where performance is invited.
    (11) Define offer and explain how an offer can get lapsed.
