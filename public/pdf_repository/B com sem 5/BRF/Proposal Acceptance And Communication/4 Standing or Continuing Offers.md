4_Standing or Continuing Offers

This section explains the concept of a standing offer, which involves a continuous supply of goods or services over a period, and discusses its legal implications and revocation.

# Standing Offers

Sometimes a proposal takes the form of a *continuing offer* or *standing offer*. This occurs when goods or services are required over a specific period, and a trader invites tenders for a continuous supply at a fixed rate.

**Example:** An offer for the continuous supply of a certain article at a certain rate over a definite period is called a 'standing offer'.

**Case Example:** *Percival Ltd. v. L.C.C. (1918) 87 L.J.K.B. 677*
P signed a tender agreeing to supply all specified goods to the London County Council to the extent ordered, upon acceptance. Although the Council accepted the tender, they did not order any goods. It was held that the Council was not bound to order any goods, but if they did, P was bound to deliver them. Thus, if the inviting party places no order or not the full quantity, there is no breach of contract.

The acceptance of a standing offer means that as goods are required, an order is placed, and each order creates a distinct contract. (Benegal Coal Co. v. Wadia (1900) 24 Bom. 97.)

## Revocation of a Standing Offer

A standing offer, like any ordinary offer, can be *revoked* at any time before its acceptance. The question arises whether a person making a tender can revoke it before the expiry of the tender period.

**Case Example:** *Offord v. Davies (1862) 142 E.R. 1336*
The defendants guaranteed payments for bills of exchange discounted by the plaintiff for M/s. Davies & Co. up to £500 over 12 months. Before the period ended and before any discount was made, the defendants withdrew the guarantee. The plaintiff ignored the withdrawal and continued discounting bills. The court held that the defendants had the right to revoke their promise before the plaintiffs acted upon it, and this right was not affected by the promise being for 12 months or by prior discounts.

**Summary of Position:** A tender for supplying goods as required during a specific period is a *Standing (or continuing) offer*. Its "acceptance" does not automatically create a contract. A binding contract only forms when a specific order is placed in terms of the tender. Until then, both parties are free to withdraw and are free from legal obligations regarding the tender.

### Unanswered Questions

*   **Q:** Short Note: Standing (open) offer.
*   **Q:** Explain the meaning of standing offer.

> **Answer:** A standing offer, also known as a continuing offer, is an offer for the continuous supply of a certain article or service at a specified rate over a definite period. Its acceptance by the offeree does not immediately form a binding contract for the entire period, but rather, each subsequent order placed under the standing offer creates a separate and distinct contract.
