3_Coercion in Contract Law

This section defines coercion, outlines its essential elements, provides illustrative examples, and discusses its features and consequences, including a comparison with the English concept of duress.

# Coercion in Contract Law

In common understanding, coercion occurs when one party is forced to agree to a contract, meaning their consent is not free.

## Definition

"Coercion is -
1.  The committing or threatening to commit,
2.  Any act forbidden by the Indian Penal Code, or
3.  Unlawfully detaining, or
4.  Threatening to detain any property,
5.  To the prejudice of any person whatever,
6.  With the intention of causing any person to enter into an agreement (Sec. 15)."

**Explanation:** It is immaterial whether the Indian Penal Code is or is not in force in the place where the coercion is employed.

## Ingredients

According to Section 15, any of the following acts constitute coercion:

### By "Committing" of any act forbidden by Indian Penal Code:
**Example:** A 13-year-old girl was forced to adopt a boy by her husband's relatives who prevented the removal of her husband's dead body until she consented. This amounted to coercion because obstructing a dead body is an offense under S. 297 of the Penal Code. The agreement was not free, and the adoption was set aside. (Rangnayakamma V/s. Alwar Chetty case (1889) 13 Mad 214).

### 'By threatening to commit' any act forbidden by Indian Penal Code:
**Example:** A threatens B with dire consequences if B does not drop a criminal proceeding against A. B agrees. The agreement is brought about by coercion.
*Charging high prices or high interest rates, or threatening to prosecute for such acts, will generally not amount to coercion as they are not forbidden by the Indian Penal Code.*

### By the unlawful detaining of property:
**Example:** An agent refuses to hand over the principal's book of accounts unless the principal agrees to release him from all past liabilities. The principal gives the release. The release was obtained by coercion and was not binding. (Muthia v. Muthu-Kurrupan 1927 So Mad. 786).

### By threatening to detain any property:
**Example:** The Government threatens to attach P's property to recover a fine due from T, P's son. P pays the fine. This payment was induced by coercion. (Bansraj v. The Secretary of State 1939 AWR 247).

### Immateriality of Indian Penal Code's Force:
It is immaterial whether the Indian Penal Code is in force in the place where coercion is employed.
**Example:** A, on an English ship on the high seas, coerces B to enter an agreement by an act amounting to criminal intimidation under the Indian Penal Code. A later sues for breach of contract in Calcutta. A employed coercion, even though the act was not an offense by English law and the Indian Penal Code was not in force where the act was committed.

## Features

Section 15 highlights the following points:
*   **Source:** Coercion is not necessarily from a party to the contract; it can proceed from a third party.
*   **Target:** It can be employed against any person, including a stranger.
*   **Intention:** All acts of coercion must be intended to cause the other party to enter into an agreement.
*   **Scope:** It includes fear, physical compulsion, and menace to goods.
*   **Timing:** The threat can be present or past.

## Special Points to be Noted

### Threat to Commit Suicide
The Madras High Court, in *Amiraja v. Seshawaran* (JWII 4) (that 27), considered whether a threat to commit suicide amounts to coercion.
*   A person threatened to commit suicide if his wife and son refused to execute a lease-deed in his favor. The wife and son executed the deed under this threat.
*   The Court, by majority, held that the threat to commit suicide amounted to coercion. Although suicide itself is not punishable by the Indian Penal Code, an *attempt* to commit suicide is. Thus, the consent obtained by such a threat was by coercion.
*   A minority view argued that only the *attempt* is punishable, not the completed act, so the threat shouldn't be coercion.

### Prosecution
A threat to prosecute a person or file a suit against them may *not* amount to coercion (E & L Insurance Co. v. Binay (1945) Cal. 214), because these acts are not forbidden by the Indian Penal Code.

## English Law: Duress

The English common law doctrine of 'Duress' involves actual or threatened violence over a person (or their spouse, parent, or child) to obtain their consent to an agreement. If the threat is regarding any other party, it is not 'duress'.

## Consequences of Coercion

According to Section 19, when consent to an agreement is caused by coercion, the agreement is a **contract voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused.** This means the aggrieved party can:
*   Have the contract set aside, or
*   Refuse to perform it, and take the defense of coercion if the other party seeks to enforce the contract, or
*   If desired, abide by the contract and insist on its performance by the other party.
