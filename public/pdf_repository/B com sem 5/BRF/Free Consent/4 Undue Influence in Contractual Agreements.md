4_Undue Influence in Contractual Agreements

This section delves into undue influence, a vitiating factor where one party exploits a dominant position over another to secure an unfair advantage in a contract.

# Undue Influence in Contractual Agreements

In certain situations, the relationship between parties to an agreement allows one party to dominate the will of the other. This can lead to the weaker party being compelled to enter into an agreement against their true will due to unfair influence. Such an agreement is said to be induced by *undue influence*.

## Definition

Section 16 (1) defines undue influence as:
"A contract is said to be induced by 'undue influence' where the relations subsisting between the parties are such that one of the parties is in a position to obtain an unfair advantage over the other."

**The Test:** The court asks: "Would any reasonable man in full possession of his senses and not under undue influence of some kind or the other do such a thing?"

## Ingredients

To establish undue influence, the following ingredients are necessary:
*   The relations between the parties must be such that one party is in a position to *dominate the will* of the other.
*   The dominant party *obtains an unfair advantage* over the other.
*   The dominant party *uses his dominant position* to obtain this unfair advantage.

The existence of a "dominant character" relationship is essential. The question of whether a party was in a position to dominate the will of another is largely a question of fact.

**Example:** In *Wajid Khan v. Ewaz Ali* ((1891) 18 Cal. 545), the Privy Council set aside a gift deed made by an old illiterate Mohamedan lady in favor of her confidential managing agent due to undue influence.

## Presumption of Undue Influence

Two things must be proven to raise a presumption of undue influence:
1.  The relationship between the contracting parties was such that one was in a position to dominate the will of the other.
2.  That position was used to obtain an unfair advantage.

**Example:** A person confined to a nursing home due to multiple ailments gifted all his properties to one son, excluding others. The Supreme Court held that the presumption of undue influence was proper. (Lakshmi Amma V.T. Narayan A.I.R. 1970 SC'1367).

## Relationship of Trust

Section 16 (2) states that a person is deemed to be in a position to dominate the will of another:
*   Where he holds a **real or apparent authority** over the other (e.g., master and servant).
*   Where he stands in a **fiduciary relation** to the other (e.g., father and son, solicitor and client, trustee and beneficiary, spiritual adviser and devotee).
*   Where he makes a contract with a person whose **mental capacity is temporarily or permanently affected** by reason of age, illness, or mental or bodily distress (e.g., doctor and patient).

**Illustrations from the section:**
*   A, having advanced money to his son B during his minority, obtains a bond from B for a greater amount than due by misusing parental influence upon B's coming of age. A employs undue influence.
*   A, enfeebled by disease or age, is induced by B's influence as his medical attendant to agree to pay B an unreasonable sum for professional services. B employs undue influence.
*   A applies to a Banker for a loan during a money market stringency. The Banker declines the loan except at an unusually higher interest rate. A accepts. This is an ordinary business transaction, and the contract is not induced by undue influence.

## Burden of Proof

Section 16 (3) states: "Where a person who is in a position to dominate the will of another, enters into a contract with him, and the transaction appears, on the face of it or on the evidence adduced, to be unconscionable, the burden of proving that contract was not induced by undue influence shall lie upon the person in a position to dominate the will of the other."

**Example:** A, indebted to B, a village money lender, contracts a fresh loan on unconscionable terms. The burden is on B to prove the contract was not induced by undue influence.

The condition that the transaction appears *unconscionable* is essential for shifting the burden of proof. Otherwise, the actual use of the dominant position must be proven as a matter of fact.

## Important Observations

### Relationships Raising Presumption of Undue Influence
The following relationships often raise a presumption of undue influence (though this list is not exhaustive):
*   Parent and child
*   Guardian and ward
*   Trustee and beneficiary
*   Doctor and patient
*   Solicitor and client
*   Religious adviser and disciple

There is generally *no presumption* of undue influence in the following cases:
*   Landlord and tenant
*   Creditor and debtor
*   Husband and wife

### Unconscionable Bargain
An unconscionable bargain is one that no reasonable person would make and no honest person would accept. Courts consider four questions:
*   Whether the transaction is righteous.
*   Whether it was improvident (lacking foresight).
*   Whether it required a legal adviser.
*   Whether the intention to make the gift originated with the donor.

**Example:** A sale deed was vitiated when the plaintiff's mother, an old, blind, illiterate tribal woman, was totally dependent on the defendant. The Supreme Court found the defendant was in a position to dominate her will and obtain an unfair advantage. (Sethani v Bhana AIR 1993 SC 956).

### High Rates of Interest
If an exorbitant interest rate makes a transaction unconscionable, the burden of proving no undue influence lies on the creditor.

### Rebutting the Presumption
The presumption of undue influence can be rebutted by showing that:
*   The injured person had *independent advice*. This advice must be competent and based on knowledge of all relevant facts.
*   All material and full disclosure of facts were made by the influencing party.

## Pardanashin Woman

*Pardanashin women* are those who observe *Purdah* according to their religion and are secluded from ordinary social interaction. Contracts with them are presumed to be induced by undue influence, and the law provides them special protection.

To prove a contract with a Pardanashin woman is valid, the other party must strictly prove that:
*   No undue influence was used.
*   She had free and independent advice.
*   She understood the contents of the contract.
*   She exercised her free will.

**Example:** A Hindu widow (Pardanashin) gifted half her properties to her paramour's son, who was also her manager, two months before her death. She had no independent advice. Held, the gift was a result of the manager's influence. (Ismail Mussajee v. Ilafiz Boo 190633 Cal. 773).

## Effect of Undue Influence

*   When consent is caused by undue influence, the agreement is a **contract voidable at the option of the party whose consent was so caused.**
*   Such a contract may be set aside absolutely, or, if the aggrieved party has received a benefit, it may be set aside upon such terms and conditions as the Court deems just. (Section 19 (a)).

**Example:** A, a money lender, advances Rs. 100 to B, an agriculturist, and by undue influence, induces B to execute a bond for Rs. 200 with interest at 6 percent per month. The court may set aside the bond, ordering B to pay Rs. 100 with such interest as may seem just.
