6_Nature of Surety's Liability

This section clarifies the extent and nature of a surety's liability, emphasizing its co-extensive nature with the principal debtor's liability and detailing when and how the surety can be held accountable.

# Nature of Surety's Liability

## Co-extensive Liability

"The liability of the surety is co-extensive with that of the principal debtor unless it is otherwise provided by the contract." (Section 128)

This means the surety is liable for what the principal debtor is liable. The word "co-extensive" clearly indicates that a surety is liable to the same extent as the principal debtor. Moreover, the surety is made jointly and severally liable with the principal debtor. (Madho Shah Vs. Sitaram (1962) A. Pat. 405.)

*   If the principal debtor is not liable on the principal debt, the surety is also not liable.
*   If the principal debt is illegal, the principal debtor and consequently the surety are also not liable. (A.V. Varadarajulul Naidu Vs. K.V. Thavasi Nadar (1963) A. Mad. 413.)
*   Similarly, if the principal debtor is discharged by the creditor's breach, the surety will not be liable. (Unity Finance Ltd. Vs. Woodcock (1963) I W.L.R. 455.)

The liability of the surety can neither be more nor less than that of the principal debtor, though by a special agreement, it may be made less than that of the principal debtor, but never greater. The liability of the surety immediately arises on the default by the principal debtor or on some breach of duty by the party causing damage to the holder of the guarantee.

The creditor is not bound to exhaust all his remedies against the principal debtor before suing the surety, and a suit may be maintained against the surety, even if the principal debtor has not been sued. (Asharafbibi Vs. Prashadilal (1959) A. Madh. p. 26. State Bank of India, Vs. Saksaria Sugar Mills LTD. A.I.R. 1956 SC 868) Thus, a surety can be sued without even suing the principal debtor or making him a co-defendant. When the principal debtor is a minor, the surety alone is liable to the creditor.

**Example:** A Bank issued a letter of guarantee to the Electricity Board on behalf of a company to the extent of Rs. 50,000 to be paid within 48 hours of the Board's demand. The company in the meantime goes into liquidation. Subsequently, the Board demands payment from the Bank. Held, that the bank was bound to make payments to the extent of Rs. 50,000. Thereafter, it is open to the Bank to have recourse to the securities given by the company in liquidation for the purpose of issuing the letter of guarantee. The Electricity Board is not concerned with what the Bank does in order to reimburse itself after paying the amount of guarantee. (Maharastra State Electricity Board Vs. Official Liquidation A.I.R. 1982 SC 1947.)

In another case (Union Bank of India Vs. Manku Narayan A.I.R. 1987 SC 1078), a loan was taken from a Bank against a mortgage of property supported by an independent guarantee. It was held that the Bank must proceed first against the mortgaged property and then against the guarantor, even if the decree is a composite one against the principal debtor, mortgaged property, and the guarantor.

## Liability of Two Persons Primarily Liable

"Where two persons contract with a third person to undertake a certain liability, and also contract with each other that one of them shall be liable only on the default of the other, the third person not being a party to such contract, the liability of each of such two persons to the third person under the first contract, is not affected by the existence of the second contract, although such third person may have been aware of its existence." (Section 132)

**Example:** A and B make a joint and several promissory note to C. A makes it in fact as surety for B, and C knows that at the time when the note is made. The fact that A, to the knowledge of C, made the note as surety for B, is no answer to a suit by C against A upon the note.
