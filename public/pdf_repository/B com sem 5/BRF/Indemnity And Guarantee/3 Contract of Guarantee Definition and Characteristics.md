3_Contract of Guarantee: Definition and Characteristics

This section defines a contract of guarantee, identifies the three parties involved, and elaborates on its key characteristics, including the nature of the surety's liability and the consideration required.

# Contract of Guarantee: Definition and Characteristics

## Definition

"A 'contract of guarantee' is a contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability of a third person in case of his default. The person who gives the guarantee is called the '**surety**', the person in respect of whose default the guarantee is given is called the '**principal debtor**', and the person to whom the guarantee is given is called the '**creditor**'. A guarantee may be either oral or written." (Section 126)

**Example:** A lends Rs. 1,000 to B and C promises A that if B does not pay the money, C will do so. This is a contract of guarantee. A is called the 'creditor', B, the 'principal debtor' and C the 'surety'.

## Characteristics

1.  **Tri-partite Contract:** A 'contract of guarantee' is a tri-partite contract, meaning there is a triangular relationship involving three collateral contracts:
    *   Between the principal debtor and the creditor.
    *   Between the surety and the principal debtor (implied promise of indemnity).
    *   Between the surety and the creditor.
2.  **Secondary or Collateral Liability:** The liability of the surety is secondary or collateral, which comes into existence only upon default by the principal debtor. (M/s. Brahmayya and Co. Official Liquidator. v. K. Srinivasan Thangirayor and Ors. A.I.R. 1952 Mad. 122.)
3.  **Requirements of a Valid Contract:** Since "contract of guarantee" is a species of contract, it must satisfy all the requirements of a valid contract (e.g., free consent, lawful object, lawful consideration, capacity of parties).
    *   **Exception regarding incompetent parties:** As to the rights of parties where a guarantee is in respect of a liability of a person incompetent to contract (e.g., a minor), it has been held that the liability of the person who gives the guarantee is not secondary, but direct and primary. Consequently, the guarantor will be liable though not the principal debtor. (Sohanlal v. Puran Singh (1916) 35 I.C. 137.)
    *   **Consideration for Guarantee:** In a contract of guarantee, the consideration received by the principal debtor is taken to be sufficient consideration for the surety. Section 127 supplies the test for consideration for guarantee thus: "Anything done, or any promise made, for the benefit of the principal debtor may be a sufficient consideration to the surety for giving the guarantee."
        **Example:** B requests A to sell and deliver to him goods on credit. A agrees to do so provided C will guarantee the payment of the price of the goods. C promises to guarantee the payment in consideration of A's promise to deliver the goods. This is a sufficient consideration for C's promise.
4.  **Not *Uberrimae Fidei*:** The contract of guarantee is not a contract of *uberrimae fidei* (of utmost good faith). Thus, there is no general obligation on the part of the creditor to disclose all material facts within his knowledge to the surety.
    **Example:** S guaranteed the account of P to the Bank. P afterwards drew from this account with a view to pay off his overdraft with yet another Bank, unknown to S. Held, that simply because the Bank was suspicious of the fact that P was trying to defraud, the bank was in no way under any obligation to communicate this suspicion to S, nor did it discharge the guarantee. (National provincial Bank of England v. Glanusk (1913) 3 K. B. 335.)
    **Exception:** If the guarantee is in the nature of a fidelity guarantee (e.g., for an employee's honesty), all material facts must necessarily be disclosed. Otherwise, the surety can avoid the contract.

> **Answer to Q: Define contract of Indemnity. State the rights of an Indemnity-holder when sued.**
> **Definition of Contract of Indemnity:** A contract of indemnity is a contract by which one party promises to save the other from loss caused to him by the conduct of the promisor himself, or by the conduct of any other person (Section 124). The indemnifier promises to make good the loss, and the indemnified (or indemnity-holder) is the person whose loss is to be made good.
> **Rights of an Indemnity-holder when sued:** As per Section 125, an indemnity-holder acting within the scope of their authority is entitled to recover from the indemnifier:
> 1.  All damages they are compelled to pay in any suit related to the indemnity.
> 2.  All costs they are compelled to pay in such a suit, provided they acted prudently and did not contravene the indemnifier's orders, or if the indemnifier authorized the suit.
> 3.  All sums paid under the terms of any compromise of such a suit, provided the compromise was prudent and not contrary to the indemnifier's orders, or if the indemnifier authorized the compromise.

> **Answer to Q: Define Contract of surety.**
> A contract of surety (or guarantee) is a contract to perform the promise, or discharge the liability of a third person in case of his default. The person who gives the guarantee is called the 'surety', the person in respect of whose default the guarantee is given is called the "principal debtor", and the person to whom the guarantee is given is called the "creditor" (Section 126).

## Purpose of Guarantee

A guarantee may be given for:
*   The payment of a debt. (See Centax (India) Ltd v Vinmar Impex ac. A.I.R. 1986 SC 1924 Bank's liability under letter of guarantee held absolute)
*   The repayment of the price of goods sold on credit.
*   The good conduct and honesty of a person employed in a particular office. In the last case, the guarantee is also called a 'fidelity guarantee'.
