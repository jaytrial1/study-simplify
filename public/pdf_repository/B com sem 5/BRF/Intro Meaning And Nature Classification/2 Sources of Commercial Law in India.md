2_Sources of Commercial Law in India

This section explores the various origins and influences shaping mercantile law in India, including statutes, English law, judicial decisions, and customs.

# Sources of Commercial Law

Mercantile Law in India primarily draws from:
*   Statutes enacted by the Indian Legislature
*   English Mercantile Law
*   Established mercantile customs and usages in India, as modified and adopted by judicial decisions

## Laws in India

The majority of Indian Mercantile Law is statutory. Key examples include:
*   Indian Contract Act, 1872
*   Partnership Act, 1932
*   Sale of Goods Act, 1930
*   Companies Act, 1956
*   Negotiable Instruments Act, 1887

It is important to note:
*   Most Indian statutes are largely based on corresponding English statutes.
*   Before these statutes were enacted, Indian Courts applied the personal law of the parties involved in a suit.

## English Mercantile Law

Many rules found in English Mercantile Law are either directly incorporated into Indian Laws through statutes or are included via judicial decisions. English Mercantile Law itself is fundamentally based on the Common Law of England, which has been modified and supplemented by Equity and Statute Law.

The principal sources of English Mercantile Law are:

### Common Law
This is a system of law derived from English customs, usages, and traditions developed over centuries by English Courts.

### Equity
This system of law emerged out of necessity when Common Law proved inadequate to achieve justice. It developed separately from Common Law. Where Common Law rules were deemed harsh or oppressive, Rules of Equity were applied to cover deficiencies. Historically, conflicts arose between Common Law and Equity. However, the Judicature Acts of 1873 and 1875 abolished the distinction between Common Law and Equity Law, integrating them.

### Roman Law
Recourse to Roman Law was often made when Common Law or Rules of Equity failed to provide a solution.

### Law Merchant
The Law Merchant was the precursor to English Mercantile Law. Maritime usages evolved during the 14th and 15th centuries among merchants trading primarily in European ports.

### Statute Law
Laws established by the British Parliament are significant sources of English Mercantile Law. They hold superiority and override any rule of Common Law or Equity.

### Case Law
Judges, while deciding cases, interpret and explain statutes. Important past judicial decisions acquire the force of *precedent* and are generally followed by courts in similar future cases.

## Judicial Decisions

Before 1947, the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council of Great Britain served as the final Court of Appeal for suits arising in India, and its decisions were binding on all Indian Courts. After 1947, the Supreme Court of India assumed this role. This practice is based on the sound legal principle that what has been decided in an earlier case should be binding in all similar future cases, frequently referred to as the "**Law of Precedent**."

## Customs and Usages

Customs and Usages have played a vital role in regulating commercial dealings and transactions. A custom develops from continuously accepted practice. For a custom to be binding on parties, it must be:
*   Ancient
*   Reasonable
*   Certain
*   Definite
*   Consistent with other customs
*   Uniformly recognized in the ordinary course of business (C.I. & B. Syndicate V. Ramchandra, A.I.R. (1968) Mys. 133)

In a case before the Bombay High Court (Jamnadas V. Chetandas, 30 Bom. L.R. 1317), the Court established a test for proving a usage in a particular trade. It must be shown that the usage is:
*   Certain
*   Reasonable
*   Not inconsistent with law
*   So universally acquiesced in that everybody engaged in the trade knows it or could ascertain it with inquiry.

**Q:** When is a custom binding? What is the test?
> **Answer:** A custom is binding when it is ancient, reasonable, certain, definite, consistent with other customs, and uniformly recognized in the ordinary course of business. The test for a binding usage in a particular trade requires it to be certain, reasonable, not inconsistent with law, and so universally accepted that anyone in the trade would know it or could easily discover it.
