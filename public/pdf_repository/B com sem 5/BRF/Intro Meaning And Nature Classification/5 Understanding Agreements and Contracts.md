5_Understanding Agreements and Contracts

This section establishes the fundamental definitions of agreement and contract, emphasizing the crucial element of legal enforceability.

# Introduction to Contractual Concepts

Contract and its performance form the bedrock of modern commercial activities. The enforceability of promises is paramount for trade and commerce, as the rights and obligations created by a contract must be protected and upheld.

The Law of Contract deals specifically with agreements that can be enforced through Courts of Law. Section 2(h) of the Indian Contract Act defines a contract as: "**An agreement enforceable by law is a contract**". This means an agreement is considered a contract only when it possesses legal enforceability. Anson suggests that only obligations directly contemplating and creating legal obligations are contracts. Agreements that do not give rise to legal obligations are not enforceable by law.

Lord Atkin noted, "The most usual forms of agreement, which do not constitute, a contract, are the agreements between husband and wife. They are not contracts because the parties do not intend that they should be attended by legal consequences" (*Balfour Vs. Balfour* (1919) 2 K.B. 571).

## Agreement Versus Contract

A contract must satisfy two conditions:
1.  There must be an **agreement**.
2.  Such an agreement must be **enforceable by law**, creating legal obligations.

The term "**Agreement**" is defined in Section 2(e) as: "Every promise and every set of promises forming the consideration for each other." An agreement is essentially an accepted proposal. Therefore:
**Proposal + Acceptance = Agreement**

However, not every agreement is a contract. For instance, if X invites Y to a dinner party or cinema, and Y accepts, it is merely a social engagement or agreement. Such social agreements do not create contractual obligations and are not enforceable in a Court of Law. If X fails to serve dinner, Y has no legal remedy.

Thus, only those agreements that are enforceable at law are considered contracts. If X offers to sell his house to Y for Rs. 1,00,000/-, and Y accepts, it becomes a concluded contract, creating legal obligations enforceable at law between them. In other words:
**Agreement + Enforceability at Law = Contract**

Therefore, for a contract to be valid, there must be:
1.  An agreement.
2.  The agreement must be enforceable at law.

Lord Mangham also observed in *Foley V. Classique Ltd.* (1934) that: "Unless all material terms of the contract are agreed, there is no binding obligation. An agreement to agree in future is not a contract; nor is there a contract; if a material term is neither settled nor implied by law and the documents contain no machinery for accepting it." The court must examine the substance of the agreement, not merely its form (C.I.T.V. Punjab Panipal W & C Mills).

**Q:** Short note: Agreement and Contract.
> **Answer:** An **agreement** is defined as "every promise and every set of promises forming the consideration for each other," which means a proposal followed by acceptance. A **contract**, however, is "an agreement enforceable by law." This crucial distinction means that while all contracts are agreements, not all agreements are contracts. Social or domestic agreements, for example, are not contracts because they typically lack the intention to create legal obligations and are therefore not enforceable in a court of law. Only agreements that create legal obligations that can be upheld by the legal system qualify as contracts.
