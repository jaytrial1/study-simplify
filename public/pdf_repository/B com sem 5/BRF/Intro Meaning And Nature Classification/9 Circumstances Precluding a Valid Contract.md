9_Circumstances Precluding a Valid Contract

This section identifies specific scenarios where an agreement will not be considered a legally binding contract due to lack of definiteness, vagueness, or mere promotional intent.

# When an Agreement is Not a Contract

As previously stated, parties must contemplate a legal relationship for a contract to arise. In the following cases, an agreement does not constitute a contract:

## "Agreeing to Agree"

If parties have not settled on the terms of the contract but have only agreed to agree in the future, no contract is formed.
*Example:* An actress was engaged for a provincial tour. The agreement also stipulated that if the play moved to London, she would be engaged at a salary "to be mutually agreed upon." The court held there was no contract because a material term (salary) was left for future agreement (*Loftus Vs. Robens* (1902) 18, T.L.R. 532).

## Vague Terms

An agreement may be too vague to be effective, indicating that the parties did not intend to be legally bound.
*Example:* A company agreed with V that upon the expiration of V's existing contract, it would "favourably consider" an application by V for a renewal. The court held that this agreement was not intended to bind the company to renew the contract with V and imposed no obligation to do so, due to the vagueness of "favourably consider" (*Montreal Gas Co. Vs. Vasey* (1900) A.C. 595).

## Mere "Puff" or Stunt

If an offer is made without an "intention to create legal relationship" and is merely a "puff" (exaggerated claim) or a "stunt" for advertising, it does not create an enforceable contract. However, if the intent is clear, it can.
*Example:* The defendants advertised they would pay £100 to anyone who caught influenza after using their smoke-ball thrice daily for two weeks. They deposited £100 in a bank to demonstrate their sincerity. Mrs. Carlill, relying on the advertisement, used the smoke-ball as prescribed but still caught influenza. She sued for the reward. The company was held liable. The court pointed out that depositing £100 in a bank clearly showed a serious intention, and therefore, it was not a mere advertising puff, making it a binding offer (*Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.* (1893) Q.B. 256).

**Q:** "The intention to create legal relations is an element in binding contract". - Explain.
> **Answer:** The intention to create legal relations is a crucial element because it distinguishes legally binding contracts from social or domestic agreements. For an agreement to be a contract, the parties must mutually intend for their agreement to have legal consequences, creating enforceable rights and obligations. Without this intention, even if an offer and acceptance occur, the agreement remains outside the realm of contract law, meaning it cannot be enforced in a court of law. Courts assess this intention objectively, based on the circumstances and the nature of the agreement.

**Summary of Contract Formation:**
*   **Proposal + Acceptance = Agreement**
*   **Agreement enforceable at law = Contract**
*   **Agreement + Legal obligation = Contract**
