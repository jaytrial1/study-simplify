3_The Indian Contract Act: Historical Context and Broad Application

This section details the historical development of the Indian Contract Act and discusses its extensive nature and purpose in regulating agreements.

# Genesis of the Indian Contract Act

The **Indian Contract Act**, passed on April 25, 1872, came into force on September 10, 1872. Its objective was to define and amend specific parts of the laws related to contracts, laying down general principles. It applies to the whole of India, except the State of Jammu and Kashmir.

Originally, the Act included provisions concerning the sale of goods and partnership. However:
*   In 1930, provisions relating to the Sale of Goods (Sections 76 to 123) were removed and incorporated into a new Act, the "**Sale of Goods Act**."
*   Similarly, in 1932, provisions relating to Partnership (Sections 239 to 266) were repealed and a separate Act, "**The Indian Partnership Act, 1932**," was passed.

The Act, in its current form, contains the general principles of contract (Sections 1 to 75) and specific contracts such as Indemnity, Suretyship, Bailments, and Agency (Sections 124 to 238). The Contract Act is not superseded by Act 299 of the Constitution of India or S 72 of the Railways Act prescribing the form of contract (Union of India V.S.S.H. Syndicate AIR 1976 SC 879).

## Nature and Scope of the Act

The Law of Contract forms the most crucial branch of Mercantile Law, serving as the backbone of trade and commerce. Its relevance extends beyond the trading community, affecting every individual. People engage in numerous contracts daily, often unconsciously – from buying milk to riding a bus, watching a movie, purchasing goods, or lending money. These actions create legal rights and obligations.

The Law of Contract is arguably the oldest branch of business law and provides the foundation for other specialized areas of commercial law. Therefore, studying the general principles of Contract Law naturally precedes specialized fields. The Indian Contract Act of 1872 (Act IX of 1872) establishes fundamental rules for contracts. It is aptly termed the 'Child of commerce,' evolving with the growth of industry and trade. However, it is important to note that the Act is not exhaustive.

While the Contract Act, 1872, does not claim to be a complete code for contract law, it is exhaustive on subjects it specifically addresses. When the Act covers a particular matter, it is not permissible to introduce principles of English law outside of the statutory provisions, unless the statute itself cannot be understood without the aid of English law (Superintendence Co. of India (P) Ltd. V. Krishan Murgai (1981) 2 Sec 246).

The primary purpose of the Law of Contract is to ensure definiteness in mercantile transactions. Dr. William Anson aptly states that the Law of Contract is intended "to ensure that what a man has been led to expect shall come to pass and what has been promised to him shall be performed."

The Act does not explicitly state that an offer or acceptance must be made with the *intention of creating a legal relationship*. However, in England, it is a settled principle that "to create a contract there must be a common intention of the parties to enter into legal obligations" (Atkin J. in Rose & Frank Co. v J.R. Crompton & Bros (1923 2 K.B. 261)). What matters is not the parties' subjective thoughts but what a reasonable person would infer their intention to be under the circumstances.

*Example:* A mother (A), her daughter (B), and a paying guest (C) jointly participated in a crossword puzzle using the mother's name, with expenses shared informally. The mother won the prize but refused to divide it. The court held she was bound to share it, as a reasonable person observing their conduct would conclude they intended to share the prize (Simpkins V. Pays (1955) 3 All E.R. 10).

Thus, it is for the Court in each case to determine whether the parties intended to enter into legal obligations.
