5_Discharge by Subsequent Impossibility or Frustration

# Discharge by Subsequent Impossibility

This section examines the concept of contract discharge due to events that make performance impossible after the contract's formation, leading to the doctrine of frustration.

## Foundation

Impossibility of performance is one of the modes of discharge of contract. When a contract to perform a certain thing becomes impossible of fulfillment owing to some supervening or subsequent circumstances, the party liable is discharged from performance. Take a common instance of B promising to paint a portrait for A before or by a certain date. Suppose, before that date B dies or A is struck with paralysis, the contract to paint A's portrait is discharged because it was an underlying assumption of the contract that B should continue to be alive and to have good health till the time of performance. That is one kind of impossibility.

According to Anson: "Impossibility of performance may appear on the face of the contract, or may exist, unknown to the parties at the time of making the contract or may arise after the contract is made." Thus:

### 1. Impossibility existing at the time of formation of contract:

"An agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void." (Sec. 56 Para (1))
Such fact of impossibility may be:
*   **Known to the parties:** This is also known as total or absolute impossibility. A contract which, at the time it was entered into, was impossible to perform is void *ab initio* and creates no rights and obligations.
    **Example:** A agrees with B to discover treasure by magic. The agreement is void.
*   **Unknown to the parties:** When both the parties are ignorant of the impossibility at the time of making the contract, the contract is void on the ground of mutual mistake.
    **Example:** A agrees to sell his horse to B but unknown to both the parties, the horse had already died at the time of making the contract. The contract is void.

On the contrary, if the promisor alone knew about the impossibility of performance at the time of making the contract, he shall have to compensate the promisee for any loss which such promisee sustains through the non-performance of the promise (Para (3) of Sec. 56).
**Example:** A contracts to marry B, being already married to C and being forbidden by the law to which he is subject to practice polygamy. A must make compensation to B for the loss caused to her by non-performance of promise.

### 2. Impossibility arising subsequent to the formation of the contract:

A contract which is not void when the agreement was entered into but becomes void due to impossibility of performance arising subsequent to the formation of contract is called *subsequent* or *supervening impossibility*. That is the basis of the doctrine of frustration. The second para of Sec. 56 enunciates the law:

"A contract to do an act which after the contract is made, becomes impossible, or, by reason of some event, which the promisor could not prevent, unlawful, becomes void, when the act becomes impossible or unlawful."

The court must be satisfied that such supervening impossibility was neither patent nor latent at the time of making the contract but has arisen subsequent to the formation of the contract. Such change of circumstances should further make the performance of the contract "impossible". In other words, the object or the basis of the contract is frustrated. Moreover, such an impossibility is caused by circumstances beyond the control of the parties.

## Doctrine of Frustration

The following are the chief ways by which supervening or subsequent impossibility leads to contract discharge:

*   **Destruction of the subject-matter:** When the subject-matter of a contract, subsequent to its formation, is destroyed without the fault of the parties to the contract, especially when the contract depends on the continued existence of such thing and that thing is destroyed, the contract is discharged.
    **Example:** A music hall, which was let out for a series of concerts on certain days, was burnt down before the date of the first concert. Held, the contract was void. (Taylor v. Coldwell (1863))

*   **Non-existence of a set of things:** A contract is discharged if it depends upon the existence or occurrence of a particular set of things, which does not in fact happen. The rule is based on the principle that every agreement presumes the existence of a certain state of thing on the basis of which the agreement was entered into.
    **Example 1:** A and B contract to marry each other. Before the time fixed for the marriage, A goes mad. The contract becomes void.
    **Example 2:** A hired a flat from B for two days for witnessing a Coronation procession of King Edward VII, both parties being aware of the object even though no special mention was made in the contract. The Coronation procession, however, was cancelled on account of the illness of the King. Held, A was excused from paying the rent for the flat on the ground that existence of the procession was the basis of the contract. The cancellation discharged the contract. (Krell v. Henry (1903))
    **Example 3:** When the number of trees auctioned for extraction is reduced due to a subsequent reservation, there is a right to repudiate the contract and claim refund of earnest money. This is because when the very foundation of the contract is altered, the auction-purchaser is entitled to repudiate the contract and claim his earnest money in deposit. (Syed Israr Masood v. State of M.P. (1982))

*   **Death or personal incapacity of the promisor:** Where the performance of a contract depends upon the personal skill or qualification of a party.
    **Example:** For a certain consideration, an artist undertook to perform at a concert. Owing to serious illness, she could not do so. Held, she was discharged due to illness. (Robinson v. Davison (1871))

*   **Change of Law:** A contract is discharged where it becomes subsequently unlawful through a change in the legal position. When there is a change of law, subsequent to the formation of a contract, by which the performance of the contract becomes impossible, the contract is discharged.
    **Example:** A, who is already married, promises to marry C. Before A could marry C, the Special Marriage Act is passed prohibiting polygamous marriage. The contract to marry becomes void.

*   **Outbreak of War:** The general effect of a declaration of war is to prohibit trading with the enemy or even with a resident in enemy country. All rights under contracts are suspended till the war comes to an end. But, if the performance of a contract will help the enemy, such a contract becomes void on the declaration of the war.
    **Exception:** Normally contracts entered into in India during war are void *ab initio*. But with special permission and licence from the Central Government, there may be a valid contract with an alien enemy during the war.

## Exceptions to the Doctrine

"Impossibility of performance, is, as a rule, not an excuse from performance" observes Scrutton L.J. (Rallis Bros. v. Camapania Niviera (1920)). Only physical or legal impossibility will excuse the parties. If one of the modes of performance has become impossible or unlawful, and the contract can be performed in other ways, the contract is not frustrated. Its performance should become absolutely impossible due to any of the circumstances already discussed above.

The doctrine of supervening impossibility will not apply in the following instances:

### (i) Difficulty of performance:

A contract is not discharged by the mere fact that it has become difficult of performance owing to some unforeseen thing.

**Example:** A sold to B a certain quantity of Finland Timber to be delivered between July and September 1941. No deliveries were made before August when war broke out and the transport was disorganised, so that A could not bring any timber from Finland. Held, B was not concerned with the way in which A was going to get the timber and, therefore, the impossibility of getting timber from Finland did not excuse performance. (Blackburn Co. V Allen & Sons (1918))

### (ii) Commercial impossibility:

Commercial impossibility, i.e., extreme or unforeseen cost, is no excuse. Therefore, a party is not discharged merely because there is a sudden and unforeseen rise in prices of raw materials, or the expectation of higher profits is not realised, or a manufacturer has not manufactured the goods, or there is unexpected depreciation of currency. These are all the risks which the parties normally could have expected.

**Example:** A contracted to buy tapestry from B, and stated that he intended to resell it in Australia. Subsequently, however, imports to Australia were prohibited. A now repudiates the contract. B sued A for damages. Held, commercial impossibility is no ground for frustration of a contract. (Samuel Fitz & Co. V. Standard Cotton Co. (1946))

If the rate initially quoted by a contractor becomes irrelevant due to subsequent price-escalation, it was held that the contractor's claim for compensation for the excess expenditure incurred due to the price rise cannot be turned down on the ground of absence of a price escalation clause in that regard in the contract. The agreement as a whole must be seen. (Tarapore & Co. V. Cochin Shipyard Ltd. (1984))

### (iii) Impossibility due to a failure of a third person on whose work the promisor relied:

A contract, the performance of which depends on the behaviour of a third party, shall not be impossible of performance, simply because the third party has not acted in a particular manner agreed upon.
**Example:** A, a wholesale dealer, enters into a contract with B for the sale of certain goods to be produced by C, a manufacturer of those goods. C does not manufacture these goods. A is liable to B for damages.

### (iv) Strike, Lock-outs and Civil Disturbances:

Strikes, lock-outs, riots, and other civil disturbances do not by themselves terminate the contract or discharge the performance of contract unless the parties have specifically agreed in this regard at the time of formation of the contract.
**Example:** The lessee of certain salt pans failed to repair them due to a strike by the workmen employed there. The strike was not unforeseen, and there was no condition that in such a contingency the parties were to be excused from further performance. Held, that the lessee was liable for rents and costs of repairs to Salt pans. (Hari Laxman v. Secy. of State for India (1928))

### (v) Failure of one of the objects:

When a contract is entered into for more than one object, the failure of one does not discharge the entire contract.
**Example:** P agreed to let out a boat to D (a) for viewing a naval exercise on the occasion of the Coronation of Edward VII and (b) to sail round the fleet. Owing to the King's illness, the naval exercise was abandoned, but the fleet was assembled. The boat, therefore, could be used to sail round the fleet. Held, the contract was not discharged. (Hem Bay Steam Boat Co. v. Hutton (1903))

### (vi) Leases:

Leases are generally not subject to frustration.

## Effects of Supervening Impossibility

Following are the effects of supervening impossibility:

1.  The contract becomes void when the act, after the contract is made, becomes impossible or unlawful (Sec. 56, Para 2).
    **Example:** A and B contract to marry each other. Before the time fixed for the marriage, A goes mad. The contract becomes void.
    The contract will not become void *ab initio*, though its further performance will be excused. All future obligations will come to an end, and parties thereto shall be discharged from their performance.
2.  "Where one person has promised to do something, which he knew, or with reasonable diligence, might have known and which the promisee did not know to be impossible or unlawful such promisor must make compensation to such promisee for any loss which such promisee sustains through the non-performance of the promise." (Sec. 56 Para. 3)
    **Example:** A contracts to marry B, being already married to C and being forbidden by the law to which he is subject to practice polygamy. A must make compensation to B for the loss caused to her by the non-performance of his promise.
3.  When a contract becomes void, any person who has received any advantage under such agreement or contract is bound to restore it, or to make compensation for it, to the persons from whom he received it (Sec. 65).
    **Example:** A pays to B Rs. 1000 in consideration of B's promising to marry C, A's daughter. C is dead at the time of the promise. The agreement is void, but B must repay A Rs. 1000.
