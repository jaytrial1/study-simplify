# Discharge by Breach of Contract

This section details the discharge of a contract when one party fails to fulfill their obligations, discussing both actual and anticipatory breaches and their remedies.

When a contract is broken by one party, the other party or parties are freed from the obligations of performing the contract. The parties thereto are entitled to take the remedial measures as provided in the Act. A breach of contract occurs when a party to a contract does not fulfil his obligations, or by his own act makes it impossible that he should perform his obligations under it, or specifically repudiates his obligations. The breach of contract may arise in two ways:
1.  Actual breach of contract
2.  Anticipatory or constructive breach of contract

## English Law: Anticipatory Breach

It may sometimes happen that even before the time for performance arrives, the promisor may definitely renounce the contract and refuse to perform it when the time arrives, or he may by his own conduct disable himself from performing the contract at the appointed time. This repudiation may be express or implied—implied from conduct inconsistent with the continuance of the contract. Now, the question in such cases is whether the other party would be entitled to treat the contract as broken, or whether he could wait till the time for performance arrives and then bring an action for damages.

### Rule in Hochster v. De la Tour (1853):

This is a leading case on this point, where a person's services as a courier for a period of three months on a foreign tour were enlisted, and some time before the tour commenced, the employer wrote that the plaintiff's services as courier would not be required. The question arose whether an action for damages could be filed before the date mentioned in the agreement for starting on the tour. The Court observed that in cases of what is called anticipatory breach of contract, it is not necessary for the plaintiff to wait till the time for performance passes. It was held that keeping the contract alive would involve an obligation on the courier to keep himself equipped for the three months foreign travel, thus preventing him from taking up any other employment so as to mitigate damages payable by the other party. It was considered reasonable that, where there is an anticipatory breach of a contract by repudiation or renunciation, it should be open to the other party to accept the renunciation as an immediate breach of the contract and sue for damages. He can claim prospectively such damages, as would be caused by a breach at the appointed time, subject to any circumstances which may operate in mitigation of the damages.

### Rule in Frost v. Knight (1872):

This principle has been extended even to conditional promises. In this case, the promise was to marry the plaintiff after the death of the defendant's father. The defendant having repudiated the contract, even during the lifetime of the father, by marrying some other person, the question arose whether the plaintiff could immediately sue for damages. It was contended that the performance of the contract was due only after the death of the father and so it was even possible that there might be no occasion for performance at all, for instance, if either of the parties were to predecease the father and that the suit was therefore not sustainable. But the principle of Hochster v. De la Tour was applied, and it was held that the plaintiff could treat the repudiation as a breach and immediately sue for damages.

### Rule In Avery v. Bowden (1856):

When a person does not treat repudiation as a breach of the contract but keeps the contract alive, he does so for the benefit of the other party as well, so that any subsequent impossibility which would have the effect of discharging the contract could be availed of even by the party who has already repudiated it. In this particular case, the contract was to load a cargo at Odessa in Russia by a certain date. On the arrival of the ship, the defendant refused to load the cargo; but the master of the ship did not accept the repudiation as he could have done, but went on making demands for the cargo. Before the ship finally set on sail, war broke out between Great Britain and Russia, and performance became impossible (because of frustration). In a suit for breach of contract for refusal to load the cargo, the Court held that the repudiation not having been accepted, the contract was kept alive for the benefit of both the contracting parties and that the defendant could take advantage of the impossibility created by the declaration of the war.

## Indian Law: Actual and Anticipatory Breach

When a contract is broken by one party, the other party or parties are freed from the obligations of performing the contract. The parties thereto are entitled to take the remedial measures as provided in the Act.

### 1. Actual Breach of Contract:

An actual breach occurs:
*   **At the time when the Performance is due:**
    **Example:** A agrees to supply B 50 bags of rice on 15/1/98. He fails to do so on 15/1/98. There is an actual breach of contract.
*   **During the performance of the contract:**
    **Example:** C agreed to supply a Railway Company 3900 tons of railway chairs. After 1989 tons had been delivered, the Company told C that no more will be required. There is an express breach of contract by the Company. (Cort v. Ambergate Railway Company (1851))

### 2. Anticipatory Breach of Contract:

Section 39 of the Act deals with:
"When a party to a contract has refused to perform or disabled himself from performing his promise in its entirety, the promisee may put an end to the contract, unless he has signified by words or conduct, his acquiescence, in its continuance."

Thus, anticipatory breach of contract occurs:
*   **By express repudiation:**
    **Example:** A, a singer, enters into a contract with B, the manager of a theatre, to sing at his theatre two nights in every week during the next two months, and B agrees to pay her Rs. 100 for each night's performance. On the sixth night, A wilfully absents herself from the theatre. B is at liberty to put an end to the contract.
*   **By Implied repudiation:**
    **Example:** A agreed to sing at B's theatre on and from a given date. Before that date, A enters into a long-term agreement agreeing to sing at a different theatre from the same date. There is an implied repudiation of contract by A.

## Remedies in case of Anticipatory Breach

Law provides two remedies for the aggrieved party in case of an anticipatory breach:

*   **May elect to rescind:** The aggrieved party may elect to rescind the contract. Even though the time of performance has not arrived, they may treat the contract as terminated and at once sue for damages.
    **Example:** K promised to marry B after the death of K's father. Father still living, K announces his intention of not fulfilling his promise on the father's death and broke off the engagement. B, without waiting for K's father's death, at once brought action for breach. It was held that although the time for performance of the contract had not arrived, B was entitled to sue for breach of promise to marry. (Frost v. Knight (1872))
*   **May elect not to rescind:** The aggrieved party may elect not to rescind but to treat the contract as still alive and operative and wait for the time of performance and then hold the other party responsible for the consequences of non-performance.
    **Example:** A, a singer, enters into a contract with B, manager of a theatre, to sing at his theatre two nights in every week during the next two months, and B agrees to pay her Rs. 100 for each night's performance. On the sixth night, A wilfully absents herself from theatre. B is at liberty to put an end to the contract.

Where a party in a contract commits an anticipatory breach of the contract, the other party to the contract may treat the breach as putting an end to the contract and sue for damages, but in that event, he cannot ask for specific performance. The option open to the aggrieved party is that he may choose to keep the contract alive till the time for performance and claim specific performance, but in that event, he cannot claim specific performance of the contract unless he shows his readiness and willingness to perform the contract. (Jawaher Lal Wadhwa v. Haripada Chakroberty (1989))

## Consequences: If Elected Not to Rescind

If the promisee elects not to rescind the contract but to treat the contract as still alive and operative and wait for the time of performance, the consequences are as under:

*   **Operative for the benefit of both the parties:** The contract remains binding on both parties.
    **Example:** A agrees to sell certain goods to B, delivery to be given at the end of 1-12-1997. Immediately thereafter, A writes to B that he will not deliver the said goods. B does not accept such refusal of performance and informs A accordingly. At the stipulated time, A delivers the goods to B, but B refuses to accept them on the ground that A had earlier repudiated the contract. A sues B for the breach of contract. A will win on the ground that B had ignored the repudiation of A and had elected to treat the contract as operative. The contract is binding on both, and hence B is liable to perform his part of the contract.
*   **Right of damages lost if some event intervenes:** If an intervening event makes the contract impossible to perform, the party who repudiated earlier can benefit from this impossibility.
    **Example:** A agrees on 1-7-1998 to sell a horse to B on 15-7-1998. On 15-7-1998, A informs B that he will not sell the horse. B disregards this and elects to wait till 15-7-98. In the meantime, the horse dies. B cannot recover any damages from A, for the contract is dissolved by the death of the horse, and A can take advantage of it.

## Measure of Damages

### (a) When repudiation accepted:

In an anticipatory breach of contract, if the promisee elects to accept repudiation and treat the contract as discharged, he may sue the promisor at once for damages. The quantum of damages will be decided by the difference between the price prevailing on the date of the breach and the contract price.

**Example:** A agrees to supply 100 tons of iron to B on 1st July 1990 at Rs. 50/- per ton. The price shot up to Rs. 80/- and on 1st May, A informs B that he will not deliver the iron. B treats this as breach and at once sues for damages. B is entitled to get a sum of Rs. 3,000/- [100 (tons) x 30 (difference i.e. Rs. 80 - Rs. 50)].

### (b) When repudiation not accepted:

If the contract is kept alive till the date of performance of the contract, the measure of damages will be the difference between the price prevailing on the date of performance and the contract price.

**Example:** In the above illustration, the price of iron was fixed at Rs. 60 per ton and not more than 10 tons of iron could be sold by the order of Government on 1st of June. If B treats the contract as alive, the amount of damages would work out to Rs. 100/-. [10 tons x 10 (difference Rs. 60 - Rs. 50)] (Phool Chand v. Jugal Kishore (1927)).
