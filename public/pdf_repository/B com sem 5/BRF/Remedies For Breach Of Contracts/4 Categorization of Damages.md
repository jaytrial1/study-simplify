4_Categorization of Damages

This section details the various categories of damages that a court may award in cases of contract breach, explaining the characteristics and application of each type.

# Categorization of Damages

Damages awarded by the Court can be of different types:

## Ordinary or Compensatory Damages

These are damages that arise in the *ordinary course of events* from the breach of contract. Ordinary damages are limited to the *proximate consequences* of the breach, and remote consequences are generally not considered. The damages must directly flow from the breach, not merely be connected by an intervening cause.

## Special Damages

While courts ordinarily award damages arising in the usual course of events, under *special circumstances*, they may allow damages not naturally arising from the breach. Such damages must reasonably have been in the *contemplation of the parties* at the time they made the contract as a probable result of the breach. To claim special damages, any special circumstances leading to a special loss must be communicated to the other party beforehand.
*   **Example:** P sends exhibition samples via a Railway Company, marking "must be at place N Monday certain." Due to the company's fault, samples arrive late for the show. P can claim damages for the loss of profit from the show.

> **Answer to Q:**
> **Point out the meaning and difference between 'ordinary' and 'special' damages.**
>
> *Ordinary damages* are those losses that naturally and directly arise in the usual course of events from a breach of contract. They are restricted to the immediate consequences of the breach.
> *Special damages*, on the other hand, are losses that do not naturally arise from the breach but are incurred due to specific, unusual circumstances known to both parties at the time the contract was made. The key difference lies in the foreseeability and communication of the special circumstances: ordinary damages are presumed to be foreseeable, while special damages require prior knowledge or contemplation by both parties.

## Vindictive or Exemplary Damages

Generally, exemplary or vindictive damages are not awarded in ordinary cases of contract breach. Injured feelings or disappointment are typically not considered when assessing damages. However, there are two exceptions:

1.  **Breach of Promise to Marry:** Courts may award special damages considering the injured feelings of the party.
2.  **Wrongful Dishonour of Cheque:** Where a banker wrongfully refuses to honor a customer's cheque, despite sufficient funds, and the customer suffers damage to their business reputation, exemplary damages may be awarded.

## Nominal or Token Damages

Where the Court finds that the party has suffered *no pecuniary loss*, or if the breach is deemed *insignificant or paltry*, the Court may award a nominal or token amount as damages. These are usually granted to acknowledge the aggrieved party's right to claim damages for the breach, even if no real financial harm occurred. A "nominal" sum, like a shilling or a rupee, signifies a breach of contract without actual demonstrable damage.
