6_Liquidated Damages and Penalty

This section clarifies the distinction and treatment of liquidated damages and penalties in contractual agreements under both English and Indian law, outlining the guiding principles for their determination.

# Liquidated Damages and Penalty

Contracting parties may stipulate a sum of money to be paid in case of breach. The crucial question is whether the Court will accept this stipulated sum as the true measure of damages.

> **Answer to Q:**
> **Distinguish between 'Liquidated damages' and 'Penalty'. State the Indian and English Law.**
>
> **Distinction:**
> *Liquidated damages* are a genuine pre-estimation of the loss likely to be caused by a breach of contract, agreed upon by the parties at the time of forming the contract. Their purpose is to ascertain probable loss.
> *Penalty*, on the other hand, is a sum stipulated *in terrorem* (to intimidate) the defaulting party, disproportionate to the actual loss, and serves as a punishment rather than a true estimation of damage.
>
> **English Law:** English law upholds *liquidated damages* clauses, allowing the entire stipulated sum to be recovered regardless of the actual loss. However, it treats *penalty* clauses as invalid and will only award reasonable compensation, ignoring the stipulated penalty sum.
>
> **Indian Law:** Indian law (Section 74) does *not* recognize the distinction between liquidated damages and penalty in terms of enforcement. In either case, the courts in India will only allow *reasonable compensation*, not exceeding the amount stipulated in the contract, whether it's termed liquidated damages or a penalty. The actual loss suffered is determined by the court, and compensation is awarded accordingly.

It is a reasonable forecast of the damages that would be difficult to calculate precisely after a breach.
>
> A *Penalty*, conversely, is a sum stipulated in the contract that is disproportionate to the probable loss and is intended to compel performance by punishing the defaulting party, rather than compensating for actual loss. It acts as a deterrent and is often excessive compared to the actual damage.

## 1. English Law

According to English law, the stipulated sum is categorized as either *liquidated damages* or a *penalty*.

*   ***Liquidated damages*** are a sum agreed upon by the parties as an assessment of the damage or loss that will result from a breach. It is a fair and genuine pre-estimation of the likely loss.
*   ***Penalty***, conversely, is a sum that bears no relation to the probable loss and is stipulated *in terrorem* (for the purpose of penalizing) the party for non-performance. A penalty is disproportionate to the likely damages.

English law gives effect to *liquidated damages*, allowing the entire sum to be recovered regardless of actual damage. However, it relieves a party against a *penalty*, treating such clauses as invalid and allowing only reasonable compensation by way of damages.

## 2. Indian Law

In India, the distinction between 'liquidated damages' and 'penalty' is *not recognized* for the purpose of enforcement. Indian Courts allow only 'reasonable compensation' under Section 74 of the Act.
*   **Example:** If a contract stipulates a debt due on a specific date but allows payment by installments, with the whole balance becoming due upon default, this might not be penal if it's merely withdrawing a concession. However, if the debt itself only becomes due on installment dates, and default makes the whole balance payable, it would be a penalty.

## Rules for Determining Penalty vs. Liquidated Damages

The following rules guide whether a stipulation is a 'penalty' or 'liquidated damages':

1.  The Court must ascertain whether the stipulated payment is truly a 'penalty' or 'liquidated damages'.
2.  A stipulation is a penalty if the sum provided for is *extravagant* or *unconscionable* compared to the likely loss from the breach, or the greatest conceivable loss.
    *   **Example:** A agrees to repay a loan of Rs. 500 on Oct 1st, with a stipulation to pay Rs. 1,000 as 'liquidated damages' if he fails. The extra Rs. 500 will be a 'penalty'.
3.  The essence of a penalty is to compel performance by punishment (*in terrorem*). The essence of liquidated damages is a *genuine pre-estimate* of damage likely to be caused by the breach.
4.  The determination of whether a sum is a penalty or liquidated damages is a question of contract construction, decided based on the terms and inherent circumstances *at the time of making the contract*, not at the time of breach.
    *   **Example:** D agreed not to sell P's car below listed prices, with a £250 'agreed damages' for every breach. The Court of Appeal held this was a penalty.
5.  If a single sum is payable upon one or more of several events, some causing serious and others trifling damages, there is a presumption (but not absolute) that it is a penalty.
    *   **Example:** An artist engaged for £3.5 per night, with a £1,000 sum payable for any default. This was held to be a penalty, as a small default would trigger a disproportionately large payment.
