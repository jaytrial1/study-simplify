7_Quantum Meruit

This section explains the principle of *quantum meruit*, a remedy allowing a party to claim reasonable compensation for services rendered or work done when a contract is unenforceable or incomplete, along with its rules and limitations.

# Quantum Meruit

The expression '*Quantum Meruit*' means 'as much as merited' or 'as much as earned'. This principle is applied when a plaintiff claims reasonable remuneration for services rendered where there is no enforceable contract or express promise regarding the amount of remuneration. If a party has performed some work or service under a contract, and further performance is prevented or made useless by the other party, they may recover reasonable compensation for the work or service. This is known as the 'doctrine of Quantum Meruit'.

The right to claim *quantum meruit* does not arise directly from a contract like damages do. It is a claim based on a *quasi-contractual obligation* that the law implies under certain circumstances.

## Rules for Quantum Meruit

1.  **Breach of Contract:** In case of a breach, the aggrieved party is entitled to claim reasonable compensation for what they have already done under the contract.
2.  **Void Contracts (Sec. 65):** When a contract is discovered to be void, any person who has received an advantage under it is bound to restore it or compensate the person from whom it was received.
3.  **Non-Gratuitous Acts (Sec. 70):** When work is lawfully done or goods supplied by a person without intending to do so gratuitously to another, and the other person enjoys the benefit, the latter is bound to compensate the former or restore the thing.
    *   **Example:** A, a trader, mistakenly leaves goods with B, not intending it as a gift. B uses the goods. B must pay for them.
4.  **Divisible Contracts:** If a contract is divisible, part performance may entitle the defaulting party to claim compensation on a *quantum meruit* basis, provided the other party has taken advantage of the work already done.
    *   **Example:** P agreed to supply 100 maunds of rice at Rs. 30 per maund in two equal installments to B. P supplies 50 maunds but fails the second. B must pay for the 50 maunds received.
5.  **Lump Sum for Completed but Defective Work:** If a lump sum is to be paid for the entire work and the work is completed, though badly, the performing party can claim the lump sum. However, the other party can claim a deduction for the defective work.
    *   **Example:** A agreed to decorate B's flat for £600. The work was done but was defective, costing £300 to rectify. A could recover £300 from B.

## Limitations to the Doctrine of Quantum Meruit

1.  **Indivisible Contracts:** In an indivisible contract where a lump sum is promised for complete work, part performance will not entitle the party to any payment.
    *   **Example:** A sailor appointed for a lump sum voyage died two-thirds through. His representatives could not recover anything.
2.  **Guilty Party:** A person guilty of the breach cannot claim payment under this doctrine. Only the aggrieved party can claim.
3.  **Inability to Reject Benefit:** When the person receiving benefit cannot reject it for valid reasons, nor has any intention of owning the contract, they cannot be compelled to pay under *quantum meruit*.
    *   **Example:** A ship was repaired with more expensive material than authorized. The contractor could not recover under *quantum meruit* because the ship owner had no chance to reject the material after repair, and merely taking his own property back was not acceptance of the unauthorized work.
4.  **Absence of Implied Promise to Pay:** If there is nothing to show, either expressly or impliedly, a promise to pay for work already done, the doctrine is not applied, and nothing can be recovered.
