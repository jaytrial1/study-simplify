5_Principles for Assessing Damages

This section delves into the foundational rules governing the assessment of damages for breach of contract, drawing heavily from the landmark case of *Hadley v. Baxendale* and its application in Indian law.

# Principles for Assessing Damages

The modern law of damages, in both India and England, is largely based on the celebrated judgment in *Hadley v. Baxendale* (1854).

*   **Example (Hadley v. Baxendale):** X, a carrier, delayed the delivery of a machine part to Y, a manufacturer. Y claimed compensation for idle workers' wages, depreciation, and loss of profits. The first two items were allowed as natural consequences of the breach, but the loss of profit was disallowed as a remote consequence.

Alderson B. observed in this case: "Where two parties have made a contract which one of them has broken, the damages which the other party ought to have received in respect of such breach of contract should be such as may be fairly and reasonably be considered either arising naturally, i.e., according to the usual course of things, from such breach of contract itself, or such as reasonably be supposed to have been in the contemplation of both the parties, at the time they made the contract as the probable result of the breach of it." This statement forms the core of the *Hadley v. Baxendale* rule.

> **Answer to Q:**
> **On what basis are damages awarded in case of a breach of contract?**
>
> Damages are awarded on the basis of compensating the injured party for the loss or damage caused by the breach. The compensation covers losses that naturally arose in the usual course of things from the breach, or losses that the parties knew, at the time of making the contract, were likely to result from the breach. Remote and indirect losses are generally not compensated.

> **Answer to Q:**
> **State rules for assessing damages for breach of contract.**
>
> The rules for assessing damages for breach of contract are primarily derived from Section 73 of the Contract Act, which aligns with the principles of *Hadley v. Baxendale*. These rules state that compensation is given for losses that naturally arise in the usual course of things from the breach, or for losses that the parties knew were likely to result from the breach. Compensation is not given for remote or indirect losses. Additionally, the injured party has a duty to mitigate damages, and difficulty in assessment does not prevent the award of damages. Specific rules apply to damages for loss of reputation, mental pain, and stipulations for interest.

## Indian Law on Damages

The principles for determining the amount of damages in India are laid down in Sections 73 to 75 of the Contract Act, which are also based on *Hadley v. Baxendale*. Section 73 stipulates:

"When a contract has been broken, the party who suffers by such breach is entitled to receive, from the party who has broken the contract, compensation for any loss or damage caused to him thereby, which naturally arose in the usual course of things from such breach, or which the parties knew, when they made the contract, to be likely to result from the breach of it." (Sec. 73, para 1)

"Such compensation is not to be given for any remote and indirect loss or damage sustained by reason of the breach." (Sec. 73, para 2)

## Key Rules for Damages

### 1. Actual Loss: Ordinary Damages

Ordinarily, the injured party is entitled to recover only the actual loss suffered. Under Section 73, a party suffering by a breach is entitled to compensation for loss or damage:
*   Which naturally arose in the usual course of things from such breach.
*   Which the parties knew to be likely to result from the breach.

The amount of damages awarded cannot exceed the actual loss suffered or likely to be suffered by the claimant, provided their actions are reasonable.

#### Natural and Usual Loss

In calculating actual loss, the Court considers only such losses as:
*   May be fairly and reasonably considered.
*   As arising naturally.
*   In the usual course of things from the breach.
*   **Example:** A contracts to buy 50 Quintals of rice from B at a stated price, no delivery time fixed. A later informs B that he will not accept the rice. B is entitled to compensation equal to the amount, if any, by which the contract price exceeds what B can obtain for the rice when A informs him of non-acceptance.

#### Should Not Be Remote

Under Section 73, compensation is not given for any remote or indirect loss or damage.
*   **Example:** A contracts to pay B Rs. 100 on a specified day. A fails to pay. B, consequently, cannot pay his debt and is ruined. A is liable only for the principal sum and interest up to the payment date, not for B's ruin, as it's a remote consequence.

### 2. Special Loss: Special Damages

The Court may allow remote or special damages not naturally arising from the breach, but which the parties knew or must have contemplated as the probable result of the breach. To claim such damages, any special circumstances leading to a special loss must have been brought to the other party's notice.

### 3. Other Rules

#### (i) Duty to Mitigate Damages Suffered

A party to a contract is bound to use all reasonable means to mitigate the damages consequent upon the other party's breach. The plaintiff bears the burden of proving that they took all reasonable steps to mitigate damages. They cannot recover any part of the damage attributable to their own neglect to mitigate.
*   **Example:** A took a shop on lease and gave an advance to B. B could not give A possession. A chose not to do business for eight months, despite other shops being available. A was entitled only to a refund of his advance, as his duty was to minimize damages by taking another shop.

#### (ii) Difficulty of Assessment

The fact that damages are difficult to assess does not prevent the injured party from receiving them.
*   **Example:** In a beauty competition, C was one of 50 ladies selected by votes, from whom 12 would be chosen for theatrical jobs. C could not attend the final selection due to H's breach of contract. C was entitled to damages, even though calculation was difficult.

#### (iii) Damages for Loss of Reputation

'Loss of reputation' is generally not a valid ground for claiming damages. An exception exists for a customer-tradesman whose cheque is wrongfully dishonored by a banker. In such a case, the trade customer can claim losses to their trade reputation. The general rule applied is: "The smaller the amount of cheque dishonoured, the larger the amount of damages awarded."

#### (iv) Damages for Mental Pain, Inconvenience and Discomfort

In ordinary cases, damages for mental pain or discomfort caused by the breach are not allowed. However, they may be allowed in special cases.
*   **Example (i):** A train pulled passengers in the wrong direction, forcing the plaintiff and his wife to walk home at midnight. The jury awarded £8 for inconvenience but not £20 for the wife's illness, reasoning that walking was foreseeable, but the cold was not a necessary or probable consequence.
*   **Example (ii):** A manager was dismissed without being allowed to work during his notice period, preventing him from earning commission. He could recover the commission he would have earned, but not for injured feelings or difficulty in finding new employment.

#### (v) Damages for Personal Inconvenience

Damages are also awarded for personal inconvenience suffered due to a breach of contract.
*   **Example:** A passenger set down by a railway company at a remote place, forcing him to walk several miles, was entitled to damages for the inconvenience. However, loss of business due to failing to keep an appointment was held to be too remote.

## Stipulation for Interest

Section 74 of the Contract Act also covers stipulations for interest in case of breach:
"When a contract has been broken, if a sum is named in the contract as the amount to be paid in case of such breach or if the contract contains any other stipulation by way of penalty, the party complaining for the breach is entitled, whether or not actual damage or loss is proved to have been caused thereby, to receive from the party who has broken the contract reasonable compensation not exceeding the amount so named or, as the case may be, the penalty stipulated for."

**Explanation:** "A stipulation for increased interest from the date of default may be stipulation by way of penalty."

**Exception:** "When any person enters into any bail-bond, recognizance or other instrument of the same nature, or, under the provisions of any law, or under the orders of the Central Government or any State Government, gives any bond for the purpose of any public duty or act in which the public are interested, he shall be liable, upon breach of the condition of any such instrument, to pay the whole sum mentioned therein."

**Explanation:** "A person who enters into a contract with Government does not necessarily thereby undertake any public duty, or promise to do an act in which the public are interested."
*   **Example (1):** A contracts with B to pay B Rs. 1,000 if he fails to pay B Rs. 500 on a given day. A fails to pay Rs. 500. B is entitled to recover reasonable compensation not exceeding Rs. 1,000.
*   **Example (2):** A gives a recognizance binding him in a penalty of Rs. 500 to appear in Court. He forfeits his recognizance. He is liable to pay the whole penalty.
*   **Example (3):** A borrows Rs. 100 from B and gives a bond for Rs. 200 payable by five yearly instalments of Rs. 40, with a stipulation that, in default of any instalment, the whole becomes due. This is a stipulation by way of penalty.

> **Answer to Q:**
> **How is a stipulation for interest construed?**
>
> A stipulation for interest in a contract, particularly for an increased rate from the date of default, may be construed as a *penalty* under Section 74 of the Contract Act. If it is deemed a penalty, the party complaining of the breach is entitled to reasonable compensation, not exceeding the stipulated amount. However, a stipulation for compound interest at the same rate as simple interest is generally not a penalty, while a higher rate is. A covenant to accept a reduced interest rate for punctual payment does not make the original rate a penalty.

#### Rules for Stipulation of Interest

##### 1. Stipulation for Enhanced Rate of Interest

*   **From the date of the bond:** A stipulation for increased interest from the date of the bond is *always* in the nature of a penalty, and the Court will award only reasonable compensation.
*   **From the date of the default:** A stipulation for increased interest from the date of the default *may be* a stipulation by way of penalty.

##### 2. Stipulation for Compound Interest

*   **At the same rate as simple interest:** A stipulation in a bond for payment of compound interest on failure to pay simple interest at the same rate as was payable upon the principal from the date of the default is *not a penalty*.
*   **At a rate higher than simple interest:** A stipulation for the payment of compound interest at a rate higher than that of simple interest *is a penalty* and would be relieved against.

##### 3. Stipulation for Payment of Interest at Lower Rate

A covenant to accept interest at a reduced rate if interest is paid punctually does not make the original rate of interest a penalty.
