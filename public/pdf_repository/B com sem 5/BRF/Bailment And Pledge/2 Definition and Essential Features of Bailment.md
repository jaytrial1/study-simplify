This section delves into the precise legal definition of bailment, tracing its etymological origin, and then elaborates on the key characteristics that define such a contract.

# Definition and Essential Features of Bailment

The term "*bailment*" originates from the French word '*Bailler*', meaning 'to deliver'. In a legal sense, bailment establishes a special relationship where movable property of one person is transferred to another for a specific purpose.

## Indian Definition: Section 148

"*A 'bailment' is the delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall, when the purpose is accomplished, be returned or otherwise disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them. The person delivering the goods is called the '*bailor*'. The person to whom they are delivered is called 'the *bailee*'."*

**Explanation:**
If a person already possesses another's goods and subsequently contracts to hold them as a bailee, they become the bailee, and the owner becomes the bailor, even if there was no initial delivery by way of bailment.

**Examples:**
1.  A delivers a radio to B for repairs. This delivery creates a contract of bailment, with A as the *bailor* and B as the *bailee*.
2.  A gives gold to B for making ornaments. This delivery creates a contract of bailment, with A as the *bailor* and B as the *bailee*.

## Characteristic Features of Bailment

### 1. Based on Contract
A bailment is typically formed by an agreement between the *bailor* and the *bailee*. However, in certain exceptional situations, such as in the case of a *finder of goods*, bailment can be implied by law between the finder and the true owner.

### 2. Delivery of Possession
A bailment necessarily involves the transfer of possession of goods from one person to another for a specific purpose under a contract.

**Example:**
In *Kaliaperumal Pillai v. Visalakshi Achi (1937) M.W.N. 1042*, merely leaving a box of jewels in a room where a goldsmith was working under the plaintiff's supervision was not considered sufficient delivery to constitute bailment, especially when the plaintiff retained the keys. Thus, the defendants were not held liable as bailees when the jewels were lost.

**How Delivery to Bailee is Made (Section 149):**
"The delivery to the bailee may be made by doing anything which has the effect of putting the goods in the possession of the intended bailee or of any person authorised to hold them on his behalf."

**Examples:**
*   In *Hardayal v. B & N.W. Rly. Co.(1929) 8 Pat. 808*, goods merely left at railway premises were held not to constitute delivery.
*   In *N.R Srinivasa Iyer v. New India Ins. Co. Ltd. (1983) 3 SCC 458*, when an insured motor vehicle was delivered to a repairer on behalf of the insurer, and the insurer agreed to the repair, the insurer was held liable as a *bailee* and the repairer as a *sub-bailee* when the vehicle was destroyed by fire in the repairer's custody.

### 3. Movable Goods
Only 'goods', meaning *movable property*, can be bailed. Therefore, *money* cannot be bailed as it is not 'goods', nor can *immovable property* like land or buildings.

### 4. Specific Purpose
The goods are delivered for a specific purpose, such as lending, hiring, safe custody, or as security for debts.

### 5. Return of Specific Goods
Upon the accomplishment of the purpose, the goods must be returned or disposed of according to the *bailor's* directions.

**Important Note:**
When a customer deposits money into a bank account, it is generally *not* a contract of bailment because the bank is not obligated to return the specific notes deposited. In this scenario, the relationship is that of *creditor and debtor*. However, if a customer opens a locker with a bank, while it might seem like a bailment, it typically isn't because the effective control (possession) remains with the customer (who holds the key), not entirely with the banker. Therefore, it does not strictly qualify as a contract of bailment.

### 6. Retention of Ownership
In a bailment, the *bailor* retains ownership of the goods, even though possession is transferred to the *bailee*. Bailment does not involve a change of ownership.

**Consideration in a Contract of Bailment:**
The detriment suffered by the *bailor* in parting with the possession of the goods is considered sufficient consideration to support a contract of bailment.

**Q: What is bailment? State its characteristic feature.**
> **Answer:** Bailment is the delivery of goods by one person to another for a specific purpose, with an agreement for their return or disposal as directed. Its characteristic features include being based on a contract (or implied by law), involving the delivery of possession of movable goods, having a specific purpose, requiring the return of the specific goods, and the bailor retaining ownership.

**Q: Define bailment.**
> **Answer:** As per Section 148 of the Indian Contract Act, "A 'bailment' is the delivery of goods by one person to another for some purpose, upon a contract that they shall, when the purpose is accomplished, be returned or otherwise disposed of according to the directions of the person delivering them."

**Q: What is bailment?**
> **Answer:** Bailment is a contractual arrangement where movable goods are temporarily transferred from an owner (bailor) to another person (bailee) for a specific purpose, with the understanding that these goods will be returned or dealt with as per the owner's instructions once the purpose is fulfilled.
