This section introduces Archimedes' principle, which quantifies the buoyant force, and discusses its applications.

# Archimedes' Principle

We can investigate the buoyant force further using a simple experiment. Suspend a stone from a spring balance in the air and note its weight. Then, slowly lower the stone into a container of water. You will observe that the reading on the spring balance decreases as the stone is immersed. The decrease in the reading indicates that an upward force (buoyant force) is acting on the stone, reducing the apparent weight measured by the balance. This decrease in reading stops once the stone is fully submerged, as the volume of displaced water (and thus the buoyant force) reaches its maximum.

This leads to **Archimedes' principle**:

When a body is immersed fully or partially in a fluid, it experiences an upward force that is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by it.

This principle explains why the spring balance reading decreased in the activity – the buoyant force equals the weight of the water the stone pushed out of the way. Once the stone was fully immersed, the volume of displaced water was constant, so the buoyant force remained constant, and no further decrease in the reading was observed.

Archimedes, a Greek scientist, is credited with this discovery. The story goes that he realized the principle when he noticed the water overflowing as he got into a bathtub. This understanding famously helped him determine if a king's crown was made of pure gold by comparing its buoyant force in water to that of pure gold of the same weight.

Archimedes' principle has many practical applications:
*   It is used in the design of **ships** and **submarines**. Ships float because they displace a weight of water equal to their own weight. Submarines control their depth by changing their overall density using ballast tanks.
*   **Lactometers**, used to test the purity of milk (based on its density), and **hydrometers**, used to measure the density of liquids, are also based on this principle.

### Questions

1. You find your mass to be 42 kg on a weighing machine. Is your mass more or less than 42 kg?
> **Answer:** Your mass is exactly 42 kg. A weighing machine on Earth measures your weight (the force of gravity on you) and converts it to a mass reading by dividing by the local value of $g$. The reading represents your mass, which is a fundamental property and does not change.

2. You have a bag of cotton and an iron bar, each indicating a mass of 100 kg when measured on a weighing machine. In reality, one is heavier than other. Can you say which one is heavier and why?
> **Answer:** The iron bar is heavier. The weighing machine measures apparent weight, which is the true weight minus the buoyant force from the air. Both objects have a mass of 100 kg, so their true weight ($m \times g$) is the same. However, the bag of cotton has a much larger volume than the iron bar because cotton is less dense. This larger volume displaces more air, resulting in a greater buoyant force from the air acting on the cotton bag. Therefore, the apparent weight of the cotton bag (measured by the scale) is less than its true weight. To show the same "mass" reading (100 kg), the cotton bag's *true* weight must be slightly *more* than the iron bar's true weight to compensate for the larger air buoyancy. So, the iron bar is heavier in terms of true weight.
