This section defines how to calculate work done when a constant force acts on an object, particularly when the force is in the same direction as the displacement. It introduces the unit of work, the joule, and discusses positive and negative work.

# Work Done by a Constant Force

Scientifically, work is defined precisely. We first consider the case where the force acts in the direction of displacement.

Let a constant force, $F$ act on an object, causing it to be displaced through a distance, $s$ in the direction of the force. Let $W$ be the work done. Work is defined as the product of the force and the displacement:

Work done $=$ force $\times$ displacement
$W = F s$

Thus, the work done by a force acting on an object is the magnitude of the force multiplied by the distance moved in the direction of the force. Work has only magnitude and no direction; it is a scalar quantity.

If $F=1 \mathrm{~N}$ and $s=1 \mathrm{~m}$ in the equation $W=Fs$, the work done is $1 \mathrm{~N} \times 1 \mathrm{~m} = 1 \mathrm{~N} \mathrm{~m}$. The unit of work is the newton metre (N m), also called the **joule (J)**.
**1 Joule (J)** is defined as the amount of work done on an object when a force of 1 Newton (N) displaces it by 1 metre (m) along the line of action of the force.

Looking at the equation $W=Fs$:
*   What is the work done when the force on the object is zero? If $F=0$, then $W = 0 \times s = 0$. Work done is zero.
*   What would be the work done when the displacement of the object is zero? If $s=0$, then $W = F \times 0 = 0$. Work done is zero.
This aligns with the conditions mentioned earlier for work to be done.

**Example 10.1** A force of 5 N is acting on an object. The object is displaced through 2 m in the direction of the force (Fig. 10.2). If the force acts on the object all through the displacement, then work done is $5 \mathrm{~N} \times 2 \mathrm{~m}=10 \mathrm{~N}$ m or 10 J.

> **Answer:**
> Given Force, $F = 7 \mathrm{~N}$.
> Given Displacement, $s = 8 \mathrm{~m}$.
> The force acts in the direction of displacement.
> Work done, $W = F \times s = 7 \mathrm{~N} \times 8 \mathrm{~m} = 56 \mathrm{~N} \mathrm{~m} = 56 \mathrm{~J}$.
> The work done in this case is 56 J.

Consider a situation where the force and displacement are in the same direction, like a baby pulling a toy car parallel to the ground (Fig. 10.4). The baby exerts a force in the direction the car moves. The work done is the product of the force and displacement. In such cases, the work done by the force is considered **positive**.

Now consider an object moving with uniform velocity in one direction, and a retarding force, $F$, is applied in the opposite direction. The angle between the force and displacement is $180^{\circ}$. If the object stops after a displacement $s$, the work done by the force $F$ is taken as **negative**. It is denoted by a minus sign. The work done is $F \times (-s)$ or $(-F \times s)$.

From this, it's clear that work done by a force can be either positive or negative.

## Activity

- Lift an object up. Work is done by the force exerted by you on the object. The object moves upwards. The force you exerted is in the direction of displacement. However, there is the force of gravity acting on the object.
- Which one of these forces is doing positive work?
- Which one is doing negative work?
- Give reasons.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Work done by your force:** Your force is upwards, and the displacement is upwards. Since the force and displacement are in the same direction, the work done by your force is **positive**.
> *   **Work done by the force of gravity:** The force of gravity is downwards, but the displacement is upwards. Since the force and displacement are in opposite directions, the work done by the force of gravity is **negative**.

Work done is negative when the force acts opposite to the direction of displacement. Work done is positive when the force is in the direction of displacement.

**Example 10.2** A porter lifts luggage of 15 kg from the ground and puts it on his head 1.5 m above the ground. Calculate the work done by him on the luggage.

**Solution:**
Mass of luggage, $m=15 \mathrm{~kg}$.
Displacement, $s=1.5 \mathrm{~m}$.
The force exerted by the porter is upwards, equal to the weight of the luggage ($F=mg$).
Taking $g \approx 10 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-2}$ (as done in the original example calculation, although $9.8 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-2}$ is also commonly used),
Force, $F = mg = 15 \mathrm{~kg} \times 10 \mathrm{~m} \mathrm{~s}^{-2} = 150 \mathrm{~N}$.
Work done, $W = F \times s = 150 \mathrm{~N} \times 1.5 \mathrm{~m} = 225 \mathrm{~N} \mathrm{~m} = 225 \mathrm{~J}$.
Work done is 225 J.

4. A pair of bullocks exerts a force of 140 N on a plough. The field being ploughed is 15 m long. How much work is done in ploughing the length of the field?

> **Answer:**
> 1.  Work is said to be done when a force acts on an object and causes it to be displaced.
> 2.  The expression for work done when a force $F$ acts on an object in the direction of its displacement $s$ is $W = F \times s$.
> 3.  1 Joule (J) of work is done on an object when a force of 1 Newton (N) displaces it by 1 metre (m) along the line of action of the force.
> 4.  Given Force, $F = 140 \mathrm{~N}$.
>     Given Displacement, $s = 15 \mathrm{~m}$.
>     Work done, $W = F \times s = 140 \mathrm{~N} \times 15 \mathrm{~m} = 2100 \mathrm{~J}$.
>     The work done in ploughing the length of the field is 2100 J.
