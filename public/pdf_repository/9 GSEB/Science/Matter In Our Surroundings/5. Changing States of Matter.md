This section explains how matter can change from one state to another by altering temperature and pressure, introducing concepts like melting, boiling, fusion, vaporization, sublimation, and deposition.

# Can Matter Change its State?

We know from observation that water exists in three states:
*   Solid, as ice.
*   Liquid, as familiar water.
*   Gas, as water vapour.

What happens inside matter when it changes state? What happens to the particles during these changes? How does this change of state occur? Let's find the answers.

## Effect of Change of Temperature

As the temperature of solids increases, the kinetic energy of their particles also increases. This increased kinetic energy causes the particles to vibrate with greater speed. The energy supplied by heating helps the particles overcome the forces of attraction holding them together. The particles then leave their fixed positions and begin to move more freely. Eventually, a point is reached where the solid melts and transforms into a liquid. The minimum temperature at which a solid melts to become a liquid at atmospheric pressure is called its *melting point*.

The melting point of a solid is a measure of the strength of the force of attraction between its particles.

The melting point of ice is 273.15 K (or 0°C). The process of melting, where a solid changes into a liquid, is also known as *fusion*. When a solid melts, its temperature remains constant until all of it has melted. So, where does the heat energy go?

During the melting experiment, you notice that the temperature of the system stays the same after reaching the melting point until all the ice is melted, even though heating continues. This heat energy is used up in changing the state of the substance by overcoming the forces of attraction between the particles. Since this heat is absorbed by the ice without causing a rise in temperature, it is considered *hidden* within the substance and is known as *latent heat*. The word "latent" means hidden. The amount of heat energy required to convert 1 kg of a solid into liquid at atmospheric pressure at its melting point is called the *latent heat of fusion*. Therefore, particles in water at 0°C (273 K) have more energy than particles in ice at the same temperature because they have absorbed the latent heat of fusion.

When we supply heat energy to water, the particles move even faster. At a specific temperature, the particles gain enough energy to break free from the forces of attraction holding them in the liquid state. At this temperature, the liquid begins to change into a gas. The temperature at which a liquid starts boiling at atmospheric pressure is called its *boiling point*. Boiling is considered a *bulk phenomenon* because particles from the entire volume (bulk) of the liquid gain enough energy to convert into the vapour state.

For water, the boiling point at atmospheric pressure is 373 K (which is 100°C; calculated as 273 + 100 = 373 K).

Can you define the latent heat of vaporisation?

> **Answer:** The latent heat of vaporisation is the amount of heat energy required to change 1 kg of a liquid into its gaseous state at atmospheric pressure at its boiling point.

Particles in steam (water vapour) at 373 K (100°C) have more energy than particles in water at the same temperature. This is because the particles in steam have absorbed extra energy in the form of latent heat of vaporisation.

From this activity, we infer that some substances can undergo sublimation.

## Effect of Change of Pressure

We've learned that the differences between the states of matter are due to the varying distances between their constituent particles. What happens if we apply pressure and compress a gas contained in a cylinder? Will the particles get closer? Do you think that changing the pressure can alter the state of matter?

Applying pressure and reducing temperature can cause gases to liquefy.

Have you heard of solid carbon dioxide (CO₂)? It is stored under high pressure. Solid CO₂ converts directly into a gaseous state when the pressure decreases to 1 atmosphere without passing through the liquid state. This is why solid carbon dioxide is also known as *dry ice*.

Thus, we can conclude that pressure and temperature together determine the state of a substance, whether it exists as a solid, liquid, or gas.

### Questions

1.  Convert the following temperature to celsius scale: a. 300 K b. 573 K

> **Answer:**
> a. To convert from Kelvin to Celsius, subtract 273: 300 K - 273 = 27°C.
> b. To convert from Kelvin to Celsius, subtract 273: 573 K - 273 = 300°C.

2.  What is the physical state of water at:
    a. 250°C
    b. 100°C?

> **Answer:**
> a. At 250°C, which is well above its boiling point (100°C), water exists in the gaseous state (as steam or water vapour) at standard atmospheric pressure.
> b. At 100°C, water is at its boiling point. It exists as both liquid water and gaseous water vapour (steam) at standard atmospheric pressure during the phase transition.

3.  For any substance, why does the temperature remain constant during the change of state?

> **Answer:** The temperature remains constant during a change of state (like melting or boiling) because the heat energy supplied is used to overcome the forces of attraction between the particles of the substance, rather than increasing their kinetic energy. This absorbed energy is stored as latent heat.

4.  Suggest a method to liquefy atmospheric gases.

> **Answer:** To liquefy atmospheric gases (which are typically at room temperature and pressure), one needs to decrease the temperature and increase the pressure. Applying high pressure brings the gas particles closer together, and cooling reduces their kinetic energy, allowing the intermolecular forces of attraction to become significant enough to transition the gas into a liquid state.
