# Physical Nature of Matter

## Matter is Made Up of Particles

Historically, there were two main viewpoints regarding the nature of matter. One perspective held that matter was continuous, similar to a solid block of wood. The other view proposed that matter was made up of discrete particles, like sand. Let's perform an activity to determine whether matter is continuous or particulate.

### Activity: Dissolving Salt or Sugar

*   Take a 100 mL beaker.
*   Fill half the beaker with water and mark the water level.
*   Dissolve some salt or sugar using a glass rod.
*   Observe if the water level changes.
*   What do you think happened to the salt?
*   Where did it disappear?
*   Did the level of water change?

> **Answer:** The salt (or sugar) dissolves and spreads throughout the water. It disappears from visible sight because its particles separate and fit into the spaces between the water particles. The level of water generally does not change significantly, or changes only slightly, because the solute particles occupy the spaces between the solvent particles rather than adding to the overall volume in a simple additive way.

To answer these questions, we use the concept that matter consists of particles. The salt or sugar from the spoon has dispersed throughout the water. This is illustrated in the accompanying figure.

## How Small Are These Particles of Matter?

### Activity: Dilution of Potassium Permanganate

*   Take 2-3 crystals of potassium permanganate and dissolve them in 100 mL of water.
*   Take out approximately 10 mL of this solution and put it into 90 mL of clear water.
*   Take out 10 mL of this solution and put it into another 90 mL of clear water.
*   Keep diluting the solution like this 5 to 8 times.
*   Is the water still coloured?

> **Answer:** Yes, the water is still coloured, although the colour becomes lighter with each dilution.

This experiment demonstrates that even a small number of potassium permanganate crystals can colour a large volume of water (about 1000 L if diluted enough times). This leads us to conclude that a single crystal of potassium permanganate must contain millions of incredibly tiny particles that continue to divide into smaller particles upon dilution.

A similar activity can be conducted using 2 mL of Dettol instead of potassium permanganate. The characteristic smell of Dettol can still be detected even after repeated dilutions.

These observations suggest that the particles of matter are exceedingly small, beyond our ordinary imagination.
