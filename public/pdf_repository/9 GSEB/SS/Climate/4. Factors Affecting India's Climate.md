This section details the geographical and atmospheric factors that influence the distribution and characteristics of climate elements across India.

# Factors Affecting Climate

Several factors on the surface of the earth control the distribution and amount of climate elements. These are called **climatic factors**. The following factors affect the climate of India:

*   **Latitude:** Climatic type changes according to the latitude of a place. There is a distance of about 111 kilometres between two successive latitudes. Sun rays fall vertically over the equatorial region, making it very hot throughout the year. India is divided into two zones of different climatic conditions: the torrid zone (south of Tropic of Cancer) and the temperate zone (north of Tropic of Cancer).
*   **Distance from Sea:** The distance of landmass from the sea is another important factor. Water and land absorb and release solar heat differently. Coastal regions experience a temperate climate, while interior places far from the sea coast experience a continental climate. For example, Mumbai has a temperate climate because it is near the sea, while Nagpur or Delhi, situated far from the sea, have a continental climate.
*   **Altitude from the Sea Level:** As altitude increases, air pressure and air temperature decrease. Higher relief areas tend to get more rain. Due to high altitude, the Himalayan peaks remain snow-covered throughout the year. Moist air cools down as it rises, leading to rain. Rainfall generally increases with the altitude of mountains. Air temperature decreases approximately 1°C for every 165 metres of ascent or by 6.5°C for every 1000 metres from the surface.
*   **Atmospheric Pressure and Winds:** India falls in the path of north-east trade winds. These winds originate from the tropical high-pressure belt in the northern hemisphere and deflect due to the Coriolis force, blowing towards the equator. Historically used for oceanic trade, they are called 'Trade Winds'. As they blow over landmass, they contain less moisture. The Indian Ocean, Arabian Sea, and Bay of Bengal surround India to the south, west, and east, respectively. Winds passing over these vast water bodies absorb moisture and bring rain to India.
    *   During winter, high pressure develops north of the Himalayas, causing cold, dry winds to blow towards the low-pressure oceanic area.
    *   During summer, low pressure develops over central Asia and the Indian landmass due to high temperature. High pressure prevails over the Indian Ocean in the south, causing winds to blow from the Indian Ocean towards North India. These are known as **'South-West Monsoon Winds'**. They are moisture-laden and bring rain to India.
