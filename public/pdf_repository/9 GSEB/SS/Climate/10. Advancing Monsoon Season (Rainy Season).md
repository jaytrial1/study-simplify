This section covers the vital rainy season in India, detailing the South-West monsoon winds, their branches, and how they bring rain across the country.

# The Advancing Monsoon (Rainy Season - June to September)

The rainy season is extremely important for India, being an agrarian country. About 80% of the country's rainfall is received between June and September. Indian farmers are busy with farming from the beginning of this season. **South-West monsoon winds** are responsible for the rain, humidity, and cloudy weather during this period, hence it is also known as the season of 'South-West Monsoon winds'. Almost entire India receives rainfall due to these winds.

By the end of May, high temperatures over India and Central Asia create a low-pressure area. Simultaneously, the Indian Ocean in the south develops high pressure. This pressure difference causes winds to blow from the high-pressure Indian Ocean towards the northern low-pressure pockets. These summer monsoon winds blow from the ocean towards India. Being moisture-laden, they bring rain.

Due to the peninsular shape of South India, the South-West monsoon winds are divided into two branches:

1.  **Arabian Sea Current:**
    *   Generally starts in early June at Kerala.
    *   The Western Ghats obstruct these winds, causing heavy rain on the western slopes (windward side).
    *   After crossing the Western Ghats, the winds lose much moisture, resulting in less rain on the leeward side (e.g., Mumbai receives >200 cm, while Pune, further east, gets ~75 cm).
    *   Rain decreases northwards along the western coast (Kerala to Maharashtra).
    *   One branch enters Madhya Pradesh through the Narmada valley and merges with the Bay of Bengal current.
    *   Another branch passes over Gujarat, Saurashtra, and Kachchh towards Rajasthan. Due to the lack of high mountains or dense forests in Gujarat, moisture condensation is less likely, resulting in less rain.
    *   These winds contain less moisture upon reaching Rajasthan, causing low rainfall; deserts get less than 10 cm annually.

2.  **Bay of Bengal Current:**
    *   First enters West Bengal and reaches up to Meghalaya.
    *   These winds contain maximum moisture, giving heavy rain in Meghalaya, especially on the slopes of the Khasi, Garo, and Jaintia hills.
    *   Winds deflect here and blow from the south-east, crossing over West Bengal, Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, and reaching Haryana.
    *   Their moisture content decreases along the way, so regions encountered earlier receive more rainfall than those later.
    *   Both the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal currents merge in the northern Himalayan region, giving good rain, with occasional snowfall.

Cyclones originating over the Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea also enter India during the monsoon season, making the monsoon more active in some areas. An average of five to six cyclones pass through India during monsoon, contributing to rainfall.

Monsoon winds do not blow throughout the year or from the same direction, and their onset is irregular. The phenomenon of **'Rain Break'** is associated with monsoons, where spells of rain are interrupted by rainless days. Variations in physical features also affect rainfall distribution, making the amount and distribution of rainfall unequal across India (e.g., heavy rain in Assam/Meghalaya vs. insignificant rain in Rajasthan deserts and Leh in Kashmir).
