This section describes the structure of the independent judiciary and the methods by which the Constitution can be amended.

# Judiciary and Amendment Process

## Independent, Impartial, Integrated Judiciary

The Constitution provides for an **independent and impartial judiciary**. The structure is integrated:

*   **Supreme Court:** At the top, binding on all subordinate courts.
*   **High Courts:** At the state level.
*   **District Courts:** At the district level.
*   **Local and Special Courts:** At the taluka level.

The Supreme Court's judgments are binding on all subordinate courts. In case of conflict between the Union and State Governments, matters relating to the Constitution, and interpretation of statutes, the final decision rests with the Supreme Court. The Supreme Court is the protector and guardian of the Constitution.

## Amendments in Constitution

Compared to other countries' Constitutions, the Constitution of India is **dynamic** and can be amended as required. Constitutional provisions can be amended in three ways:

*   **Simple Majority:** Amendments can be made by a simple majority of members present and voting in the Parliament.
*   **Special Majority:** Certain amendments require a special majority, which is a two-third ($\frac{2}{3}$) majority of members present and voting.
*   **Special Majority + State Ratification:** Concerning Union-State relations, a special majority ($\frac{2}{3}$) and the concurrence of not less than 50% of the State Legislatures is required.

If an amendment affects the inter-relations between the Union and States, the structure of the Union, or the Supreme Judiciary, consent from half of the total States is required. Amendments can also be brought about by Parliament based on court judgments, provided they do not change the basic structure of the Constitution. The Constitution is considered a mixture of flexible (amendable by simple majority) and rigid (requiring special majority or state consent) features.
