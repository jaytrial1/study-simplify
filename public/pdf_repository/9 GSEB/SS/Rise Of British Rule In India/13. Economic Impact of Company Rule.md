# Economic Impact of the Company Rule

Evaluating the 100 years of Company rule reveals a drastic change in India's economic status. India, once renowned globally, was reduced to a supplier of raw materials for England and a market for goods produced in British factories.

Bengal, which previously exported cotton cloth, raw silk, sugar, jute, and muslin (from 1708 to 1756), saw its economic prosperity decline after the **Dual Power Policy**.

### Key Concepts & Examples

*   **Dual Power Policy (Dwimukhi Shasan):** Introduced in Bengal, this system gave the British the right to collect revenue (Diwani) while leaving the responsibility for people's welfare and administration (Nizamat) with the Nawab. This arrangement gave the British power without responsibility and the Nawab responsibility without power, leading to economic exploitation and neglect of governance.

The Company's unfair revenue collection policies indebted Indian peasants. The British government imposed heavy taxes on Indian textile industries. They employed deceitful practices to ruin prosperous Indian industries, leading to their collapse. Indian artisans became poor and unemployed. Company traders forced Bengal weavers into contracts for fixed quantities of cloth within short deadlines, punishing those who refused with lashing or imprisonment.

Before the Company's arrival, Indian villages were largely self-reliant and prosperous. Under Company rule, they became dependent and impoverished.

Despite the negative impacts, some infrastructure developments occurred during Company rule, such as the first railway line (Mumbai-Thane), steamer service between India and England, and the development of ports in Mumbai, Madras (Chennai), and Kolkata.
