This section describes the formation, extent, and characteristics of the vast northern plains of India, highlighting their importance and sub-divisions.

# Great Northern Plain

This plain is situated between the northern mountainous region and the southern peninsula. It is formed by the alluvial sediments brought by Himalayan rivers like Satluj, Ganga, and Brahmaputra. Sediment layers can be up to 50 metres thick in some areas. As these plains cover a large area in North India, they are known as the **great plains of Northern India**.

The plain is about 2400 km long and is considered one of the largest river plains globally. Its western part is narrower than the eastern part. It is mostly a level land, with no part higher than 180 metres above sea level. The plain is narrow near Delhi, with the Satluj plain to the west and the Ganga plain to the east.

This region is considered the most prosperous region of India. Important cities like Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow, Allahabad, Varanasi, Patna, and Kolkata are located here.

The Sindhu river and its tributaries (Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Satluj) originate in the Himalayas. A region between two rivers is called '**DOAB**' (Do = two, Ab = water). The plain formed by five rivers is called '**Punjab**' (Panj + Ab). Most of this plain is in Pakistan.

Based on physiography, the plain is divided into four parts:
1.  Bhabar
2.  Tarai
3.  Bangar
4.  Khadar

A narrow belt of gravels and stones (8-16 km wide) parallel to the rivers from Sindhu up to Tista in the Shivalik foothills is called **Bhabar**. Next is the **Tarai** region, which is more humid and marshy, characterized by dense forests and diverse wildlife. The old alluvium in the plains is called '**Bangar**'. Due to consistent deposition, it forms a terrace shape higher than flood plains. The new sediment deposited during floods is called '**Khadar**'.
