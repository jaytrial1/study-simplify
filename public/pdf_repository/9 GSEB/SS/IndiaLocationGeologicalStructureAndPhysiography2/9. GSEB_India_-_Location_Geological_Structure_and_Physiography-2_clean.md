2_Northern Mountainous Region: The Himalayas

This section details the Northern Mountainous Region, focusing on the vast Himalayan mountain system and its sub-divisions.

# Northern Mountainous Region: The Himalayas

This is an important natural region of India, known as the **Himalayan mountain system**. It spreads over 2400 km in a west-east direction in the north, forming an arc shape. Its width varies between 240 km and 320 km.

The Himalaya is not a single mountain but a complex of many ranges. It extends from Afghanistan in the west to Myanmar in the east, passing through India, Nepal, and Bhutan. It is part of the mountain system known as the **Pamir knot**.

The Himalayas can be broadly divided into two main divisions:
1.  Northern Himalayan Region
2.  Eastern Himalayas

## Northern Himalayan Region

This region consists of three mountain ranges running parallel to each other.

### Greater Himalayas

This is the northernmost range and is the loftiest among the Himalayas. It contains more than 40 peaks exceeding 7000 metres in height.
*   The most famous peak is **Mt. Everest**, standing at 8848 metres on the Nepal-China border, known as Sagarmaththa in Tibet.
*   **Godwin Austin** or **K²** (8611 m) is the highest peak in India.

Other notable high peaks mentioned:
*   Mt. Everest: 8848 metres
*   K2 (Mt. Godwin Austin): 8611 metres
*   Kanchenjungha: 8598 metres
*   Makalu: 8481 metres
*   Dhavalgiri: 8198 metres
*   Annapurna: 8070 metres

High mountain passes in the Greater Himalayas include Jelep La, Nathu La, and Shipki La. Mansarovar (China), a sacred holy place, is located in this range.

### Central Himalayas (Lesser Himalayas)

Located south of the Greater Himalayas, this range is spread over a wider area with a width ranging from 80 to 100 km. It includes ranges like Pir Panjal, Mahabharat, and Nagtiba. Many hill stations have developed here due to its moderate height, such as Dalhousie, Dharamshala, Shimla, Mussoorie, Ranikhet, Almoda, Nainital, and Darjeeling. Famous holy places like Gangotri, Yamunotri, Badrinath, Kedarnath, and Hemkund Sahib are also located here. Extremely beautiful natural valleys like Kullu, Kangada, and Kashmir are found in this region.

### Shivalik (Outer Himalayas)

Situated further south, this is the southernmost range, with most of it falling within India. It is about 10 to 15 km wide and has an average height of 1000 metres. This range features peculiar valley formations covered by gravel, stones, and thick sediments. Locally, these valleys are known as '**DUN**', examples being Dehra Dun, Patli Dun, and Kothari Dun.

## Eastern Himalayas

Ranges in the eastern part of the Himalayas have lesser height and are often known as Hills. These hills are spread as smaller ranges.
*   Patkai Hills are in Arunachal Pradesh.
*   Naga Hills are in Nagaland.
*   Lushai (Mizo) Hills are in Mizoram.
These hills are near the eastern border and continue into the Arakan Yoma range of Myanmar. Garo, Khasi, and Jaintia Hills are located in Meghalaya.

Mountainous regions here receive more rainfall, leading to the development of dense forests. Due to the forested nature, road and railway development is limited.

3_Great Northern Plain

This section describes the formation, extent, and characteristics of the vast northern plains of India, highlighting their importance and sub-divisions.

# Great Northern Plain

This plain is situated between the northern mountainous region and the southern peninsula. It is formed by the alluvial sediments brought by Himalayan rivers like Satluj, Ganga, and Brahmaputra. Sediment layers can be up to 50 metres thick in some areas. As these plains cover a large area in North India, they are known as the **great plains of Northern India**.

The plain is about 2400 km long and is considered one of the largest river plains globally. Its western part is narrower than the eastern part. It is mostly a level land, with no part higher than 180 metres above sea level. The plain is narrow near Delhi, with the Satluj plain to the west and the Ganga plain to the east.

This region is considered the most prosperous region of India. Important cities like Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow, Allahabad, Varanasi, Patna, and Kolkata are located here.

The Sindhu river and its tributaries (Jhelum, Chenab, Ravi, Beas, and Satluj) originate in the Himalayas. A region between two rivers is called '**DOAB**' (Do = two, Ab = water). The plain formed by five rivers is called '**Punjab**' (Panj + Ab). Most of this plain is in Pakistan.

Based on physiography, the plain is divided into four parts:
1.  Bhabar
2.  Tarai
3.  Bangar
4.  Khadar

A narrow belt of gravels and stones (8-16 km wide) parallel to the rivers from Sindhu up to Tista in the Shivalik foothills is called **Bhabar**. Next is the **Tarai** region, which is more humid and marshy, characterized by dense forests and diverse wildlife. The old alluvium in the plains is called '**Bangar**'. Due to consistent deposition, it forms a terrace shape higher than flood plains. The new sediment deposited during floods is called '**Khadar**'.

4_Peninsular Plateau

This section describes the Peninsular Plateau, highlighting its ancient origin, general characteristics, and its division into the Malwa Plateau and the Deccan Plateau.

# Peninsular Plateau

This is the oldest region of India, appearing as an inverted triangle. Its average height is about 600 to 900 metres. The northern part slopes towards the north-east, as shown by the flow of Chambal, Son, and Damodar rivers. The southern part slopes towards the south-east. Most of its area is in the south, hence it is also called the Southern Plateau. Being surrounded by sea on three sides, it is known as a **Peninsular Plateau**.

The Peninsular Plateau is divided into two parts:
1.  Malwa Plateau
2.  Deccan Plateau

## Malwa Plateau

The **Aravalli Range**, one of the oldest fold mountains in the world, is situated to the north-west of the Malwa plateau. **Mt. Abu** is a famous hill station on this range. Its highest peak is **Gurushikhar** (1722 metres).

To the south of this region, rivers Chambal and Betwa originate from Vindhyachal and flow northwards to meet river Yamuna. River Son flows northwards to meet river Ganga. The direction of these rivers indicates that the region slopes northwards. The north-eastern part of this central upland is known as Bundelkhand.

Rivers Luni and Banas originate in the Aravalli range in the north-east; they vanish in the Rann of Kachchh. Sabarmati and Mahi rivers meet the Gulf of Khambhat. The flow of these rivers indicates that the western part of the Malwa Plateau slopes towards the south-west. Rajmahal Hills and Shillong plateau are considered part of the Chhota Nagpur plateau, which includes the Ranchi plateau.

## Deccan Plateau

The **Deccan Plateau** is located south of the Satpuda, Mahadev, and Maikal ranges, which are south of the Malwa plateau. The north-western parts of the plateau are made of lava deposits.

Its western boundary is marked by the **Western Ghats**, which run north-south along the Arabian Sea coast. The Western Ghats have several local names:
*   Sahyadri in Maharashtra and Karnataka
*   Nilgiri in Tamil Nadu
*   Annamalai and Cardamum ranges along the border of Kerala and Tamil Nadu

The southern portion of the Western Ghats is higher.

The average height of the Deccan Plateau is generally 900 to 1000 metres, but some areas are higher. Isolated hills over 900 metres form the eastern boundary, known as the **Eastern Ghats**. The general slope of the Deccan Plateau is towards the south-east, evident from the direction of river flow. Most rivers flow eastwards into the Bay of Bengal, except for the Narmada and Tapi rivers, which flow westwards.

5_Coastal Plains and Archipelagos

This section describes the narrow plains along the Indian coast and the island groups (archipelagos) belonging to India.

# Coastal Plains and Archipelagos

## Coastal Plains (Plains along the sea coast)

The Peninsular plateau is bordered by a narrow belt of plains from Kachchh to Odisha, divided into western and eastern coastal plains.

### Western Coastal Plain

Extends from Gujarat to Kerala. It is mostly narrow, except in Gujarat, and is very undulating. The southern part, south of Goa, is known as the **Malabar Coast**. West coast rivers have developed creeks at their estuaries, which are mostly submerged valleys formed by the emergence of the sea shore, providing good conditions for fishery. Many natural ports, including Mumbai and Marmagao, are located on the western coast. Backwaters, locally known as **Kayal**, have developed along the southern coast of Kerala.

### Eastern Coastal Plain

This plain is much broader than the west coast plain. It has substantial alluvial deposition in the del deltas of rivers like Kaveri, Krishna, Godavari, and Mahanadi. The northern part is known as the **North Sircar Coast**, and the Tamil Nadu coast is known as the **Coromandel coast**.

## Archipelagos

India has a few **archipelagos** (groups of islands). The major ones are Andaman-Nicobar and Lakshadweep.

### Lakshadweep

Many smaller islands located off the Kerala coast. These coral islands have a horse-shoe shape and are called '**Atolls**'.

### Andaman - Nicobar

Situated in the Bay of Bengal, these islands are numerous and located far from the Indian coast. Some are formed by volcanic activity. The islands are spread over 350 km and hold strategic importance.

### Key Concepts & Examples

*   The only active volcano in India is on **Barren Island** in the Andaman-Nicobar archipelago.
*   Narkondam, nearby, is a dormant volcano.

Thus, India exhibits great diversity in its physical features due to variations in climate and relief. Each region has distinct characteristics and contributes to the nation's progress. The fertile northern plains are known as the 'Storehouse of Grains', the southern plateau is rich in mineral resources, and the northern mountains are the source of many rivers and home to diverse forest resources.

6_Rocks: Definition and Types

This section defines rocks and classifies them into three major types based on their formation process.

# Rocks: Definition and Types

A **Rock** is a composite matter made up of one or more minerals. Rocks can vary in hardness, softness, porosity, and weight. Different processes lead to the formation of various types of rocks.

Rocks are divided into three categories based on their formation:
1.  Igneous rocks
2.  Stratified rocks
3.  Metamorphic rocks

## Igneous Rocks

These rocks are formed by the cooling and solidification of **magma**, the semi-liquid matter found in the intensely hot interior of the earth. As they are formed from heat, they are called **Igneous (Agneya) rocks**. They were the first rocks to form the earth's crust, earning them the name **primary rocks**.

Examples of igneous rocks include Granite (a plutonic rock) and Basalt. Such rocks are found in many parts of Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, and the southern peninsula in India. Igneous rocks are among the most solid types of rocks.

## Stratified Rocks

Also known as **Sedimentary rocks**, these are formed from the disintegration of igneous rocks due to factors like water and other forces. The broken rock material is deposited in water over time, forming layers. As more layers are deposited, the upper layers exert pressure on the lower ones, leading to the formation of rocks with distinct layers.

Examples include gypsum, limestone, and coal. Coal and gypsum are obtained from states like Bihar and Jharkhand.

## Metamorphic Rocks

In specific conditions involving high temperature and pressure from rock strata, the form, composition, and characteristics of igneous and sedimentary rocks change entirely into a new form. These newly formed rocks are called **Metamorphic rocks**.

Marble and quartzite found in Rajasthan are good examples of metamorphic rocks.

7_Minerals: Definition and Classification

This section defines minerals and provides a general classification based on their properties and uses.

# Minerals: Definition and Classification

A '**Mineral**' is a matter formed due to natural organic or inorganic processes, possessing a specific chemical composition. Minerals are found within the earth in solid, liquid, and gaseous forms. Their availability depends on the geological structure of the earth's surface.

Minerals like iron, copper, nickel, gold, and silver are found in igneous rocks. Coal, mineral oil, and natural gas are typically available from stratified rocks. Slate, marble, and diamond are found in metamorphic rocks.

About 200 minerals are used directly or indirectly in daily life. While a universally accepted classification is difficult, a general classification is as follows:

## Classification of Minerals

1.  **Metallic minerals**:
    *   Precious metallic minerals: Gold, silver, platinum
    *   Light metallic minerals: Magnesium, bauxite, titanium
    *   Minerals of general use: Iron, copper, lead, zinc, tin, nickel
    *   Minerals used in alloys: Chromium, manganese, tungsten, vanadium
2.  **Non-Metallic minerals**: Limestone, chalk, asbestos, mica, fluorspar, gypsum, sulphur, diamond
3.  **Energy resources**: Coal, mineral oil and natural gas, uranium, thorium

9_Soil: Definition and Formation

This section defines soil and explains the process by which it is formed from the breakdown of rocks.

# Soil: Definition and Formation

**Soil** is a basic resource, especially important for agriculture. It is a thin layer formed of organic and inorganic matter on the surface of the earth. It is the thin layer on the earth's crust.

## Soil Formation

Soil formation is a long-term process resulting from the **denudation** (erosion and weathering) of rocks. Factors like temperature, rain, snow, air, vegetation, and insects erode the rock surface, turning it into a powdery form, which creates the land layer.

This initial layer contains gravels, smaller stones, pebbles, and soil particles, collectively known as '**Regolith**'. Regolith initially contains only mineral contents. Eventually, biotic matter (like decomposed plants and animals), air, and water mix with the regolith, and this mixture forms the soil.

10_Soil: Types in India

This section classifies and describes the six major types of soils found in India, highlighting their characteristics and distribution.

# Soil: Types in India

Soils in India are classified into six major types:
1.  Alluvial Soil
2.  Black (or regur) soil
3.  Red soil
4.  Laterite soil
5.  Mountainous soil
6.  Desert soil

## Alluvial Soil

Alluvial soil is divided into two types: **Khadar** and **Bangar**.
*   **Khadar soil** is formed from fresh alluvial deposits, typically found near rivers due to floods. It is generally sandy and very fertile.
*   **Bangar soil** contains old alluvium, found in the upper valley regions. It is sticky and has a dark colour.

Alluvial soil is found in many parts of the country, including Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, and West Bengal. Its fertility varies by location, but generally, it is very fertile.

## Black Soil (Regur Soil)

This soil is found mostly in Maharashtra, western Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, and Tamil Nadu. It is considered a 'gift' of the peninsular plateau. **Black soil** is very sticky and fertile and can retain humidity for a long time. It is formed from igneous rocks and is highly useful for cotton cultivation, hence its name **Black Cotton Soil**. It is also known as **regur soil**.

## Red Soil

Found in regions with igneous and metamorphic rocks. Its characteristic red colour comes from ferrous and other humus contents. **Red soil** is porous and fertile. It is seen in Goa, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, and Jharkhand.

## Laterite Soil

**Laterite soil** develops due to excessive erosion by rain. Heavy rainfall causes leaching, where humus contents from the top soil descend into lower layers. As it contains less humus, it is less fertile. The red colour results from the erosion of red sandstones containing iron and aluminium. Such soil is found in the mountainous regions of the Deccan, Karnataka, Kerala, Odisha, and parts of the North-East.

## Mountainous Soil

Found in the mountainous regions like Meghalaya, Arunachal Pradesh, eastern hill ranges, Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu-Kashmir, and Ladakh. **Mountainous soil** often has more humus content due to forests, though this varies. Soil on the Shivalik Range is less fertile and developed, being sandy, porous, and lacking humus.

## Desert Soil

Found in arid and semi-arid regions of Gujarat, Rajasthan, Punjab, and Haryana. **Desert soil** is typically alkaline and has low humus content. Agriculture is possible in this soil primarily through irrigation.

The diversity in soils across India is a result of the diverse climate and relief features of the nation.
