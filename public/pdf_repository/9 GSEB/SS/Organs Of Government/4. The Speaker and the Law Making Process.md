This section details the role of the Speaker in the Lok Sabha and explains how a proposed law (Bill) becomes an actual law (Act).

# The Speaker of Loksabha

The **Speaker** is the presiding officer of the Lok Sabha.

*   **Role:** To organize the proceedings of the Lok Sabha according to prescribed rules, maintain discipline, order, and dignity of the House.
*   **Election:** The Speaker and Deputy Speaker are elected by the members of the Lok Sabha from among themselves.
*   **Authority:** The Speaker presides over sessions, controls functioning, expects discipline, and is the protector of the dignity of the House. The Speaker's decisions in the House are final.

The Speaker is expected to function in a firm and impartial manner, even if they belong to a political party. The Speaker can be removed from their post by a resolution passed by a majority of members of the Lok Sabha, after giving a 14-day notice period.

All speeches and criticisms in the Lok Sabha are addressed to the Speaker. Proceedings can be conducted in Hindi or English, but the Speaker can permit a member to speak in their own language if they don't know Hindi or English. A member's seat is declared vacant if they are absent for 60 continuous days without informing the Speaker.

### Casting Vote

If there is a tie in the number of votes for and against a matter (Bill, question, etc.) during proceedings, the Speaker can cast a deciding vote, known as the **'Casting vote'**. The Speaker generally does not vote otherwise. Shri Ganesh Vasudev Mavlankar was the first Speaker of Lok Sabha.

In the absence of the Speaker, the Deputy Speaker presides. If both are absent, a 'Speaker's Panel' of senior, experienced members familiar with parliamentary proceedings from different parties conducts the proceedings.

## When can a Bill Become an Act?

The Parliament's important work includes making new laws, updating old ones, and removing obsolete ones. A proposal for a law is called a **Bill**. Bills can be Ordinary Bills, Money Bills, or Bills to amend the Constitution. The process for a Bill to become an Act involves passing through both Houses of Parliament and receiving the President's assent.

Any Bill can be presented in either House of Parliament by a Minister or any member. If there is a dispute between the two Houses on a Bill, the President can call a joint sitting, which is chaired by the Speaker of the Lok Sabha. The Bill can be passed in a joint sitting by a majority vote of the members present.

### Procedure of making an Act from Bill - Ordinary Bill (Non-Financial)

The process involves multiple stages:

1.  **First Reading:** The Bill is introduced in a House. The Minister or member presenting it describes its title, objectives, and reasons. General discussion on the Bill takes place. Objections must be reasoned, and voting may occur if necessary.
2.  **Second Reading:** This involves a detailed, section-wise discussion of the Bill. Its objectives and implications are examined. Amendments can be proposed based on public opinion, group suggestions, or opposition party input. Voting is carried out on these amendments.
    *   Sometimes, the Bill is sent to a committee of subject experts and members with special knowledge for detailed examination and suggestions for changes based on public opinion. The committee's report is submitted to the House.
3.  **Third Reading:** This is mainly a formal stage. Arguments for or against the acceptance of changes are presented, and a final vote is taken. If the Bill gains a majority, it is declared passed by that House.
4.  **Passage to the Second House:** The Bill, signed by the Chairman/Speaker of the first House, is sent to the second House. It goes through the same process (First, Second, and Third readings) in the second House.
5.  **Agreement and President's Assent:** If the second House passes the Bill (with or without amendments that the first House accepts), it is sent to the President for signature. After receiving the President's assent, the Bill becomes an Act.

### Disagreement or Delay

*   If the second House does not pass the Bill or keeps it for more than six months, it is considered rejected.
*   If the two Houses disagree, a joint session can be summoned by the President to resolve the dispute by majority vote.

### President's Options on a Bill

When a Bill is sent to the President, he has three options:

1.  Sign the Bill, thereby approving it.
2.  Withhold his signature (keep the Bill).
3.  Send the Bill back to the Parliament for reconsideration.

Once the President signs, the Bill becomes an Act and is published in the Government Gazette, coming into force from a specified date.

### Amendment to the Constitution Bill

A Bill to amend the Constitution requires a special majority in each House of Parliament:

*   Must be passed by a majority of the total membership of that House.
*   Must be passed by a $\frac{2}{3}$rd majority of the members present and voting.
*   If the Bill relates to matters affecting Centre-State relations, it also requires ratification by the Legislatures of more than half of the States.
*   This Bill also follows a process similar to Ordinary Bills.

### Procedure Subject to Money Bill

*   **Identification:** The Speaker of the Lok Sabha decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill.
*   **Definition:** A Bill containing recommendations related to the budget or financial matters is a Money Bill.
*   **Origin:** Money Bills can only be introduced in the **Lok Sabha**. The Union budget is typically presented in the Lok Sabha around February 28th by the Finance Minister.

The process for a Money Bill in Lok Sabha:

1.  Preliminary discussion.
2.  Discussion on expenses mentioned in the budget, with the Finance Minister providing financial details.
3.  Ministers from various departments (Agriculture, Defence, etc.) present their demands, and voting occurs to approve these demands.
4.  Sources of income and taxes are presented separately and require separate approval.

After being approved by the Lok Sabha, the Money Bill is sent to the **Rajya Sabha** for recommendations.

*   The Rajya Sabha must return the Bill to the Lok Sabha with its recommendations within **14 days**.
*   The Lok Sabha can either accept or reject the recommendations of the Rajya Sabha (totally or partially).
*   If the Rajya Sabha does not return the Bill within 14 days, it is considered passed by the Rajya Sabha.
*   If the Lok Sabha accepts the recommendations, the Bill is passed by both Houses.
*   If the Lok Sabha rejects the recommendations and passes the Bill again, the Money Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses.

The Rajya Sabha has limited powers regarding Money Bills.

After passing both Houses, the Money Bill is sent to the President, who **must** sign it. If the budget is not passed, the government must resign.

Besides law-making, Parliament discusses social, economic, political, defence, and foreign matters. The Lok Sabha specifically monitors and controls the Executive (Cabinet of Ministers and bureaucrats). Parliament is the custodian of public funds, ensuring proper utilization. No new taxes can be levied or existing ones changed without Parliament's consent. Parliament also has the power to remove high officials like the President, Vice President, Judges of Supreme/High Courts, Chief Election Commissioner, Auditor General, or Attorney General through **Impeachment** for proven misbehaviour, inefficiency, or violation of the Constitution.
