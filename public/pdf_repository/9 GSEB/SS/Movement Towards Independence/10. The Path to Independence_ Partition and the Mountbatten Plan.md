This section describes the events leading to India's independence, including the trial of INA commanders, naval mutiny, the British decision to leave, the appointment of Mountbatten, growing communal tensions, and the eventual acceptance of partition by Indian leaders.

# Achieving Independence and Partition of India

The commanders of the Indian National Army were arrested as prisoners of war, accused of treason, and put on trial. Prominent lawyers like *Bulabhai Desai* and *Jawaharlal Nehru* defended them. Although the British Government initially handed down punishments, widespread protests across the country forced the government to withdraw the sentences, bowing to populist opinion.

These events, coupled with the *naval mutiny* in Bombay (1946), made the British realize that their rule in India was no longer sustainable. On *February 20, 1947*, *Attlee*, the British Prime Minister, announced that the British Government would transfer all powers to the Government of India according to the Cabinet Mission formula and would leave India by June 1948.

In this changing scenario, *Lord Mountbatten* was appointed as Viceroy, replacing Lord Wavell. Upon taking charge, India was experiencing severe *communal riots*, creating a pathetic situation. In July 1946, elections for the Constituent Assembly saw Congress secure 201 out of 210 seats from the provinces, and the Muslim League secure 73 out of 78 seats. The League intensified its pressure for Pakistan, leading to terrible communal riots and unrest across India, particularly after *August 16, 1946*.

Observing the situation, leaders like *Sardar Patel*, *Jawaharlal Nehru*, and other Congress leaders reluctantly felt that accepting the Muslim League's demand for Pakistan was the only alternative, as the League and *Jinnah* were adamant. The Muslim League boycotted the first Cabinet meeting on December 9, 1946, and its executive decided on January 31, 1947, to boycott cabinet meetings and launch a rapid program for the demand of Pakistan.

Mountbatten convinced Nehru and Sardar Patel, arguing that a well-organized Central Government with unified administrative units would be more peaceful and progressive than a weak Central Government composed of numerous autonomous and opposing constituents. The national leaders found this argument acceptable. Sardar Patel and Nehru discussed the matter with Gandhiji, who, with a heavy heart, also helplessly accepted the arrangement for the partition of India.
