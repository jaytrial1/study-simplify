This section discusses the Khilafat movement, which arose from post-World War I events affecting Turkey, and its connection to the Non-Co-operation movement launched by Mahatma Gandhi and the Congress.

# Khilafat Movement

During the First World War, Turkey joined the German side. When England won, the subsequent treaty imposed harsh and unjust terms on the defeated side. The Sultan of Turkey was also the Khalifa, the religious head of Muslims worldwide. The movement in India opposing the strict terms imposed on Turkey and the Sultan became known as the *Khilafat Movement*. The chief leaders were the *Ali brothers* - *Maulana Shaukat Ali* and *Maulana Mohammad Ali*. Gandhiji requested the Congress to support the Movement to foster Hindu-Muslim unity, and with Congress support, the Movement became severe.

# Non Co-operation Movement (1920-22)

The Nagpur Conference in December 1920 approved the *Non-Co-operation Movement*. The Congress now strongly demanded complete independence (*Swarajya*) instead of merely self-government (*Swashasan*) under British rule.

## Constructive Aspect

The positive aspects of the movement included strengthening Hindu-Muslim unity, emphasizing the use of *Swadeshi* articles, reviving spinning wheels (*charkha*) in every home, collecting one crore rupees for the '*Tilak Swarajya Fund*', abolishing untouchability, promoting national education, and advocating for prohibition.

## Negative Aspect

The negative aspects involved giving up government jobs, boycotting government school-colleges, boycotting legislatures, resigning from government courts and local self-government bodies, boycotting foreign cloth and other articles, and boycotting government functions and titles.

## Programmes for Non Co-operation Movement

At the beginning of the Movement, Mahatma Gandhi and Rabindranath Tagore surrendered their titles, '*Kaiser-e-hind*' and '*Knighthood honour*', respectively. Other national leaders also gave up their titles and positions. Students left English schools and colleges. Foreign clothes were publicly burned in various places. The visit of the Duke of Connaught to India was boycotted (November 1921). The ceremony honoring the Prince of Wales was also boycotted. These incidents generated considerable national excitement.

On the other hand, national schools and colleges were established, such as Kashi, Bihar, Jamia-Milia, and Gujarat Vidyapiths. Due to vigorous promotion of *Swadeshi* goods, imports of cloth, footwear, and luxurious items from England drastically reduced. This reaction alarmed the British Parliament, and the government was shocked by the significant economic loss to England.

More than one crore rupees were collected in the Tilak Fund. Hindu-Muslim unity was evident on many occasions. However, the '*Mopla Revolt*' (Malabar) between Hindu landlords and Muslim peasants was an exception, which was criticized and suppressed by the British army.

The government adopted a policy of suppression, using uncontrolled lathi-charge, firing, mass arrests, and inhuman torture. Unsuccessful attempts were made to break Hindu-Muslim unity.

# The Chauri-Chaura Incident and Withdrawal of the Movement

On February 5, 1922, people took out a rally in the Chauri-Chaura Village in Gorakhpur, Uttar Pradesh. The police opened fire on the crowd. When the police ran out of bullets, the people attacked the police station and set it on fire, resulting in the death of 21 policemen. Upon hearing this news, Gandhiji stated, "I have committed a Himalayan blunder by giving the tool of Satyagraha in the hands of people who do not understand the value of non-violence." He immediately suspended the Movement.

## Importance of Non-Co-operation and its effects

Although the Movement did not fully achieve its immediate aims, its negative and constructive aspects successfully raised awareness among people about their rights. It created a negative atmosphere towards the government and a strong feeling of opposition against injustice. Political awareness spread across all classes in India. The faith in independence grew stronger, and the fear of lathi charges, punishment, and imprisonment was reduced. Youth and women came forward to serve the nation, and the Congress transformed into an organization of the people. National education schools were established, and Hindi gained more importance than English. The Movement, which had been limited to cities, towns, and intellectuals, spread to the common people and villages.
