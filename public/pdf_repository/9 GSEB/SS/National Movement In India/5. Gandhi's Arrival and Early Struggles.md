This section covers Mahatma Gandhi's return to India, his political and spiritual influences, and the early movements he became involved in, including the controversial Rowlatt Act and the tragic Jallianwala Bagh massacre.

# Arrival of Gandhiji to India (1915)

Gandhiji returned to India in 1915 after successfully using *satyagraha* to fight against racism in South Africa. His political mentor was *Gopalkrishna Gokhle*, and his spiritual mentor was *Shrimad Rajchandra*, both of whom inspired him. Gandhiji traveled across India, observing the lives of its people. He identified exploitation as the root cause of poverty and began planning to abolish British rule.

# Rowlatt Act (1919)

The British government formed the *Rowlatt Act* in 1919 under the Chairmanship of Rowlatt, the Minister of Law.

This act was intended to suppress revolutionaries and nationalists. It became known as the '*Black Act*' because it curtailed individual freedom and freedom of speech.

According to this Act, any suspicious person could be arrested and jailed without a trial. Gandhiji called it the '*Black Act*', and Motilal Nehru described it as '*snatching away, of argument, appeal and advocacy*'.

The British government gained immense power under this Act to suppress any opposition. Leaders and the general public expressed strong opposition. Meetings, rallies, shows, and strikes were organized. Gandhiji was arrested in Delhi. The agitation intensified when Dr. Satyapal and Dr. Kitchlu were arrested in Punjab. The government attempted to suppress all opposition forcefully.

# Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (13th March 1919)

When popular leaders Dr. Satyapal and Dr. Kitchlu were arrested in Amritsar, Punjab, a public gathering was organized in the Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar on the day of Baisakhi to protest the arrests.

General O'Dyer (referred to as Odonil Dyer in the text) arrived with his troops and, without warning, opened fire on the innocent people gathered there with a machine gun. The Bagh had high walls on four sides, an unused well in the middle, and only one narrow exit. Many people died in the firing.

According to the government report, 379 people died and 1200 were injured. The committee appointed by the Congress estimated that 1000 people died. The 'Hunter Commission', which investigated the case for the British Government, defended General Dyer, considering it 'an innocent mistake that happened unknowingly'. In contrast, when Dyer returned to England, he was honored with a sword and 2000 pounds. This event deeply shocked Indians. After this inhuman act, Gandhiji's faith in the British government was completely lost. This tragedy played a crucial role in uniting the nation and became a foundational event for the future Non-Co-operation movement.
