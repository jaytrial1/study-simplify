This section details the British policy of 'divide and rule', specifically focusing on the partition of Bengal in 1905, the resulting protest, and the emergence of the Swadeshi and Boycott movements.

# Boycott and Indigenous Movements

The Boycott and Indigenous movement, alongside the Bangbhang Movement (1905), was a crucial event in the Indian national movements. It gained momentum due to the British policy of 'divide and rule'.

Bengal was a large province under British rule, encompassing present-day Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha. It was a central point of national awakening and the source of impetus and thought for the national movement. The English, using the 'divide and rule' strategy, encouraged communalism and division among people, particularly because Hindus and Muslims had fought together against them in the 1857 revolt.

Viceroy Curzon (1899-1905) divided the vast Bengal Province into East Bengal and West Bengal, ostensibly for administrative efficiency.

There was strong opposition to the division of Bengal. When Bengal was divided on October 16, 1905, the entire region observed the day as 'National Mourning Day'. On the same day, an announcement was made to boycott all foreign goods and encourage the use of indigenous goods. Not only Bengalis but other Indians also believed the division was intended to suppress the growing feeling of nationalism. On that day, Hindus and Muslims tied *rakhis* to each other across Bengal to express their unity.

This movement had three major characteristics:
1.  Adopt indigenous products (*Swadeshi*)
2.  Boycott foreign goods
3.  Adopt national education

India greatly benefited from the *Swadeshi Movement*, while English industry was severely affected by the boycott of foreign goods. Imports of cloth from Manchester stopped. Imports of sugar, shoes, cigarettes, tobacco, etc., from England also reduced, while the sale of Indian-made cloth increased. Factories for making indigenous goods were established. The movement's effect was felt in other provinces like Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Mumbai, and Madras, besides Bengal. The echo of the *Swadeshi Movement* was also strongly felt in Gujarat and the British Parliament. Within six years (1911), the partition of Bengal was scrapped after reconsideration by the Parliament. This was a significant victory for the Indians, demonstrating the power of relentless united efforts and a new awakening against English rule.
