This section provides an overview of how the British East India Company established its control in India, the administrative structure they built, and the exploitation that led to the major uprising of 1857.

# Introduction

During their time in India, the British became familiar with the country's political state. The British East India Company exploited India's internal weaknesses and political disputes. Starting from 1757 A.D., after gaining a foothold in Bengal, the Company established its dominance over India within 100 years, culminating in the uprising of 1857 A.D. To maintain this supremacy, the English gradually developed a strong administrative structure. This structure introduced common laws, a unified administration, and a legal system based on the British model. However, the laws and administration were designed primarily to benefit England, making it prosperous at the cost of extreme exploitation of India.

# The Great National Uprising of 1857

The uprising of 1857 was caused by several factors, including political dissatisfaction, economic exploitation, social and religious issues, and military factors. An immediate cause was the introduction of the Enfield Rifle in the army. *Mangal Pandey* was the first martyr of the uprising. Many revolutionaries participated, such as *Nanasaheb*, *Tatya Tope*, *Raja Kuwarsinh*, *Rani Laxmibai*, and *Bahadurshah Zafar*.

The uprising had significant effects, including the end of the Company Rule and the transfer of power to the British Crown (ruled by Queen Victoria). It also led to changes in administrative, military, social, and religious policies.

Reasons for the failure of the 1857 uprising included its premature start before the planned time, lack of central leadership, insufficient and unsophisticated weapons, revolutionaries prioritizing self-interest over nationalism, and a lack of suitable and organized leadership.
