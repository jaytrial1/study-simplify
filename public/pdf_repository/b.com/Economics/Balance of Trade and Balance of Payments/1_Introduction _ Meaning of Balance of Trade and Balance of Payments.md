1. Introduction: Meaning of Balance of Trade and Balance of Payments

The concepts of balance of trade and balance of payments occupy a very important place in the sphere of international trade. We shall discuss about these concepts in this chapter.

# Meaning of Balance of Trade
The balance of trade of a country represents a systematic record of transactions pertaining to all visible items of exports and imports during a given period. The value of all goods and commodities imported and exported are shown therein. A country has to make payments for the goods imported while it receives payments for the goods exported. The balance of trade a country, thus, indicates the relationship between the aggregate value of exports and the aggregate value of imports during a given year.

The balance of trade of a country is said to be in balance when the money value of its exports is equal to the money value of its imports. However, if the money value of exports is higher than the money value of imports, the balance of trade is said to be favourable. On the other hand if the money value of imports is higher than the money value of exports, the balance of trade is said to be unfavourable for the country.

# Meaning of Balance of Payments
According to Kindleberger, "The balance of payments of a country is a systematic record of all economic transactions between the residents of a reporting country and residents of foreign countries during a given period of time." Balance of payments is, thus, a flow account and is a record of all economic transactions of a country with the other countries of the world. It indicates the total payments which a country has to make to the other countries of the world and the total payments which it has to receive from the other countries of the world. Balance of payments, thus, refers to total receipts and payments of a country in terms of foreign exchange during a given period of time. The receipts and payments are made mainly on account of the following transactions:

(a) Visible exports and imports, that is to say, exports and imports of goods and commodities.
(b) Invisible exports and imports, that is, exports and imports of services like banking, insurance, shipping, etc, tourist traffic and so on.
(c) International movements of capital.

Thus, while explaining the concept of balance of payments, the following points should be borne in mind:

(i) Balance of payments is a systematic presentation of all economic transactions which a country has entered into with other countries of the world.
(ii) The transactions include both visible and invisible items of exports and imports.
(iii) It refers to a specific period of time which is generally a year.
(iv) There are two sides of the balance of payments - the credit side and the debit side. Receipts are shown on the credit side and payments on the debit side.
(v) From a purely accounting angle, balance of payments of a country is always in a balance.

# Difference Between Balance of Trade and Balance of Payments
It is necessary to bear in mind the difference between balance of trade and balance of payments. The balance of trade of a country consists only of transactions relating to visible items of exports and imports, that is, physical goods, These are called visible items of trade because they are recorded at the ports and in the returns of the customs department.

On the other hand, the term balance of payments is more comprehensive and wider in scope; it refers to total debits and credits due to all the items on account of which a country makes payments to other countries and receives payments from the other countries. Thus, the term balance of payments includes receipts and payments not only on account of visible items of exports and imports, but also invisible items of exports and imports, capital transfers, interest payments, gifts, grants, etc. Balance of trade, thus, is only a part of the balance of payments. In a way, the balance of trade is simply the difference between the money value of commodity exports and imports. In itself it has no analytical significance. However, the balance of trade is usually the largest - component of the balance of payments. Besides this, the major components of the balance of payments are the payments made to or received from other countries on account of shipping, banking and insurance services, tourists expenses, interest and loans, capital movements, sale or purchase of gold, gifts, repatriation payments, etc.

In short, the balance of payments is a systematic record of all receipts and payments of a country on account of transactions with the other countries of the world during the year. The balance of trade forms only a part of the balance of payments.

# Balance of Payments-Current Account and Capital Account
The balance of payments of a country has two accounts:

(i) Current Account
(ii) Capital Account.

**(i) Current Account:**

The current account balance of payments refers current international transactions which lead to an addition or utilisation of national income. It includes visible exports and imports both on private and government account, invisible items like shipping, banking, insurance charges, tourists expenses, receipts of dividends and interest on investments made abroad, transfer payments like gift, donations, grants, war indemnity, etc.

It should be noted here that the current account balance of payments is a record of current international transactions and a country tries to achieve a balance in its current account. However, if there is a deficit, it is met out of transfer from the capital account.

**(ii) Capital Account:**

Capital account balance of payments indicates long term investments and capital movements as also gold transactions and changes in the currency reserves including changes in the foreign exchange assets with the central bank of the country.

It should also be noted here that it is the balance of payments and not the balance of trade which presents a real picture of the overall economy of a country. A surplus in the balance of trade is not necessarily an indication of a country's prosperity nor a deficit in the balance of trade an indication of a poor state of the country's economy. Thus, for example, the balance of trade of a country may be in surplus, but if it has to make large payments on account of invisible items of imports, the over-all balance of payment may be in a deficit. In fact, such a situation did prevail in India prior to independence. The balance of trade during this period was deliberately kept in surplus by the British government so as to enable us to make various payments on account of invisible items of imports, and so the over-all position was unfavourable. Likewise, a country may be having a deficit in it's balance of trade, but if it receives large payments on account of exports of invisible items, the over all situation may be favourable.

# Balance of Payments Always Balances
In the accounting sense, the balance of payments must always balance. Debit's must be equal to credits if the entries are consistently made. The balance of payments accounts are maintained in the form of double - entry book - keeping. Thus, whenever there is a credit for a country, there is a corresponding debit for the other country and there is debit entry (loan). The credit and debit accounts must be equal in the accounting sense. This is the meaning of the statement; balance of payments always balances. This statement is a truism.

At the same time, it should also be noted here that while a country's international accounts must always balance, its own accounts need not be in equilibrium. Thus, for example, if a country has a debit balance on current account, it means that it is either importing capital on long or short term; or it is exporting gold; or it is receiving donation from foreign countries and there by it's credit in the current account is extended to the extent of debit in the current account. Thus, the normal analytical approach to the balance of payments is to regard it as the difference between receipts from aid payments to foreigners by the residents of the country. It may be expressed symbolically as follows:

$$
B=R-P
$$

Where $B$ is balance of payments, $R$ is receipts from foreigners and $P$ is payments made to foreigners.

A country's balance of payments is said to be in equilibrium when its receipts are equal to payments on account of its transactions with other countries of the world. Such a country is often called a 'country in external balance.'

On the other hand, a country's balance of payments is said to be favourable if its total receipts from the foreign countries exceed the total payments to be made to foreign countries. In other words, when B is positive it is called favourable balance of payments. Such a country is often called as a 'surplus country'.

Likewise, a counrty's balance of payments is said to be unfavourable if its receipts from foreign countries fall short of the payments which it has to make to the foreign countries. In other words, when B is negative, it is called an unfavourable balance of payments. Such a country is often called a 'deficit country'

To put it symbolically,
(i) if $B$ is zero ( 0 ), the balance of payments is in equilibrium.
(ii) if $B$ is positive, $(+)$, the balance of payment is in surplus
(iii) if $B$ is negative, $(-)$, the balance of payment is in deficit.

It may also be pointed out here that the phenomenon of disequilibrium in the balance of payments (surplus or deficit) is viewed from the balance of transactions on the current account. In this sense, a disequiribrium (surplus or a deficit) in the balance of payments shows a strengthening or weakening of a country's external capital position which is measured by the balance of it's external capital assets to liabilities.
