Phases of a Trade Cycle

A typical trade cycle has four main phases :
(1) Expansion phase or prosperity phase
(2) Recession phase - or the upper turning point
(3) Depression phase or contraction phase.
(4) Recovery phase or revival phase - or the lower turning point.

It should, however, be noted here that there is no hard and fast limit for a cycle and its phases. Burns and Mitchell regard the 'peaks' and 'troughs'

as the two main 'mark - off points' of a trade cycle. According to them, there are two major parts of a trade cycle expansion and contraction. The expansion phase starts from revival and includes prosperity and boom. The expansion phase at its beginning is called upswing on the upper ${ }^{\text {point }}$; boom is the peak point of prosperity. The contraction phase includes recession, depression and trough. The trough is the lowest point of a depression.

Let us now discuss the various phases of a trade cycle.

# (1) The Expansion phase or the prosperity phase

The expansion phase of the trade cycle is characterised by increased production, high capital investment in basic industries, expansion of bank credit, rising prices, rising profits and rising employment. There is a general feeling of optimism amongst business men and industrialists. It is the stage of rapid expansion in over all economic activity.

Rising profits and expectations of further rise in profits generate boom conditions in the stock exchange marke:. The mood of optimism and confidence created by high profit expectations reinforces expansion. The highest period of prosperity is called 'boom'. The spirit of confidence is reflected in stock piling by merchants and manufacturers. The commercial banks encourage further addition to fixed capital by liberal lending policies. The continuance of investment even after the stage of full employment results in sharp inflationary rise in prices. This causes undue optimism amongst business men and industrialists who by the lure of rising profits make additional investments in the different sectors of the economy. This adds fuel to the fire and run-away inflation raises its head with prices

soaring high touching a new peak. The tempo of the boom reaches new heights and there is an atmosphere of overoptimism all around. Thus, beginning with a mild price rise, expansion phase grows in a cumulative way until the economy is brought to the high pitch of inflation.

But this expansion phase or boom conditions do not continue indefinitely; the developing boom carries with itself the seeds of self-destruction. Bottlenecks, soon, begin to appear in the various sectors of the economy and a number of limiting forces arise from within the economy which progressively tend to weaken the expansion phase and finally take it over to start business recession. Some of these main forces are as follows:

(a) Rising Costs:

Eventually rents, wages, interest, etc rise faster than prices. The rising costs can be accounted for by the scarcity of factors of production, over-time work, use of less efficient factors, etc. These rising costs make further expansion less attractive.

(b) Inability and Unwillingness of Banks to Advance further Loans:

Continuous rise in prices in the face of rising production is possible only if money supply were to extend steadily. But as banks advance more and more loans, pressure on their reserves accumulates. This makes banks both unable and unwilling to sanction further credit. They raise interest rate, introduce credit rationing and call for the repayment of the past loans.

(c) Declining Marginal Propensity to Consume:

As factors of production get more incomes during expansion and boom period, consumption increases but less than proportionately. An increasing portion of rising incomes is more likely to be saved rather than spent. In other words, a declining marginal propensity to consume means declining aggregate demand. On the other hand, projects started in the beginning result in a substantial increase in the supply of finished goods. Stockpiling of goods, high pressure salesmanship, etc. are strong evidences of the end of expansion and the start of recession, as if coming events cast their shadows before. All these factors conspire to depress the price level and reverse the movement to start depression.

# (2) The Recession Phase

The recession phase marks the end of the expansion phase and beginning of a prolonged phase of economic stagnation or contraction. The over-optimism of prosperity is now replaced by over-pessimism characterised by a feeling of hesitation, doubt and fear. Not only new enterprises and projects are postponed for future, but even those on hand are abandoned, Liquidation in the stock exchange, money and commodity markets are the signs of the advancing recession. There is panic in the

stock market and the equities continue to remain dull. The banks also get panicky; they not only refuse new loans, but are not even ready to maintain their existing loans. There is, thus, contraction of credit which stops business expansion. Orders are cancelled and workers are laid off. Dealers in commodities clear-off their stocks in their anxiety to get out of the market. Many business enterprises fail; new investment stops and unemployment increases. There is rise in liquidity preference and people prefer cash assets to securities. Building activity slackens and unemployment appears in construction and other basic and capital goods industries.

This intial unemployment rapidly spreads in other sectors of the economy through its multiplier effect. A cumulative contractionary process, thus, sets in leading to fall in production and employment and consequently in income, expenditure and prices. If recession continues for long, new investment is reduced to a very low level. Worn out capital goods are not replaced since idle capacity is fast increasing. Thus, recession has a cumulative effect; once a recession starts, it goes on gathering momentum, if unchecked, and turns into a depression. With this, the phase of recession merges into cuntraction.

# (3) Depression Phase or Contraction Phase

During depression there is an over-all reduction in economic activities which are at a low ebb. Depression is characterised by a sharp reduction in production, massive unemployment, falling prices, falling projects, low wages, contraction of bank credit, a high rate of business failures and an atmosphere of all round pressimism and despair. A decline in investment and production is accompanied by a reduction in the volume of employment leading to a low level of consumer demand.

With most prices falling, business profits are reduced to an extremely low level and even turn negative.Investment falls due to lack of confidence amongst businessmen and industrialists. As a result of this, there is a low demand for loanable funds. Banks, during this period, are faced with surplus funds, because no body is willing to take the risk and therefore interest rates tend to be low.

Again, during depression, all construction activity, whether it is building construction or making of machines virtually comes to a standstill. The industries which suffer most during depression are capital goods industries like steel, cement, machine-building, automobiles, etc. Consumer goods industries like food, clothing, etc. are not so much affected. Since costs are sticky and do not fall as rapidly as prices, manufacturers suffer huge financial losses; many of the firms being closed down on account of accumulated losses.

Besides, falling prices are greatly supported by reduction in money supply and reduced velocity of circulation of money. Banks stop fresh lending and call for the loans already given. As in expansion, so also in contracion, price fall is universal, but not uniform. Prices of agricultural products and raw materials fall sharply than the prices of finished manufactured goods. The lowest point of depression is called trough.

In short, depression phase or contraction phase is marked by over all curtailment of aggregate economic activity which reaches at its bottom. It should however, be noted here that depression and prosperity differ in degree rather than in kind. In depression, the economic activity is at its trough, while in prosperity, the economic activity is at its peak.

However, the depression phase described above does not remain a permanent phenomenon. In fact the very forces which made depression so serious work for the revival of the economy. These forces come from within and from very process of depression itself; the finally weaken depression and set in the process of recovery.

# (4) The Recovery Phase or Revival Phase

The recovery phase or the revival phase refers to the lower-turning point at which an economy undergoes changes from depression to prosperity.

It arises from the fact that the accumulated stocks with traders and manufacturers during depression starts getting exhausted with the passage of time. Besides, the propensity to consume cannot become zero even during the lowest point of depression. This raises the demand for stock renewal in case of consumer items of mass use. Likewise, the demand for durable consumer goods which was postponed during depression tends to revive after a certain period of time. Similarly, capital goods like plant, machinery, etc. which become wear and tear out after a certain period of time need to be repaired, replaced and renewed. This cannot be postponed for a very long time.

Hence after a period of time there will be a moderate revival in the demand for durable goods from the side of the consumers and for capital goods from the side of the producers. This will, naturally, call for increase in investment leading to increase in employment, income and effective demand.

Normally, the process of recovery begins with the revival of the demand for capital goods for replacement and renewal of old and worn out machines and other capital equipment. In order to meet this demand, investment and employment in capital goods industries increases. This leads to general increase in effective demand which in turn leads to rise in prices and profits resulting in further in investment, employment and income. This phase of trade cycle is known as the phase of revival.

The recovery phase gets gradually cumulative and once again investment, output, employment and income start looking up. During this period of recovery, the marginal efficiency of capital increases which improves business optimism. The pessimism and despair of the earlierperiod is replaced by an atmosphere of all round cautious optimism.

Besides, rise in security prices also marks the start of revival process. The companies approach the market for new issues of securities. Subscriptions to the securities and equities make it easy to finance the expanding economic activities. Business optimism develops and the banks too increase their lending operations. The wave of recovery once initiated begins to feed upon itself and due to the interaction of multiplier and acceleration, the initial increase in investment causes a further increase in investment, employment, income and effective demand. This gives added force to the process of revival and stimulates over all economic activity further till the revival phase merges with the phase of expansion and prosperity. And with the arrival of expansion, the trade cycle completes its first round and becomes ready to repeat itself again for the second round.

It should, however, be noted here that the above phases of the trade cycle seldom show a regular periodicity nor is the length of each phase always definite, Thus, it is quite likely that a state of fairly stable depression may be followed by a slow recovery as was the case in 1930s. Likewise, the depression may be unstable and recovery very rapid. Similarly at times, prosperity phase may be fairly stable and at other times it may turn out to be lightly unstable.
