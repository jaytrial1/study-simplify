We know that the aggregate effective demand which determines the volume of employment in the economy comprises consumption demand and investment demand. As discussed earlier, the consumption function is more or less a stable phenomenon at least in the short run and therefore, in order to increase the level of employment, more stress has to be laid on investment. In other words, the consumption function or the propensity to consume being stable, as income increases, the gap between income and consumption is widened and this is to be bridged by investment expenditure in order to have a sufficient demand to sustain a high level of employment in the economy. It is in this sense that Keynes regards the investment function as the strategic variable in his theory of employment.

It should, however, be noted here that the investment function is viewed in the context of the marginal efficiency of capital and the rate of interest. Changes in both or any of these variables bring about changes in the volume of investment. Thus, during a given period, investment is a function of the marginal efficiency of capital and the rate of interest. To put it in the form of an equation:

$$
I=f(e, r)
$$

Where, I is investment, e is the marginal efficiency of capital, $r$ is the rate of interest and f denotes 'a function of' or 'depends upon'.

# Determinants of Investment

According to Keynes, the volume of investment undertaken by private entrepreneurs in the economy depends on two factors:
(1) The marginal efficiency of capital
(2) The rate of interest.

Since the rate of interest is sticky and unlikely to change substantially at least in the short run, the marginal efficiency of capital is the principal determinant of investment. We, therefore, now turn towards the discussion of this concept.

# Marginal Efficiency of Capital

Keynes first introduced the term marginal efficiency of capital in 1936 and according to him it is a key determinant of investment. In common parlance, the term marginal efficiency of capital means the expected rate of profit. It refers to the expected rate of return over cost or the expected profitability of a capital asset. Keynes defines the marginal efficiency of capital "as the rate of discount which would make the present

value of the annual returns expected from the capital asset during its life just equal to its supply price."

According to Kurihara, 'marginal efficiency of capital is the ratio between the prospective yield of additional capital assets and their supply price. It may be expressed symbolically as follows:

$$
e=\frac{Q}{P}
$$

where $e$ is the marginal efficiency of capital
$Q$ is the expected yield of a capital asset per unit of time.
$P$ is the supply price of the asset.
The marginal efficiency of capital, thus, depends on two factors:
(i) The prospective yield from the capital asset
(ii) The supply price of this asset.

(i) The prospective yield from the capital asset refers to the amount of annual income which an investor expects to get by selling the output of his investment or capital asset, after deducting the running expenses of obtaining that output during its life time. Thus, the prospective yield of a capital asset is the aggregate return expected from it during its life time.
(ii) The supply price of the capital asset is the price which an investor has to pay to acquire it or the cost of producing it. It refers to the cost of producing a brand new asset of that kind and not the price of existing assets of that type.

In other words, the marginal efficiency of a capital asset is the rate at which the prospective yield expected from one additional unit (marginal unit) of that asset must be discounted if it is just equal to the cost, that is, the supply price of that asset. # Marginal Efficiency of Capital and Volume of Investment

The marginal efficiency of capital is determined by relating the prospective yield of an asset to its supply price. But it should be noted that the marginal efficiency of capital is also affected by the volume of investment. Normally the marginal efficiency of capital of an asset tends to progressively diminish as investment in that asset increases. This is due to two reasons:
(a) The marginal efficiency of capital of an asset, that is, the prospective yields from an asset will fall as more and more units of that asset are produced, just as the price of a commodity falls when more and more units of it are offered for sale in the market. This is because as more and more units of an asset are produced, they will compete against each other to meet consumers demand and in this process, their general earnings or prospective yields will decline.
(b) The supply price of such an asset will rise as more units of this asset are produced. This is due to the rising costs in the industry which produces these assets.

Thus, with an increase in investment, the marginal efficiency of capital tends to decline because of the decline in prospective - yield and the increase in the supply price of the asset. This can be illustrated with this help of a schedule and a curve.

# Marginal Efficiency of Capital and the Rate of Interest

We know that investment depends on the marginal efficiency of capital and the rate of interest. Marginal efficiency of capital is the resultant of the supply price and the prospective yield of the capital asset. The rate of interest, on the other hand, is determined by the demand for and supply of money. On the demand side, the rate of interest is determined by the liquidity preference of the people and on the supply side by the supply of money available in the economy.

Now, since the marginal efficiency of capital is expressed as a rate or ratio, it can be compared directly with the rate of interest. Such comparison is essential because investment in a capital asset depends upon a rational comparison between the supply price of an asset and its demand price.

The supply price of an asset is the sum of prospective yields discounted by the marginal efficiency of capital, while its demand price is the sum of the expected future yields discounted at the current rate of interest.

Thus, by comparing the supply price and the demand price, the following observations may be made as regards invest decisions:
(i) As long as the marginal efficiency of capital is greater than the rate of interest, that is to say demand price is higher than the supply price, the investor will continue to make fresh investments. The effect on investment, will, thus, be favourable.
(ii) When the marginal efficiency of capital is equal to the rate of interest, that is to say, supply price is equal to demand price, the effect on investment will be neutral.
(iii) When the marginal efficiency of capital is less than the rate of interest, that is to say, demand price is less than the supply price, the investor will stop making fresh investments. The effect on investment will, thus, be unfavourable.

# Difference Between Marginal Efficiency of Capital and Marginal Productivity of Capital

It would be useful here to distinguish between marginal efficiency of capital and marginal productivity of capital:
(i) The marginal productivity of capital is related with the increment in value due to the employment of one additional unit of capital, while marginal efficiency of capital is the rate of return over cost.
(ii) Marginal productivity is the increment of value obtained by using an additional quantity of capital in the existing situation; on the other hand marginal efficiency of capital relates to the series of the capital assets.
(iii) Marginal productivity refers to the current productivity capital, while marginal efficiency of capital is the net return over cost. In other words, marginal productivity of capital simply refers to the expected current product, while the marginal efficiency of capital

# Factors Affecting Marginal Efficiency of Capital
The main factors affecting marginal efficiency of capital are as follows:
(1) Business Expectations and Business Confidence:

In general, it can be said that anything that moves "business confidence" will change the marginal efficiency of capital. We know that the marginal efficiency of capital is more volatile than the rate of interest, because prospective yields of capital assets depend upon 'business expectations'. The entrepreneurs while making new investment are often guided by the current state of affairs in the business like, the consumer's demand for their product, behaviour of factor prices, cost of production etc. If business expectations are high and optimistic, the marginal efficiency of capital will be high, but if business expectations are low and if there is an atmosphere of pessimism in business world, the marginal efficiency of capital will be low.
(2) Stock of Capital Goods:

If the existing stock of capital goods is large, the marginal efficiency of capital will be low, because in the first place, due to large capital stock, marginal productivity of capital will be low and secondly, due to large capital stock, production of finished goods will be large. Hence, the market prices of finished goods will be low leading to low prospective yield of capital assets.
(3) Technological Progress:

Technological progress in the form of inventions and innovations tend to raise marginal efficiency of capital.
(4) Development of New Areas:

The opening up of the new areas and new markets increases the marginal efficiency of capital.
(5) New Product:

If the sale prospects of a new product are high and expected revenues are more than the cost, the marginal efficiency of capital will be high.
(6) Population Growth:

A rapidly growing population implies a rapid increase in the demand for all types of goods - consumption goods as well as investment goods. Hence, marginal efficiency of capital will rise in many industries. A declining

population, on the other hand, has the opposite effect on the marginal efficiency of capital.
(7) Government Policy:

If the government imposes heavy tax burden on companies and corporations, the marginal efficiency of capital will be low. Likewise, indirect taxes raise the prices of the products and reduce their market demand; this leads to a fall in the marginal efficiency of capital. On the other hand, if the state provides infrastructural facilities like transport, communication, power, etc. and provides credit, marketing and other facilities at subsidised rates, the marginal efficiency of capital will rise.
(8) Political Climate:

If there is political instability in the country, marginal efficiency of capital will be adversely affected. Likewise the out - break of a revolution or war with some other country also has an adverse effect on the marginal efficiency of capital, On the other hand, a stable government with strong political hold will creates confidence in the business community which has a favourable impact on the marginal efficiency of capital. From the above discussion, it would be convenient to list the factors affecting the marginal efficiency of capital under two heads.
(a) Exogenous factors
(b) Indigenous factors.
(a) Exogenous Factors:

These factors are also known as external factors and affect the marginal efficiency of capital from outside. They include: (i) Innovations (ii) Discovery of new natural resources (iii) Fiscal and monetary policy of the government (iv) Population (v) Foreign trade (vi) Labour movement (vii) Political situation (viii) Natural calamities like earthquakes, floods, famines etc. (ix) Political elections (x) War.

Although these exogenous factors are difficult to control, they remain more or less constant in the short run.
(b) Indigenous Factors:

These factors are also known as internal factors and operate from within the economy. They include:
(i) Activity in the stock-exchange market
(ii) State of consumer's demand
(iii) Factor prices, especially wage rates
(iv) Level of income
(v) Stock of fixed capital

Although these indigenous factors are easier to control, they are liable to change even in the short run.

If these exogenous and indigenous factors raise hopes of profit, investment activity will be stimulated leading to a rise in the marginal efficiency of capital; on the other hand, if they depress profit prospects, investment activity will slow down resulting in a decline in the marginal efficiency of capital.
