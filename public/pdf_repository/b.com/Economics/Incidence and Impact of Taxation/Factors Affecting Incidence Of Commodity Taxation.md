# 3_Factors Affecting Incidence of Commodity Taxation
[Sharing of Tax Incidence Between Sellers and Buyers] :
As said above, the modern theory of tax incidence explains the sharing of tax incidence between the producers or traders or sellers and the buyers (consumers) with the help of value analysis. The tax burden is shifted mostly through an increase in the price of taxed commodity or service. The producer tries to shift on the burden down to the consumer by raising the price of the product. In actual practice, however, the sharing of incidence between the sellers and the buyers is influenced by several factors.

In other words, a number of factors have a bearing on the incidence of commodity taxation of which the important ones are discussed below:
(i) Elasticity of Demand and Supply of a Commodity :

The direct money burden of a tax is distributed between the buyers and sellers in proportion to the elasticity of supply of the commodity taxed to the elasticity of demand for it. Dalton has stated two general propositions in this connection :
"The first is that other things being equal, the more elastic the demand for the object of taxation (that is, commodity), the more will be the incidence of the tax on the seller, and the second is that, 'other things being equal, the more elastic the supply of the object of taxation, the more will be the incidence of the tax on the buyer."

The reason for this phenomenon is that when the demand for the commodity is elastic, the buyer would reduce its consumption as soon as the price is raised by the seller because of the tax imposed on the commodity. Hence, the burden of the tax will fall more on the seller. On the other hand, if the demand for the commodity is inelastic, the buyer would not be able to reduce his demand substantially even though the price goes up by the full amount of the tax. In such a case, the incidence will be more on the buyers.

Similarly, when the supply of acommodity is elastic, with a rise in its price due to the imposition of the tax, the demand may fall, but with it the supply will also be curtailed by the seller and therefore, the seller can easily pass on the burden to the buyer. On the contrary, if the supply of a commodity is inelastic, the incidence of the tax will be more on the seller because he cannot curtail the supply of the product.

In the words of Dalton : "The sellers, in short, try to put the incidence on the buyers by reducing supply and the buyers try to put it on the buyers by reducing demand. The relative ability of the two groups to carry out their objects, with the minimum cost to themselves detemines the result."

From the foregoing discussion, we can make the following observations:
(i) Other things being equal, the incidence will completely be on the seller if the demand for the taxed commodity is perfectly elastic.
(ii) Other things being equal, the incidence will completely be on the buyer, if the supply of the taxed commodity is perfectly elastic.

(iii) Other things being equal, the incidence will completely be on the buyer, if the demand for the commodity is perfectly inelastic.
(iv) Other things being equal, the incidence will completely be on the seller, if the supply is perfectly inelastic.
(v) Other things being equal, the incidence will be equally destributed between the buyer and the seller, if the elasticity of supply is equal to the elasticity of demand. In this case, the price of the taxed commodity will increase by half the amound of the tax.
In general, the incidence of a tax is distributed between the buyer and the seller in the ratio of the elasticity of supply to the elesticity of demand.

(ii) Influence of the Laws of Produciton :

While studying the incidence of a commodity tax, we should also take into consideration the laws to which the production of that commodity is subject to.

The taxing of a commodity tends to raise its price which leads to a fall in its demand and consequently reduction in producion. Now, if the production of that commodity is subject to the law of constant returns or constant cost, the price will go up by the full amount of the tax. It is, of course, true that its demand will fall with a rise in price, but as all units of the commodity are produced at the same cost, whatever the scale of production, the price will increase by not more than the amount of the tax.

However, if the commodity is produced under conditions of diminishing returns or increasing cost, the imposition of a tax will raise its price but not by the full amount of the tax. This is because, with rise in price due to levy of tax, the demand for the commodity will fall and consequently the production shall have to be reduced. But since the commodity is being produced under the law of diminishing returns, the cost of production per unit will decline consequent on the reduction in production and therefore, the price of the commodity will rise by less than the amount of the tax.

And when the commodity is produced under the conditions of increasing returns or diminishing cost, the imposition of a tax will raise in price due to the levy of tax, the demand for the comodity will fall and consequently the production shall have to be reduced. But since the

commodity is being produced under the law of increasing returns, the cost of production per unit will rise consequent on the reducion in production and therefore the price of the commodity will rise by more than the amount of the tax.

Hence, it has been advocated that taxes should be imposed on commodities produced under the conditions of diminishing returns and subsidies and bounties should be given to commodities produced under conditions of increasing returns.

(iii) Amount of Tax Relative to the Price of the Taxed Product : 

If the amount of tax relative to the price of the taxed product is small and negligible, the seller may not like to pass on its burden to the consumer and avoid irritating or displeasing him by recovering the tax from him. However, if the amount of tax is fairly large in relation to the price of the taxed product, the seller will certainly try to pass on the burden to the consumer.

(iv) Nature of the Taxed Product :

The nature of the product also makes a difference. If the price of a product fluctuates frequently, it will be rather easy for the seller to pass on the burden of the tax to the buyers by raising the price of the product. Thus, for example, a tax on a commodity like sugar gets rapidly adjusted and shifted, However, in cases where prices are fixed by contracts and hence remain steady for a certain period it will not be easy to transfer the burden of the tax. For example, house tax cannot be so readily shifted for rent is fixed for a certain period and during that period no change can be made.

(v) Availability of Substitutes :

If the substitutes for the commodity which is taxed are easily available, in that case the seller may not be able to pass on the tax incidence to the buyer. On the contrary, if substitutes for the taxed article are not easily available, the seller will be in a position to shift the tax burden on to the buyer.

(vi) Inflationary and Deflationary Situation :

In times of inflation and boom periods, on the one hand, money incomes and employment show a rising trend, and on the other hand there prevalence a relative shortage of goods and commodities. There is too much money chasing too few goods, and accordingly in such a situation it is easier for the seller to pass on the tax burden of the commodities on to the consumers. In a period of depression, however, with falling employment and income, if the seller tries the shift the tax burden on the the consumer, the demand will fall still further which would affect his profits. In such a situation,

therefore, either he himself will bear the tax burden or will try for backward shifting of the tax.

(vii) Nature of Competition in the Market : 

If there is perfect competition in the market, the incidence of the tax will fall on the buyer in the long run. This is because, under conditions of perfect competition all the firms in the long run must earn normal profits which cover their total cost of which the tax also forms a part. Now, if the firm has itself to bear the tax burden in the long run, it will leave the industruy. This will result in fall in production and supply which will ultimately lead to a rise in price in a marner so as to cover the amount of the tax.

Howerever, in monopoly and monopolistic competition, if the seller is earning super normal profits, he may be prepared to bear a part of the tax incidence by accepting lower profit. If, however, he aims at retaining the level of super normal profits, he will raise the price of the product by reducing output and in that case the tax burden will be shared between the buyers and sellers according to the elasticity of demand and supply.

(viii) Period of Time :

Time period also affects the distribution of tax incidence between the buyers and the sellers. This factor, of course, operates through the elasticity of supply. In the short run, the supply of a commodity is normally inelastic and hence the tax incidence is likely to fall more on the seller, because during short period, the tax tends to stick where it is imposed. In the long run, however, supply tends to become more elastic and hence the seller will succeed in shifting the tax incidence on to the buyers.

(ix) Mobility of Capital :

If capital is perfectly mobile, that is, if it can be easily withdrawn from its present use, the producer can easily shift the tax burden on to the consumer. If, on the other hand, large fixed capital is locked up in present business and the producer cannot withdraw it easily, his position is. correspondingly weakened and he may not be in a position to pass on the tax burden to the consumers. In that case, the probability is that the tax burden will be borne by himself.
