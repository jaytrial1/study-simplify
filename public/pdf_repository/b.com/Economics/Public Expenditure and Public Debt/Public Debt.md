# 4_Public Debt
## Meaning of Public Debt
Public debt refers to borrowing by public authority like the government (central and state), local bodies, public corporations and the like. Normally, public debt arises when the State borrows from within the country or from abroad. Public debt or public borrowing is an important source of revenue to a modern government. Looked at from one point of view, public debt is a part and parcel of public revenue and public expenditure. This is because, when the government incurs debt, that is, when it raises a loaninternally or externally-it gets revenue equal to the amount of loan raised and so from this angle, the loan or debt is a source of revenue for the government on its capital account. Thus, for example, if the government has raised a loan of Rs. 100 crores, it means that it gets a revenue equal to Rs 100 crores which is shown as capital receipts. But it should be noted here that this loan is to be repaid alongwith interest. So, when a provision is made for debt repayment and debt servicing in the budget, it becomes a part of the public expenditure and a charge on the revenue account of the budget. It is in a this sense that public debt, in way, is considered as a part of public revenue and public expenditure.

In modern times, public debt forms a separate part of the study of public finance. This is because, the method of raising public debt, the way in

which it is spent and the mode of its repayment and servicing bear a farre aching influence on the economy of the country.

The classical economists had not fully recognised the importance of public debt as branch of public finance. This was because of their utter belief in the principle of sound finance according to which the State should incur its expenditure within the limits of its revenue. As in private finance, so also in public finance, the government should balance its budget and if possible show some surplus, which was considered as a virtue. They were not in favour of deficit budgeting which they used to view with grave concern.

But in modern times, there has been a radical shift in the attitude towards public debt. The principle of sound finance has been replaced by functional finance According to modern thinking, there cannot be any rigid or dogmatic stand regarding the budgetary policy. Whether the budgets should be balanced, surplus or deficit ones depend upon the prevailing economic situation in the country. Thus, in times of depression, a policy of deficit budgeting and in times of inflation, a policy of surplus budgeting is normally pursued. Public debt, in modern times, is not only a source of revenue for the government, but is considered as an important tool for achieving economic and social objectives. As an integral part of fiscal policy, public debt has come to occupy an important place in sphere of public finance.

## Classification of Public Debt
Public debt is broadly classified as follows :
(1) Internal and external debt.
(2) Productive and unproductive debt
(3) Short term and long term debt
(4) Redeemable and irredeemable debt
(5) Funded and unfunded debt
(6) Voluntary and compulsory debt
(7) Marketable and non-marketable debt
(8) Callable and non-callable debt
(9) Gross and Net-Debt.

We discuss below, in brief the main types of public debt.
(i) Internal Debt and External Debt :

When the government borrows from within the nationals of the country, it is known as internal debt. Thus, in the case of internal debt, loans are subscribed by the citizen's or various institutions of the country. On the other hand external debts are raised in a foreign country. Here the loan is

ilosted and subscribed by the citizens and institutions of a foreign country. In the case of an internal debt, the payment of interest and the principal amount means only a trnasfer or redistribution of national income on purchasing power within the community; the expenditure on this account is simply a trnasfer expenditure. However, in the case of an external debt, the payment of interest etc. means transfer of wealth to the foreigners. In other words, when a foreign debt is contracted, purchasing flows from home to abroad.

It should, however, be noted here that an internal debt may become an external debt if public securities are purchased by foreigners; likewise, if government securities held by foreigners are purchased by the citizens of the debtor country, an external debt may be converted into an internal debt.

(ii) Productive Debt and Unproductive Debt :
When the amount raised through public debt is utilised for the creation of certain assets out of which interest and principal amount can be repaid, it is known as productive debt. Thus, a productive debt is supported and full covered by the possession of assets. When money obtained by borrowing is spent on say irrigation and power projects or in the setting up of certain basic and key industries, it creates assets from the income of which it will be possible to pay interest and the principal amount of the debt. Productive debts, therefore, are not burdensome. Public expenditure on education, health etc. also tends to increase the efficiency of the people in the long run. On the other hand, a public debt which does not result in the creation of any asset is known as an unproductive debt. Expenditure or an avoidable war or on unnecessary imports is unproductive debt. It is also called, `dead weight debt', as it creates no assets to pay off the debt. It may be noted here that the interest on productive debt is paid out of the income from the assets created, while interest on unproductive debt is paid out of the general revenues of the state; it has to be repaid by taxation or fresh borrowing.

(iii) Short-term Debt and Long-term Debt :
The distinction between short-term and long-term debt is not very clear. There is no rigid time-wall between the two. However, it may be said that the debt which is to be repaid in three to six months or one year is termed as short term debt. Thus, for example, in India, treasury bills of 90 days or 180 days are in the nature of short term debt; likewise, ways and means of advances from the Reserve Bank also represent short period debt. These are in the nature of overdrafts on the current account of the government. Short-term debt is also known as 'unfunded' or floating debt'. On the otherhand a debt (internal or external) contracted for a period exceeding

five or seven or ten years is usually called a long term debt. Such debt is also known as 'funded debt', although in funded debt the time for repayment of the principal amount is not fixed. The government at its option can repay it at any time. Long term debt is mostly resorted to for undertaking programmes of economic development. National Savings Certificates of five year duration of Naitonal Bonds of Seven or ten years duration are examples of long term debt.

(iv) Voluntary Debt and Compulsory Debt : 
A voluntary debt is that in which the citizens of the country are not forced or covered to subscribe to the loans raised by the government. Individuals and institutions are free to subscribe to the loans both in the matter of amount and time. Under normal circumstances public debt is by and large voluntary. Thus, for example, in the matter of public loans, small savings, public provident fund etc. people are free to decide as to whether to subscribe to these schemes or not, at what time and in what amount.

However, under an emergency situation like war or a situation of 'run-away inflation' when the government compels the citizens and various institutions to subscribe a part of their income or wealth in public loans, it is generally known as a compulsory debt. Looked at from this point of view, compulsory debt exhibits the characteristics of a tax - which is also a compulsory payment made by the citizens of the country to meet with common expenses of the state. However, there is on basic difference between compulsory debt and a tax. In the case of a tax, the amount raised by the government is not returned to the tax payers, where as in the case of compulsory debt, the amount raised is returend to the subscribed alongwith the interest accrued on it. Thus, for example, in India the compulsory Deposit Scheme introduced in 1963 was an example of compulsory debt. However, this scheme was discontinued after sometime. Likewise, a similar scheme introduced for two years in 1974 was also a form of compulsory debt, though this scheme remained in operation for only one year.

(v) Mrs. Hicks Classification of Public Debt :
Mrs. Hicks has classified public debt into three classes, namely, dead weight debt, passive debt and active debt.
(a) Dead weight debts are incurred in consequence of expenditures which do not increase the productive capacity of the country.
(b) Passive debts are those loans which have been spent for purposes that do not yield a money income or increase the productivity of the country. But these debts are incurred to yield utilities and enjoyments such as public buildings, parks etc.

(c) Active debts are those that yield a money income and add to the productive capacity of the nation.
(vi) Funded Debt and Unfunded or Floating Debt :

There are three different senses in which these terms have been used.
(a) According to Adam Smith, unfunded debts are those debts which have been borrowed by the government, "without assigning or mortgaging any particular fund for the repayment of the debt;" while funded debts are those debts against which particular funds or sources of revenue have been pledged.
But modern writers do not make such distinction between these two classes of debts.
(b) According to modern writers, usually the expression 'unfunded debts' is used to mean those loans which are repayable within a comparatively short period, say one year. On the other hand 'funded debts' are loans repayable after a long period of time. The distinction, however, is not clear, for the expressions `short - period' and 'long period' do not refer to a particular period of time. There may be some who would classify debts having a maturity of three to five years as unfunded debts. Strictly speaking, only debts repayable within a year should be classified as unfunded debts, while debts maturing after a longer period should be regrded as funded debts. Treasury bills usually repayable within a maximum period of three to six months as also ways and means advances are examples of unfunded or floating debts.
(c) It should be noted that in English official language, these terms are used in a special sense. Funded debts are those loans the principal amount of which the government is not under any obligtion to repay at any time and on which the government undertakes to pay interest only. In other words, funded debts are permanent loans like the British "consols". Unfunded debts, on the other hand, are debts whose principal amount will be repaid on some fixed date. Thus, for example in India provident funds, small savings, reserve funds and other deposits come under the category of unfunded or floating debts.
