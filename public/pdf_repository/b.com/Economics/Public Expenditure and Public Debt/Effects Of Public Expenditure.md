# 3_Effects of Public Expenditure
The effects of public expenditure can broadly be studied under three heads:
(1) Effects of Public Expenditure on Production
(2) Effects of Public Expenditure on Distribution
(3) Other Effects of Public Expenditure.

## Effects of Public Expenditure on Production
Dalton has discussed the effects of public expenditure on production under the following three sub-heads :
(a) Effect on the Ability to Work and Save
(b) Effect on the Will to Work and Save
(c) Diversion of Resources between Employments and Localities.
(a) Effect on the Ability to Work and Save : Just as taxation tends to diminish an individual's ability and willingness to work and save if it reduces his efficiency, public expenditure, on the other hand, can increase peoples' ability to work and save. Thus, for example, public expenditure on education, health, houseing and other social security schemes increases the productive efficiency of the people and consequently their ability to work and save. Likewise, when the government incurs expenditure for the developement of agriculture, industry, energy, transport and communications etc, it adds to the productive capacity of the country. Such investments in material and human capital increases the operational efficiency of the people which increases their power to work leading ultimately to more and better production. It must be remembered that knowledge is the most powerful engine of production, which is never subject to the law of diminishing returns. Therefore, money spent on education, research, training, health care, etc increases the capacity to work and save to a significant extent.

Likewise, public expenditure also increases the ability to save and invest. Just as taxation tends to diminish the tax payer's ability to save by reducing his income, so public expenditure tends to increase his ability to save and invest, by causing an increase in the beneficiaries' real income. Thus, public expenditure on eduction, health, houseing etc. results in an increase in the real income of those who derive its benefits. Besides, as seen above, public expenditure increases the productive efficiency of the people and thereby results in an increase in his income leading to an increase in their ability to save.

(b) Effect on the Will to Work and Save : The effect of public expenditure on the will to work and save depends on the character of public expenditure and the policy governing it. Thus, public expenditure on education, health, social security schemes like old age pensions, unemployment benetits, etc. may provide incentive or disincentive on the individual's desire to work and save depending on the beneficiary's elasticity of demand for income. If the beneficiary's demand for income is inelastic, it may depress or weaken his willingness to work hard and save more. The reverse is likely to happen, if his demand for income is elastic.

In other words, the granting of old age pensions, insurance against sickness and unemployment, provision of education and health facilities at State expense, may blunt the edge of the desire to work and save; it may make the people indifferent towards the future and make them neglect saving. This is bound to affect adversely. People will work less and save less because of a feeling of future security. But if such expenditure is kept within proper limits and if it really helps the helpless as also if the benefits are made conditional (for example, contributory provident fund or contributory social security scheme, etc), this will have little adverse effects on the desire to work and save.
(c) Diversion of Resources between Different Employments and Localities : Public expenditure can influence the diversion of productive resources amongst different industries and localities. Thus, for example, if the government desires to attract productive resources to a particular industry, it can start providing economic assistance from its own funds to such an industry. Thus, through a system of subsidies and bounties, the government may succeed in diverting resources to hitherto neglected sectors and set up new industries. Again, private sector being profit-oriented is not likely to enter into in certain fields. The State can and should plan to enter these neglected fields and arrange its public expenditure programme accordingly. Likewise, public expenditure on infrastructure and socioeconomic overheads adds to the productivity capacity of both the public and private sectors.

Similarly, public expenditure may influence the diversion of resources as between different localities and regions. If the government wishes to attract productive resources to a particular area or region of the country, it can offer a variety of incentives to industrialists and entrepreneurs. Likewise, oublic expenditure on infrastructure and socio-economic overheads in underdeveloped regions as also liberal provision for subsidies, grants, rebate and other types of economic assistance helps in attracting private capital investments in these areas. At times, the State itself may set up industrial units and initiate certain developmental programmes in these regions. Thus,

through a suitable public expenditure policy, the government can divert the productive resources towards the neglected areas of the country and ensure balanced regional development.

We may sum up the role of public expenditure in production in the words of Dalton as: "Just as taxation, other things being equal, should reduce production as little as possible, so public expenditure should increase it as much as possible."

## Effects of Public Expenditure on Distribution
Public expenditure can have a very significant influence on the distribution of income within the community. It can help in reducing income inequalities and narrow the gap between the rich and the poor. Just as taxation can improve distribution and reduce inequalities of income by following the principle of "ability to pay", similarly, public expenditure can reduce income disparities by following the maxim of "ability to receive".

Redistributive expenditure can take several forms. Thus, for example:
(a) It may be in the shape of cash grants like old age pensions, sickness, unemployment and accident benefits, etc. These grants provide help to the people at the time when it is most needed, and they have now come to occupy an important place in the expenditure schemes of most of the modern governments.
(b) It may be in the form of free or subsidised services and commodities like education, health care, subsidies on food, houseing, provision of free milk or mid-day meal to school children, etc. are examples of this type of expenditure. The provision of such services to the poor and the needy enables them to live a fuller and better life.
(c) A third, though more difficult step towards bringing about a better distribution of income could be taken, if public expenditure could be adjusted to each individual's or family's needs. Thus, a poor person with a large family is clearly in greater financial difficulties than an equally poor person with a small family. Likewise, a person, who has to support an ailing wife or aged parents, is in a worse economic condition than another person with the same income who does not have such obligations. Many of these difficulties, perhaps, will not arise, if education, medical aid, etc. are supplied free or at subsidised rates. Further help may be provided by old age pensions, widow's pensions, sickness, maternity and accidents benefits. Family allowances based on the number of dependents may help still further in this direction.

The benefits accruing to the poor from the above services may be regarded as a net addition to their income; and more so, when the revenue for financing these schemes is obtained by taxing the rich, the conclusion a inescapable that inequalities of income have been reduced to an extent.

But, here again, much depends on the character of public apenditure and the policy underlying it. Just as there are proportional, progressive and regressive taxes, in the same manner public expenditure in the form of grant or cash assistance may be proportional, progressive or regressive. If public expenditure is really to make the distribution of income more even and fair, it must be progressive. It must be according to ability to receive, corresponding to ability to pay in taxation. Likewise, corresponding to the principle of minimum sacrifice in taxation, there is the principle of maximum benefit in public expenditure. Judged in this context, we can say that expenditure on debt servicing is regressive because it gives more income to the already rich, while on the other hand, granting of old age pensions and benefits of social insurance are progressive. If the government subsidises the production of commodities largely consumed by the poor, it is progressive, otherwise regressive.

Better Distribution Versus More Production :
It should, however, be noted here that an important limitation of attempting a redistribution of income through public expenditure is that it may have an adverse effect on savings; firstly on those who are taxed and secondly on those who receive the benefits of such expenditure.

Thus, it is likely that the provision of free or subsidised education, medical aid, social security schemes may make the people indifferent to save for the future which has been duly taken care of by the State. And if that happens, it will tend to reduce saving. On the other hand, large scale public expenditure to provide these facilities to the poor would require heavy taxation-the burden of which would fall mainly on the rich. It is likely that heavy taxation may produce an adverse effect and discourage savings, investment and enterprise leading to a fall in production, employment and income. And if this is so, redistribution would mean only a 'redistribution of proverty' rather than 'wealth and income' As Colwyn commitee had said, the effects of public expenditure on production seems to be in conflict with that on distribution'. But upto a certain point this need not be the case. The aim of the government's policy should be to strike a balance between the objective of more production and better distribution. Equal distribution does not have much meaning, if there is only a small amount to distribute; similarly, inproved production has no meaning unless its benefits are made available

to all. The issue has to be considered in the context of the principle of maximum social advantage.

On the whole, public expenditure in modern times tends to make the distribution of wealth in the community more equitable. In the words of Dalton, "That system of public expenditure is the best which has the strongest tendency to reduce the inequality of income."

## Other Effects of Public Expenditure
The other effects of public expenditure are as follows :
(a) Effect on Administrative Cost : Just as one of the features of a good tax system is economy, that is, the cost of tax collection should be minimum, similarly public expenditure policies should entail minimum cost of administration. In other words, the administrative cost of implementing public expenditure policies should be minimum. If the cost of administering public expenditure policies is exhorbitant and top heavy, it tantamounts to waste and misuse of resources.
(a) Effect on Employment : Formerly. public expenditure policies were designed to maintain effective demand at a certain level and to prevent rise in unemployment. But in modern times, public expenditure policies are tailored to achieve full employment or near full employment in the economy; the paramount objective is to sustain a high level of employment in the economy.

In the case of developed countries, public expenditure can vitally influence the level of employment and income, and maintain economic stability by eliminating economic fluctuations. Thus, for example, wide spread unemployment during periods of economic depression can be tackled through liberal public expenditure on public works. The increase in public investment results in a much more increase in employment and income through multiplier and acceleration effects. On the other hand, during boon period, the government should reduce public expenditure because this will help save the economy from the adverse effects of inflation.

The problem in a developing economy is, however, different. Here, the low level of employment and income is due not so much to the deficiency of demand as in developed countries during depression, but due to deficiency of capital stock. It is for the State to make up this deficiency through a national policy relating to public expenditure. In developing economies, therefore, public expenditure is mainly directed to promote economic development. The government, through its welldesigned public expenditure policy can make investment in providing infrastructure and socio-economic overheads and for the development of agriculture, industry and other sectors of the economy. At times the State

itself can set up public enterprises in the core sector. All this tends to have a favourable effect on employment and income. Thus, public expenditure in developing economies is an effective instrument for capital formation and plays a vital role in raising the level of employment and income in the country.
(c) Economic Stability and Economic Growth : Public expenditure can also help in maintaining economic stability by eliminating economic fluctuations. Thus, for example, in times of depression, through a liberal public expenditure on public works, it can help to lift the economy out of the jaws of depression. Likewise, in times of inflation, in the first place, public expenditure incurred for increasing the production and supply of consumer goods helps to keep inflationary pressures under control. However, if the economy has reached the level of full employment, public expenditure is likely to aggravate inflationary pressures, and so the government should reduce its expenditure and adopt the policy of surplus budgeting.
