The multiplier concept developed by Keynes goes by the name of investment multiplier. It is derived form the concept of propensity to consume and refers to the effects of changes in the investment outplay on aggregate income through induced consumption expenditures. Thus, the multiplier expresses the relationship between an initial increment in investment and the final increment in aggregate income.

Whenever an investment is made in the economy, it results in increasing the aggregate income not only by the amount of the original investment but by something more than that. The reason for this is that the initial investment increases the income not only in those industries where the investment is made but also in certain other industries whose products are demanded by people employed in investment industries. Thus, for example, if initial investment increases by Rs. 10 crores and the aggregate income rises by Rs. 30 crores, then the multiplier is 3 . In its essence, therefore, the multiplier is the ratio of the change in income to the change in investment. We may put it symbolically as :

$$
\text { multiplier }=\frac{\Delta \mathrm{Y}}{\Delta \mathrm{I}}
$$

Where $\Delta \mathrm{Y}$ is change in investment and $\Delta \mathrm{I}$ is change in income.

# Determination of the Value of Multiplier
According to Keynes, the value of investment multiplier depends directly upon the marginal propensity to consume. It is large if the marginal propensity to consume is large and it is small if the marginal propensity to consume is small. In other words, higher the marginal propensity to consume, higher shall be the value of multiplier and lower the marginal propensity to consume, lower shall be the value of multiplier. The multiplier is, thus, directly related to the marginal propensity to consume and is inversely related to marginal propensity to save. The value of multiplier is, in fact, determined by the marginal propensity to consume, that is, the ratio of increase in consumption to increase in income.
i.e. by $: \frac{\Delta \mathrm{C}}{\Delta \mathrm{Y}}$.

Investment multiplier is, thus, a direct function of the marginal propensity to comsume. The multiplier is equal to the reciprocal of 1 minus the marginal propenstiy to consume. Keynes has given a general formula for the multiplier as follows:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathrm{K}=\frac{1}{1-\frac{\Delta \mathrm{C}}{\Delta \mathrm{Y}}} \\
& \text { or } \mathrm{K}=\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{MPC}}
\end{aligned}
$$

Here K denotes investment multiplier, $\Delta \mathrm{C}$ is change in consumption, $\Delta \mathrm{Y}$ is change in income and MPC is marginal propensity to consume.

Thus, if the marginal propensity to consume is known to us, we can calculate the value of multiplier. Suppose the marginal propensity to consume $3 / 4$ the value of multiplier can be calulated as follows :

$$
K=\frac{1}{1-\frac{3}{4}}=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{4}}=4
$$

The value of multiplier in this example is 4.
It should be noted here that the marginal propensity to consume plus the marginal propensity to save is equal to 1 .

If the marginal propensity to consume is deducted from 1 , we get the marginal propensity to save. The above formula can, therefore, be expressed in the following form :

$$
\mathrm{K}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{MPS}}
$$

where MPS is marginal propensity to save.
Now, if we know the marginal propensity to save, we can calculate the value of multiplier. Let us suppose that the marginal propensity to save is $\frac{1}{10}$, the value of multiplier will then be.

$$
K=\frac{1}{M P S} \text { or } K=\frac{1}{1 / 10}=10
$$

The value of multiplier here is 10 .
From the above discussion, the following two observations can be made:
(1) There is a direct relationship between marginal propensity to consume and the value of multiplier. Higher the marginal propensity to consume, higher shall be the value of multiplier and vice versa.

If the marginal propensity to consume is given, the following formula is used to calculate the value of the multiplier :

$$
K=\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{MPC}}
$$

(2) There is an inverse relationship between marginal propensity to save and the value of multiplier. Higher the marginal propensity to save, lower shall be the value of multiplier and vice versa.

If the marginal propensity to save is given, the following formula is used to calculate the value of the multiplier :

$$
K=\frac{1}{M P S}
$$

In short, the multiplier is the reciprocal of the marginal propensity to save which is equal 1 minus the marginal propensity to save.

# The Process of Income Generation through Multiplier
Let us now study the process of income generation through multiplier As said earlier, multiplier is the ratio of change in income to change in investment. Suppose that in any given period, an additional investment of Rs. 20 crores is undertaken for the construction of a railway line or at irrigation project and the marginal propensity to consume is $3 / 4$ Accordingly, the marginal propensity to save will be $1 / 4$ and the value of multiplier shall be 4 , Now, this additional investment of Rs. 20 crores will create a total income of Rs. 20 crores $\times 4=$ Rs. 80 crores. This income will be generated in two ways as follows :

(a) Primary Generation of Income :

When and additional investment of Rs. 20 crores is made for the construction say of a railway line, the different categories of people employed in this project taken together will earn an income of Rs. 20 crores This is known as primary income.

(b) Secondary Generation of Income :

Now, assuming marginal propensity to consume as $3 / 4$, Rs. 15 crore ( $3 / 4$ of Rs. 20 crores) will be spent by these people on food, clothing an other consumer items. This means that Rs. 15 crores is received as income by businessmen; shopkeepers and others. This logic is based on the fundamental proposition that one man's consumption expenditure is anothe man's income, so that an amount spent on consumption means a furthe amount of income received or generated within the economy. The recepient of Rs. 15 crores in turn spend Rs. 11.25 crores ( $3 / 4$ of Rs. 15 crores) in the second round. Similarly, Rs. 8.44 crores ( $3 / 4$ of Rs. 11.25 crores) will be generated in the third round till finally an aggregate income of Rs. 80 crores is genereated. At this stage, saving out of additional income of Rs. 80 crores is Rs. 20 crores as the marginal propensity to save is $1 / 4$. Here, saving is equal to investment and hence the process of income expansion comes to an end.

Thus, to sum up :
(i) Additional investment $=$ Rs. 20 crores
(ii) Primary income generation $=$ Rs. 20 crores.
(iii) Secondary generation of income $=$ Rs 15 crores + Rs. 11.25 crore

Hence;
Rs. $\left[20+\frac{3}{4}(20)+\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{2} 20\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{3}(20)+\ldots \ldots\right.$.crores. $]$

$$
\begin{aligned}
& =20\left\{\frac{1}{1-\frac{3}{4}}\right\} \\
& =20\left\{\frac{1}{\frac{1}{4}}\right\}=20 \times 4=\text { Rs. } 80 \text { crores. }
\end{aligned}
$$

It will, thus, be seen from the above example that the process of income expansion continues till the total increment in income becomes so large that it generates additional savings which is equal to the initial increase in investment. In the above example, Rs. 20 crores of initial investment generates an aggregate income of Rs. 80 crores. At this stage, saving out of additonal income of Rs. 80 crores is Rs. 20 crores as the marginal propensity to save is $1 / 4$. Thus, at this stage saving equals investment and the process of income propagation comes to an end.

It should, however, be also noted here that this whole process of income generation is spread over a period of time. In the above example, the aggregate income does not increase to Rs. 80 crores all at once and simultaneously, but over a period of time. Thus, for example, if each round

# Assumptions of Multiplier Analysis
Keynes' theory of multiplier is based on the following assumptions:
(1) There is a change in autonomous investment, that is, there is autonomous change in spending, namely, investment, government spending, export and consumption.
(2) Consumption is a function of income and there is no change in the marginal propensity to consume.
(3) It is not enough for the gross investment to be positive; the net investment or net addition to society's stock of capital has also to be positive. In other words, there should be a net increase in investment.
(4) There is continuty in invesment, that is, the various increments in investment should be maintained for the completion of the multiplier process.
(5) There should be a steady availability and flow of consumption goods.
(6) The economy is assumed to be closed one having no trade relations with the rest of the world.
(7) There is no time lag between the receipt of income and expenditure on consumption. An increase in investment is immediately followed by a multiple increase in income.
(8) Taxes are assumed to be lump-sum. If a portion of increased income is taxed away at every stage of income generation, the multiplier effect will be smaller.
(9) There are unemployed resources in the economy, that is to say, there must exist involuntary idle resources including man power. The economy operates at a situation of less than full employment.

# Leakages of Multiplier
The multiplier effect in the process of income generation is not fully realised on account of the following major leakages:

(i) Saving :

If the marginal propensity to consume is equal to 1 and marginal propensity to save is equal to zero, then the entire new income generated as a result of an increase in investement would be spent by the people in buying consumer goods. In such a situation, even a once for all increase in

investment would go on creating extra income till the economy attains the stage of full employment. But in practice, marginal propensity to consume is hardly equal to 1 . In fact, people do not spend the entire increase in income on consumer goods. They save a certain portion of it, and the portion they save (that is, do not spend) peters out or disappears form the circular flow, there by reducing the value of the multiplier. Thus, the higher the marginal propensity to save of the people, the lower shall be the value of the investment multiolier.

(ii) Debt Repayment :

According to James Duesenberry people do not spend their entire income on consumption goods, many a times they use a part of it to pay their past debt. As a result, there is a leakage from the income stream and the value of the multiplier gets reduced.
(iii) Accumulation of Idle Cash Balances :

A part of the increased income may be saved in the form of idle cash balances in the banks. This idle money does not come into circulation and therefore is not likely to lead to an increase in consumption spending.
(iv) Stock-exchange Transactions :

A part of the new income may be used in buying old stocks, securities and bonds from others who in turn do not spend, constitutes another leakage, because such transactions are unlikely to raise society's total consumption appreciably.
(v) Imports :

Money spent in buying imported goods does not add to domestic income and employment. To the extent, we spend a certain portion of our new income on imported goods, money leaks out of the country. In other words, the money value of imports peters out of the income stream, thus limiting the value of the multiplier.
(vi) Price Inflation :

During inflation, money incomes may rise, but real income falls. Thus, real consumption spending which determines the value of the multiplier will fall. In other words, a major part of increase in money income will be neutralised by price inflation instead of stimulating consumption, income and employment.
(vii) Taxes :

If the government takes away a certain portion of the extra income generated in the economy through taxes, the value of the multiplier will fall. So like savings, taxes also act as a leakage from the circular flow. Taxes have a contractionary effect in as much as they reduce real

consumption spending by reducing disposable income. Likewise, commodity taxes tend to raise prices and hence a part of additional expenditure is dissipated on higher prices.

(viii) Corporate Savings :

Business and industrial corporations do not always distribute their entire net profits as dividend amongst share holders. They retain a certain portion of their net profits for expansion, diversification and modernisation. To the extent that they follow the policy of retaining a certain portion of their net profits as savings, it acts as a leakage, because they are not available to the shareholders for being spent on consumer goods. Accordingly, it weakenss the multiplier process.

It is because of these leakages that the process of income generation slows down after some time. If such leakages in the income stream did not exist, the process of income generation would continue till the economy attains the stage of full employment. To the extent to which these leakages could be identified and their severity reduced, the value of the multiplier would increase and the process of income generation would become faster than before.

# Limitations of the Multiplier Theory
The following are the main limitations of the multiplier theory :
(1) The theory of multiplier is static in nature. It assumes that the marginal propensity to consume is constant and hence the value of multiplier is also constant. But in reality, the marginal propensity to consume declines with an increase in income ; the value of multiplier is not constant.
(2) Besides, it is difficult to calculate the aggregate marginal propensity to consume. Of course, it can be calculated for changes in the level of income in the past. But projection of the aggregate consumption function based on past data may not be useful because the behaviour of the people may change.
(3) The multiplier theory, under certain static assumptions, shows the process of income generation from one point of equilibrium to another. It fails to analyse the actual sequence of events and the reckoning of time-lags.
(4) The theory explains the effects of a change in investment on income and consumption, but does not take into account the effects of changes in income and consumption on invesmtent.
(5) Likewise, it considers only the effects of "induced consumption" on income and ignores the effects of "induced consumption" on "induced investment." It fails to appreciate that there is a typical relationship between the demand for capital goods and the demand for consumption goods, that is to say, the demand for capital goods is a derived demand.
(6) The multiplier theory ignores the time element in the process of income generation. It does not say anything about the time period during which additional investment generates multiple expansion of income. There is always a time-lag between the receipt of income and consumption spending and its reappearence as income.
(7) Besides time-lag, there may also be an output lag, that is, if the producers do not immediately increase the output to meet the increase in total demand and allow their stocks of goods to run down, the multiplier effect is partly lost.
(8) The theory of multiplier works only when idle resources are available in the economy. If there is full employment, additional investment will result into an inflationary rise in prices which will neutralise a major portion of the increased money incomes.
(9) If investment does not come up in sufficient quantity, the multiplier will not work. Besides, it is also difficult to know the size of net additional investment. At times, when investment is increased in the public sector, it is reduced in the private sector. Hence, it becomes difficult to compute net additional investment.
(10) And lastly, Keyne's multiplier theory operates in a depression stage of an advanced economy; it however fails to work in the case of an underdeveloped economy. Keynes has assumed excess capacity for the operation of the multiplier effect. But in an underdeveloped economy, ther is no such excess capacity, on the contrary there is the problem of increasing the capacity.

# Significance of the Concept of Multiplier
In spite of certain limitations of the multiplier concept, it has proved to be a useful tool of economic analysis. Keynes theory of investment multiplier is regarded as an important and permanent contribution to economic theory. Its significance will be clear from the following points:
(1) The concept has highlighted the importance of investment as the major dynamic element in the economy. It can be used to make a strong case for public investment, particularly in a period of deep depression and widespread unemployment. This is because a small increase in public expenditure at such a time may lead to a large increase in income, output and employment.

(2) The concept is useful to government in formulating an appropriate employment policy during depression.
(3) The concept has brought into focus the important point that employment is directly created by increasing investment. It reveals that income is generated throughout the economy due to secondary consumption spending which is induced by an initial or primary act of investment.
(4) Besides, a background knowledge of the multiplier is of paramount importance not only in analysing business cycle movements but in formulating an appropriate contra - cyclical policy to achieve economic stability.
(5) The concept of multiplier can be used by the governments and business men to forecast the over all effects of a public expenditure programme. For example, if it is known that Rs. 100 crores are to be spent in building new roads and it is calculated that the aggregate marginal propensity to consume is $3 / 4$, then the total income will rise by Rs. 400 crores and total consumption by Rs. 300 crores. The government can now assess its effects on the economy as whole and especially on the level of employment. Likewise, producers of consumer goods may be able to forecast how the overall rise in demand will affect the demand for its product.
(6) The concept also highlights the fact that the level of investment is a function of the level of income and therefore, the government can use the multiplier to determine the policy needed to achieve full employment.
(7) According to Prof. Samuelson, the multiplier theory explains why an easy money policy is ineffective and a deficit spending is effective.
(8) The concept is also used to explain the phenomenon of expansion in different spheres of economic activities. For example, different concepts of multiplier like the credit multiplier, consumption goods multiplier, balanced budget multiplier, employment multiplier, etc. have been developed in recent years.
Hansen has very aptly described the importance of the multiplier concept when he writes, "But the important thing to note here is the fact that owing to the work of Kahn and Keynes, we are now able to attack the problem of the effect of investment on income with more precise tools of analysis than were formerly available".
