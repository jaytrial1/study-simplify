
The principle of acceleration explains changes in investment due to changes in consumption or income. We saw in Keynes's analysis that an increase in investment leads to an increase in income according to the value of the multiplier, while the principle of acceleration gives us the opposite explanation from the analysis of the multiplier. Acceleration ratio i.e. accelerator shows the relationship of changes in investment to changes in consumption or income.

The French economist Albert Aftalion is prominent among those who introduced this theory. However, the credit for systematizing this theory goes to the American economist J.M. Clark. Also Kuznet, Pigou and Harrod have helped to develop this theory. Harrod called the acceleration as "The Relation". Additionally, economists such as Mitchell, Robertson, Spaithoff, etc. have woven acceleration with other aspects in their theories of the business cycle.

In the theory of multipliers we see how consumption expenditure progressively gives rise to new and new consumption expenditure. But not only does this consumption expenditure create a long series of new consumption expenditure, but also a large increase in the demand for producers' goods to meet the growing demand for consumer goods. A slight increase in the demand for consumer goods leads to a manifold increase in the demand for productive goods like machinery. In this way, the drastic increase in the demand for productive goods due to the general increase in demand for consumer goods is called `acceleration of derived demand'. Similarly, the theory of momentum can also be explained in terms of changes in the stock of a finished product due to changes in demand.

In short, the Principle Of Acceleration presents two main points:
(1) As consumption increases, investment increases not only in the production of consumer goods but also in the production of capital goods,
(2) Investment in production for capital goods is many times higher than for production of consumption goods.

Criticisms of Acceleration Theory:
The main criticisms of this theory are as follows :
(1) If the economy as a whole is at full employment or if the means of production are inelastic, the production of capital goods cannot be increased by an increase in the demand for finished goods.
(2) This theory assumes that the ratio of capital to output remains constant. In reality, this ratio does not remain constant due to technology and also when working in two shifts instead of one shift on the same machine. In other words, the theory of momentum is built on the assumption that there is a constant relationship between the capital equipment and the volume of output. But in reality this relationship is not stable. Production can be increased by making more use of fixed capital equipment, similarly by employing more men on machines or taking more time from them, without increasing the quantity of production equipment.
(3) Certain investments are independent of changes in current demand which we call independent investments. This independent investment is driven by technology and entrepreneurial instincts that are not affected by short-term demand fluctuations.
(4) An increase in the demand for a commodity cannot increase investment as long as there is residual productive capacity in the units of production.
(5) Instead of increasing investment only as demand increases, producers consider how long the increase in demand will last. Thus predictions about the future are very influential in investment decisions. Prof. Hanson states that some types of investments are made based on the needs of the very distant future. For example, the work of laying a railway line in a new region. In this type of investment, there is very little correlation between the ongoing demand for the consumer goods or services and the amount of investment. Here long-term assumptions play an important role.
(6) An important limitation of the momentum theory is that when demand falls sharply, the demand for capital resources necessary

for re-establishment eventually ceases. But the investment cannot go lower than that. Therefore, the acceleration ratio may not work as strongly in a contraction as it does in an expansion of the economy.

Interaction between Multiplier and Acceleration :
In the economy, when both the multiplier and the accelerator affect each other, the national income changes according to their value. In the mathematical analysis of the interaction between the multiplier and the accelerator, Prof. Samuelson's name is prominent. However, Harrod, Hanson, Hicks, etc. authors have explained the business cycle through such interactions.

Here we need to know about two types of investment:
(1) Autonomous investment and (2) Induced investment. Independent investment is determined independently of changes in consumption or income. Private investment is determined by technology development (or government investment). On the other hand, induced investment is determined by changes in consumption (or income).

Thus, autonomous investment affects the economy as an external factor, while induced investment is determined by growth as an internal factor.

Comparison of Multiplier and Acceleration Laws : 

The multiplier and acceleration are two important factors that affect the speed and duration of business cycles in the economy. The multiplier shows how a change in investment affects consumption and income. Whereas the momentum law shows how changes in consumption affect investment or the demand for productive goods. Briefly, the multiplier shows how consumption expenditure depends on investment expenditure, while the momentum law shows how investment depends on consumption expenditure.

As seen above, although both enjoy equal importance in the economy, there is a difference between the two. Multiplier is a process based on instinct, while acceleration depends on technical principle Multiplier depends on the user's preferences and habits, while acceleration depends on the machine lifespan. In multiplier theory we study the effects on income and consumption, while in momentum theory we analyze the effects of income growth on investment.
