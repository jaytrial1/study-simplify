The theory of income and employment occupies a very important place in economic literature. As per our syllabus, we shall discuss in this chapter the Classical and Keynesian approaches to the theory of income and employment. However, it would be useful to understand in the first instance, the precise meaning of the term full employment - a term which has been frequently used in both these approaches.

As we know, maintenance of full employment has come to be regarded as a cardinal objective of the economic and social policy of the State in modern times. Full employment does not literally mean cent-percent employment or no unemployment at all. In fact, the concept of full employment is quite consistent with some amount of seasonal, frictional and structural unemployment. Full employment refers to a situation where the extent of involuntary unemployment is minimum, that is, most of those (if not all) who are able and willing to work at the current wage rates get employment. According to some economists, full employment refers to that state of the economy in which the number of vacant jobs far exceeds the number of unemployed persons. Obviously, such a situation would be clearly inflamatory, because it will tempt the workers to push their wages by taking advantages of their scarcity value. Prof. Paul Einzig describes this situation as over-full employment. According to Sir William Beveridge, full employment means that, "unemployement is reduced to short intervals of standing by, with th certainty that very soon one will be wanted in one's old job again or will wanted in a new job that is within one's power."
