6\. DEMOGRAPHIC BASES:\*

Demographic variables are the most important bases for market segmentation. The buyers are grouped into different market segments on the bases of some demographic variables like sex, age, size of the family, income, occupation etc. Some of these variables are discussed in some details below:

(1) Sex Significant differences are observed in the preferences and buying behaviour between the male and female customers. The sex is a decisive variable for products like cloth, shoes, cosmetics, hair dressing, watches, magazines, bi-cycles. purse, jewellery, umbrella etc. In some cases, however, a clear and sharp segmentation of the market is gradually getting mixed up due to change in fashions. For example, jeans pants are now purchased by both boys and girls both. They are now becoming popular among the girls also. Scooter and motor driving is not limited to males only. In most of the foreign countries, women are as fond of smoking as men. In the U.S.A. 'Eve' brand cigarettes are popular among women, while 'Marlburrow' cigarettes are popular among the men.

Recently, the proportion of women employees is on the increase. The women have now come in the forefront in the field of buying, They like to make purchases independently. Not only this, they have begun to play a decisive role in the purchase of goods meant for the males also. The producers cannot ignore this fact. The employed women give more importance to quality than price. They prefer prepared food, are eager to use time-saving home appliances like cooker and take more interest in cosmetics. On the other hand, average women seem to be more economical. They are price- conscious. The producers are thus required in the modern age to take into account, not just the sex variable, but also the continuous changes in social institutions, customs and traditions while making market segmentations.

(2) Age: Age is also an important criterion for market segmentation. There are marked differences in the wants and preferences of the customers of different ages. Hence the customers of different age-groups are purchasing different types of goods. On the basis of age, buyers can be divided into such groups as children, adolescents, young persons, middle aged persons and old persons. The proportion of children to the total population is high in India and for this group, goods such as toys, readymade garments, chocolates, milk powder etc. are produced on a large scale. The fashionable readymade garments, shoes with attractive design and colour, newly designed motor bikes etc. are made for the young men and women. And vitamin tablets and nutritive medicines are produced to meet the requirements of the grown-ups.

Market segmentation based on age is not rigid, however. For instance, chocolates are meant for infants and adolescents; but Amul and Cadbury chocolates are advertising to attract the young persons and grown-ups nowadays. A particular variety of cloth may be introduced for young boys, but young girls may get attracted to it. Despite this difficulty, the segmentation of market on the basis of age has proved helpful to the producers.

(3) Size of Family: The family size can also be used as a criterion for market segmentation. The buyers can be divided into different groups according to the number of family members such as families with 2 or less members, families with 3 to 4 members and families with 5 or more members. This criterion is more relevant to such products as tooth powder and tooth paste, tonic, hair oil, washing powder and soaps, biscuits and food packets, crockery and refrigerators etc.

(4) Income: Consumers' purchasing power depends on their incomes. Hence from the level of their income, it can be judged which goods they will buy and how much they will buy. The low income group persons spend a large portion of their income on such primary necessities as food and clothing. The goods like refrigerators and television sets are beyond their reach.

The producers have to adjust production to the needs of different income groups. The producer must be aware of the income group to which he wants to serve. For instance, the Reliance company selling 'Vimal' brand cloth, makes cloth which appeals almost to all income groups, while the Raymond company makes cloth which appeals only to the high income group people. Sometimes, a producer may make a product meant only for a specific income group, but gradually he may take up the production of different varieties meant for other income groups. For instance, the Cadbury Company initially concentrated on the production of high quality chocolates which only rich persons could afford. But now it has introduced some low quality chocolates for low income group people also.

Of course, producers must realise that income groups are not rigidly fixed. With economic development in the country, people shift from the low income group to the high income group. It is also necessary to remember that a product meant for high income groups can be sold to low income group people by providing the facility of payments by easy instalments.

(5) Family Life Cycle Family life cycle is a complex variable as it covers such elements as the age of different members of the family, marital status, age of the house wife, current age of the children etc. The purchase behaviour is conditioned by the stage of the family life cycle. It is deeply influenced by the stage of the family life cycle in respect of perishable and durable consumer products. The life cycle differs from one family to another. This fact must be kept in mind while dividing a market into different segments. A family's ups and downs, its mental maturity, change in the age of its members, its economic condition, change in the total number of members in it must be taken into consideration, because each of these variables affects the purchase of a product.

The farmers prefer tractors and tempos generally. It is essential therefore that the producers of each product are able to clearly identify various occupational groups of buyers.

(7) Religion: In countries like India, there are many religious groups of people, each having a distinct set of economic requirements. For instance, the dress style of Hindus is different from that of Muslims. The differences in female dressing are more glaring in these religions. A producer can prepare an effective marketing strategy if he is aware of the types and quantities of goods purchased by different religious communities in different markets.

(8) Education: Customers can be divided into two groups of educated and uneducated on the basis of this variable. The books and magazines are purchased by the educated persons. The literacy ratio is different in different states. This fact is to be kept in mind while formulating marketing strategy in respect of reading materials. This criterion is most relevant in the fields of newspapers and magazine publication.
