# INTRODUCTION
A manager is expected to get things done through others skilfully for the success of the business. Management has been defined as the art of getting things done through others. Unless he is able to motivate people, he cannot achieve his business goals. Motivation refers to those wishes, desires, drives etc. that stimulate or activate a man to do things. A man may be immensely capable of work but it is of no use to his employer if he is not willing to apply this to his work. His technical skill, physical strength, intelligence or mental alertness are useless for the employer if the willingness to work is lacking in him. In this connection, Mr. Clarence Francis; former Chairman of the General Foods Corporation, has rightly remarked, "You can buy a man's time, you can buy a man's physical presence at a given place, but you cannot buy his enthusiasm, initiative or loyalty. You have to earn those things." A manager has to deal with human beings. He has to keep them happy and contented. At the same time, he has to ensure that they work in the interest of business. This requires deep insight into human nature on the part of management. Manager must know what his subordinates can do and what they are unable to do.

For a long time after Industrial Revolution it was believed that production can be increased with the help of better production methods and techniques, better tools and better machines only.

It was Frederick Taylor who drew attention to another type of scientific investigation "the accurate study of the motives which influence man." Hugo Munsterberg, the father of industrial psychology also emphasised the role of psychological aspects of the working conditions leading to increased production. After some years Mary Parker Follett stressed the need to recognise the emotional factors in human beings.

Thus, an ideology in which worker was to be considered as human being and his psychological attitudes were to be assigned priority over technological and organisational matters, began to spread gradually. Some experiments were also conducted in this direction. Among them the experiments conducted at the Hawthorne plant of the Western Electric Company by the Harvard School of Business Administration are quite well-known. These experiments very clearly revealed that economic incentives alone are not enough to motivate the workers, but his psychological needs (such as their urge to work in groups) must also be satisfied.
