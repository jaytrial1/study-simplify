
Before you read
This chapter presents a pictorial account of India's history from 1757 to 1857, using pictures and 'speech bubbles' to illustrate the conditions that led to the First War of Independence in 1857.

1. The Martyrs
A scene at a function in Delhi shows a woman addressing a gathering, holding a document.
Woman: Oh my countrymen! Let your eyes fill with tears, as you recall the sacrifices of India’s martyrs.

2. The Company's conquests (1757-1849)
A map of India in 1765 shows British Territory as a small area and Indian Princes' territories covering most of the subcontinent.
With its superior weapons, the British East India Company was extending its power in 18th century India.

A panel depicts Indian princes in discussion.
Indian princes were short-sighted.
One Prince: That upstart Rajah Bah! Call the English merchants. They will help me to defeat him.
The people had no peace due to such constant fights.

A battle scene shows British soldiers fighting Indian forces.
The rivalries helped the East India Company and it could easily subdue Indian princes one by one.
A portrait of Tipu Sultan appears next to a description.
A far-seeing ruler like the brave Tipu of Mysore fought the British till he died fighting!

A group of Indians are shown in discussion, with varying opinions.
Man 1: Thank God, there is peace in the country now! No more wars and no looting by thugs!
Man 2: It is God who sent the British!
Man 3 (in response to "How did Indians react to these conquests?"): Our destiny is linked with them!
Man 4: The white man has killed or dethroned our kings.
Man 5: Some kings were not good, but after all, they were of this land.
Man 6: Now we have become slaves of foreigners!

3. British Rule (1765-1836)
A group of religious leaders are shown.
Religious leaders preached ideas like untouchability and child marriage.
One Leader: Anyone who crosses the seas loses his religion.
Another scene shows a woman amidst suffering people.
Woman: All the misery in the world is due to women.

A scene shows a British officer and an Indian man.
The truth was that Indians had lost self-respect. The British scorned them.
British Officer: The natives are unworthy of trust, incapable of honesty.
Indian Man: True, your honour, but I am honest.

A farmer speaks to a British tax collector.
Being merchants, the British wanted quick profits, their heavy taxes forced farmers to abandon their fields.
Farmer: But your men are taking all my crop!
Tax Collector: You are still in arrears. If you don't pay next week, I will send you to jail.

A scene shows British officials discussing goods.
Still, the British invented other methods which gave them more profits.
British Official 1: The goods manufactured in England should not have any import duty when brought into India.
British Official 2: A good idea!
The East India Company's laws began to cripple Indian industries.

A scene shows starving Indians.
Inevitably famines followed. Between 1822 and 1836, fifteen lakh Indians died of starvation.
A man sits dejectedly, surrounded by his tools.
The British policies ruined the expert artisans and their business.

4. Ram Mohan Roy (1772-1833)
Ram Mohan Roy, a learned man from Bengal, understood what was wrong with the country.
Ram Mohan Roy: Let us not despise ourselves, our ancient culture is great. And we are capable of greater achievements. We must first reform our society. Superstitions have been ruining us.

Ram Mohan Roy speaks to his wife, Uma.
He told his wife Uma—
Ram Mohan Roy: Cows are of different colours, but the colour of their milk is the same. Different teachers have different opinions but the essence of every religion is the same.

Ram Mohan Roy is shown studying and discussing with others.
He was attracted by science and modern knowledge.
Ram Mohan Roy: Knowledge should be practical and scientific.
He started newspapers but the suspicious British stopped them in 1823.

Ram Mohan Roy speaks to British officials in England.
He crossed the seas and went to England to see what made the British powerful. There he told them—
Ram Mohan Roy: We accept you as rulers, and you must accept us as subjects. But you must remember the responsibility a ruler owes to his subjects.

5. Oppression (1765-1835)
An Indian man is shown in jail.
But the British continued to oppress Indians. In 1818, they had passed Regulation III. Under it, an Indian could be jailed without trial in a court.

Three British officers are shown, appearing pleased.
All the time British officers in India drew big salaries and also made fortunes in private business.

A busy scene depicts Indian laborers and British buildings.
By 1829, Britain was exporting British goods worth seven crore rupees to India.
A portrait of Governor-General Bentinck.
Governor-General Bentinck reported back home—
Governor-General Bentinck: "The bones of cotton weavers are bleaching the plains of India."
A scene shows thin, struggling cotton weavers.
The British prospered on the Company's loot while Indian industries began to die.

6. Dissatisfaction (1835-56)
A British official, Macaulay, speaks to an Indian man.
Education in India was in Persian and Sanskrit. In 1835, an Englishman named Macaulay suggested a change.
Macaulay: We should teach the natives through the English language.
Indian Man: I agree.

A group of Indian intellectuals are shown discussing.
English education produced clerks to whom the British gave petty jobs under them. Incidentally, it also produced a new generation of intellectuals.
Intellectual 1: We must educate our brothers.
Intellectual 2: And try to improve their material conditions.
Intellectual 3: For that we must convey our grievances to the British Parliament.

A map of India in 1856 shows British area covering almost the entire subcontinent, with only small pockets of 'States'.
By 1856, the British had conquered the whole of India.
A group of distressed Indians are shown discussing their plight.
They cared little about the needs of Indians.
Man 1: Our kings have become puppets, and we have lost our old jobs.
Man 2: And lands.
Man 3: They are converting our brothers!
Man 4: You only talk! Do something to drive them out!

7. The Sparks (1855-57)
A chaotic scene depicts the Santhal rebellion, with armed Santhals fighting.
Taxes continued to ruin the peasants. In Bengal, the Santhals who had lost their lands under new land rules, became desperate. In 1855, they rose in rebellion and massacred Europeans and their supporters alike.

Sepoys are shown discussing their grievances.
Discontent was brewing in the East India Company's army too.
Sepoy 1: The white soldier gets huge pay, mansions to live in, servants.
Sepoy 2: While we get a pittance and slow promotions!
Sepoy 3: The Angrez asks us to cross the sea which is against our religion. Who is the topiwala to abolish our age-old customs?
Sepoy 4: We must drive out the Angrez.

A scene shows Indian sepoys being stripped of uniforms and put in irons.
Thousands of other sepoys revolted. They were stripped of their uniforms, humiliated and put in irons.
Mangal Pande is shown attacking a British officer.
Sepoy Mangal Pande attacked the adjutant of his regiment and was executed.

British officers explain to Brahmin soldiers about greased cartridges.
Few Englishmen had cared to understand Indian customs or the people's mind.
British Officer: Oh, proud Brahmin soldiers, do you know that the grease on the bullet you have to bite is made from the fat of cows and pigs?
Brahmin Soldier: What?
Another Brahmin Soldier: The white man has deceived us too!

A man hands a chapati to another in a village.
Soon, chapaties were sent from village to village to tell the people that their emperor would want their services.
Man: Yes, all my village men will be ready.
A lotus flower is shown circulating among Indian soldiers.
Similarly lotus flowers circulated among Indian soldiers.
Soldier: Death to the foreigner!
People gather, showing support.
The masses gave all help and shelter to the patriots.

8. Revolt (1857)
A violent scene depicts sepoys revolting in Meerut.
Then there was a violent outbreak at Meerut.
Sepoys march towards Delhi, shouting slogans.
The sepoys marched to Delhi.
Sepoys: Long live our Emperor Bahadur Shah!

A scene shows the rebellion spreading across the countryside.
The rebellion spread wider.
Indian landlords are shown in discussion.
Many landlords had lost their lands because of the British policies, and they were sore.
Landlord 1: The white man's rule must end!
Landlord 2: Yes, we will help you.

9. The Fight for Freedom (1857)
Begum Hazrat Mahal is shown sitting on a throne.
Many former rulers like Begum Hazrat Mahal of Lucknow were bitter.
Begum Hazrat Mahal: The white man has taken away my kingdom!
They joined the upsurge against the foreigner.

Maulvi Ahmedulla of Faizabad addresses a crowd.
Popular leaders like Maulvi Ahmedulla of Faizabad told the people—
Maulvi Ahmedulla: Rise, brothers, rise! The Angrez is ruining our land!
The people rose everywhere, in Bareilly, Kanpur and Allahabad.

Azimulla Khan speaks to Tatya Tope.
Azimulla Khan: We should have Peshwa Nana Saheb as our leader in this war of independence.
Kunwar Singh is shown in a boat, with a bleeding wrist.
Eighty-year old Kunwar Singh of Bihar received a bullet in his wrist.
Kunwar Singh: Mother Ganga! This is my last offering to you!
A battle scene in North India.
The patriots pounced upon the British and fought pitched battles all over North India.

From Our Freedom Movement, S.D. Sawant
