# What official records do not tell

While official records are a vast source of information, they represent the viewpoint of the British officials. They tell us what the officials thought, what they were interested in, and what they wanted to preserve for the future. These records do not always help us understand what ordinary people in the country felt, how they experienced British rule, or the reasons behind their actions.

To understand the perspectives of other people, historians need to look for alternative sources. These sources are often more difficult to find but exist in plenty. They include:
*   Diaries of people
*   Accounts of pilgrims and travellers
*   Autobiographies of important personalities
*   Popular booklets sold in local markets

With the spread of printing, **newspapers** became a crucial source. Issues were debated publicly, leaders and reformers wrote to spread their ideas, and poets and novelists expressed their feelings.

However, most of these sources were produced by people who were literate. They may not fully represent the experiences of **tribals**, **peasants**, mine workers, or the poor on the streets. Understanding the lives of these groups is more challenging but possible with dedicated effort, as this book will show.

## Source 2

### "Not fit for human consumption"

This source provides an excerpt from a newspaper report in the Hindustan Times from March 22, 1946, describing a police strike in Delhi. Over 2000 policemen refused food to protest low salaries and poor quality food from the Police Lines kitchen. A striking policeman is quoted saying the food was "not fit for human consumption" and that "even cattle would not eat the chappattis and dal."

This newspaper report offers a different perspective than official records might. It highlights the grievances of the police force, giving voice to their complaints about living conditions and pay.
