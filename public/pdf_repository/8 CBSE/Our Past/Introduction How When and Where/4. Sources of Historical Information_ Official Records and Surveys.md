This section examines the primary sources historians use to study the last 250 years of Indian history, focusing on the official records generated by the British administration and the importance they placed on surveys.

# How do We Know?

Historians use various sources to reconstruct the past. For the last 250 years of Indian history, key sources include official records and other documents from the British administration.

## Administration produces records

The British administration placed great importance on the act of writing. They believed that every instruction, plan, policy decision, agreement, and investigation should be clearly written down so it could be properly studied and debated. This led to an administrative culture of memos, notings, and reports.

They also felt it was crucial to preserve all important documents and letters. To this end, they established **record rooms** attached to all administrative institutions, from village level offices to provincial secretariats and law courts. Specialised institutions like **archives** and **museums** were also set up specifically to preserve important records.

Letters and memos from the early 19th century administrative branches can still be found in archives. Notes and reports prepared by district officials, as well as instructions from top officials to provincial administrators, are also preserved.

In the early 19th century, documents were carefully copied and beautifully written by **calligraphists**, specialists in the art of beautiful writing. By the mid-19th century, with the advent of printing, multiple copies of these records were printed as proceedings of government departments.

### Reports to the Home Department

This source provides examples of official reports from 1946 during a mutiny by the Royal Indian Navy.
*   **Bombay:** Army taking over ships/establishments. Royal Navy ships outside harbour.
*   **Karachi:** 301 mutineers arrested, military guard on establishments.
*   **Vizagapatnam:** Position under control, no violence, military guards placed. Some men expected to refuse work.

These reports show the administration's perspective and focus on controlling the situation.

## Surveys become important

The British colonial administration also heavily relied on **surveys**. They believed that to administer a country effectively, they first needed to know it thoroughly.

By the early 19th century, detailed surveys were conducted to map the entire country. **Revenue surveys** were carried out in villages to understand the topography, soil quality, flora, fauna, local histories, and cropping patterns – information considered essential for administering the region.

From the late 19th century, **Census** operations were conducted every ten years, providing detailed records of the population in all provinces, including information on castes, religions, and occupations.

Many other types of surveys were also conducted, such as:
*   Botanical surveys
*   Zoological surveys
*   Archaeological surveys
*   Anthropological surveys
*   Forest surveys

These surveys provided the British with detailed information about India's resources, people, and geography, which helped them manage and control the country.
