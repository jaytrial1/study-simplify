This section explores how historians divide the past into periods, examining the criteria used and the problems associated with different periodisation schemes, particularly James Mill's classification of Indian history.

# Understanding Historical Periodisation

Historians divide the past into different periods to help understand its characteristics and central features. The terms used for **periodisation** (dividing history into periods) are significant because they reflect the historian's ideas about the past and what they see as important changes.

## Which Dates?

The dates we consider important are not inherently significant; they become important because we focus on specific events or individuals. For example, British historians writing about India often focused on the rule of each **Governor-General**, starting with **Warren Hastings** and ending with the last Viceroy, **Lord Mountbatten**. Histories were structured around their activities, policies, and achievements.

This approach makes it difficult to include the experiences and activities of different groups and classes within Indian society. To tell a more complete story that includes the lives of Indians, a different structure and focus are needed. This would mean that the dates associated with the lives of Governor-Generals would become less important, and a new set of dates related to Indian experiences would become more significant.

When writing history, we divide it into chapters to create coherence and a logical flow. This process involves selecting events that fit the narrative we are telling. If the narrative focuses solely on British Governor-Generals, the lives of Indians may not fit or have space. Therefore, a different approach to periodisation is necessary to tell a different kind of history.

## How do we periodise?

In 1817, **James Mill**, a Scottish economist and philosopher, published *A History of British India*, dividing Indian history into three periods: **Hindu**, **Muslim**, and **British**. This periodisation became widely accepted but has significant problems.

Mill believed that Asian societies were less civilised than Europe. He argued that before the British, India was ruled by Hindu and Muslim rulers who were despotic, and social life was dominated by religious intolerance, caste taboos, and superstitions. Mill felt that British rule was necessary to civilise India by introducing European manners, arts, institutions, and laws. He even suggested the British should conquer all of India for the enlightenment and happiness of its people, as he believed India was incapable of progress without British help.

This view portrays British rule as representing progress and civilisation, while the period before is seen as one of darkness. This perspective is problematic and cannot be accepted today.

Furthermore, characterising historical periods solely by the religion of the rulers is inaccurate and misleading. During the "Hindu" and "Muslim" periods, various faiths coexisted. This classification ignores the lives and practices of people who did not share the ruler's faith. Also, rulers in ancient India did not all belong to the same faith.

Moving away from Mill's classification, historians commonly divide Indian history into **'ancient'**, **'medieval'**, and **'modern'**. This Western-borrowed periodisation also has issues. In the West, the 'modern' period is associated with progress, science, reason, democracy, liberty, and equality. The 'medieval' period was seen as lacking these features. Applying this uncritically to India under British rule is problematic because, as this book will show, people did not have equality, freedom, or liberty, and the period was not one of uniform economic growth and progress for everyone.

Many historians therefore prefer to refer to the period of British rule in India as **'colonial'**.
