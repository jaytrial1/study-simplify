# Let's imagine

Imagine that you are a historian wanting to find out about how agriculture changed in a remote tribal area after independence. List the different ways in which you would find information on this.
> **Answer:** To find information on how agriculture changed in a remote tribal area after independence, a historian could:
> *   Look for government records related to agriculture, land reforms, and tribal welfare schemes in that region.
> *   Search for reports from surveys conducted by government or non-governmental organisations on agriculture, land use, and tribal communities.
> *   Find accounts from travellers, anthropologists, or researchers who visited the area.
> *   Look for local histories or writings produced by people from the area, if available.
> *   Conduct interviews with older residents of the area (oral history) to learn about their experiences and memories of changes in farming practices and livelihoods.
> *   Examine old maps, photographs, or illustrations related to the area and its agriculture.

## Let's discuss

2. What is the problem with the periodisation of Indian history that James Mill offers?
> **Answer:** James Mill's periodisation is problematic because:
> *   It characterises entire periods based solely on the religion of the ruling power (Hindu or Muslim), ignoring the fact that multiple faiths coexisted and the lives of people of other religions.
> *   It presents a biased view of India before British rule as a period of darkness, despotism, and superstition, implying that only British rule could bring progress and civilisation.
> *   It does not accurately reflect the complex social, economic, and cultural changes that occurred during these periods.

3. Why did the British preserve official documents?
> **Answer:** The British preserved official documents because:
> *   They believed that writing down every instruction, plan, policy, and agreement was essential for proper study and debate.
> *   They felt that all important documents and letters needed to be carefully preserved for future reference and record-keeping.
> *   Preserving records helped maintain an administrative culture of memos, notings, and reports, ensuring accountability and continuity in governance.
> *   These records served as a source of information for administering the country and understanding past decisions and events.

4. How will the information historians get from old newspapers be different from that found in police reports?
> **Answer:** Information from old newspapers will likely be different from police reports in several ways:
> *   **Perspective:** Newspapers often represent a wider range of perspectives, including public opinion, views of leaders or reformers, and reports from various sources, whereas police reports primarily reflect the official view and focus on maintaining order and documenting events from the administration's standpoint.
> *   **Detail:** Newspapers might provide more narrative detail, context, and human interest stories, sometimes including quotes or interviews, while police reports tend to be concise, factual summaries focused on legal or administrative aspects.
> *   **Scope:** Newspapers cover a broader range of topics debated in public, including social, economic, and political issues, while police reports are limited to matters related to law and order, crime, or specific incidents requiring police intervention.
> *   **Bias:** Both sources have biases, but they differ. Police reports have an official bias towards justifying actions and maintaining control. Newspapers can have biases depending on the editor, owner, or political leanings, but they may also offer critical views or report on public grievances not highlighted in official records.

## Let's Do

5. Can you think of examples of surveys in your world today? Think about how toy companies get information about what young people enjoy playing with or how the government finds out about the number of young people in school. What can a historian derive from such surveys?
> **Answer:** Examples of surveys today include:
> *   **Market research surveys:** Companies ask consumers (like young people) about their preferences, habits, and opinions on products (like toys) to understand what is popular and why.
> *   **Census:** The government conducts a national census to count the population and collect data on demographics, education, occupation, etc. This helps them understand the number of young people in school, among other things.
> *   **Opinion polls:** Surveys conducted by media or research groups to gauge public opinion on various issues.
> *   **School surveys:** Schools might survey students about their interests, study habits, or satisfaction levels.
>
> A historian studying the future could derive several things from such surveys:
> *   **Social trends:** Understand what was important to people at a specific time (e.g., changing tastes in toys, educational priorities).
> *   **Demographic information:** Get data on population distribution, age groups, education levels, and occupations, which is crucial for understanding society.
> *   **Economic conditions:** Infer economic activity from market research or employment data from census surveys.
> *   **Government priorities:** Understand what information the government considered important to collect (reflected in census questions or types of surveys conducted).
> *   **Public opinion:** Gauge the attitudes and beliefs of different groups within society on various issues.
> *   **Daily life:** Learn about aspects of everyday life, consumption patterns, and leisure activities from market surveys.
