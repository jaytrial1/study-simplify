This section details the different ways tribal groups lived in the nineteenth century, including shifting cultivation, hunting and gathering, herding, and settled agriculture.

# How did Tribal Groups Live?

By the nineteenth century, tribal people across India engaged in various activities.

## Some were jhum cultivators

Some practiced **jhum cultivation**, also known as **shifting cultivation**. This involved cultivating small patches of land, usually in forests. Cultivators would cut treetops to let sunlight in, burn vegetation to clear the land, and spread the ash (containing potash) to fertilise the soil. They used axes to cut trees and hoes to prepare the soil, scattering seeds (broadcasting) instead of ploughing. After harvest, they moved to another field, leaving the cultivated field fallow for several years.

Shifting cultivation was common in the hilly and forested areas of north-east and central India. The lives of these tribals depended on free movement in forests and access to land for cultivation.

**Bewar** - A term used in Madhya Pradesh for shifting cultivation.

## Some were hunters and gatherers

Many tribal groups survived by hunting animals and gathering forest produce, seeing forests as vital for survival. The **Khonds** of Orissa were one such community. They hunted collectively and shared the meat. They ate fruits and roots, and cooked with oil from sal and mahua seeds. They used forest plants for medicine and sold forest produce. Local weavers and leather workers bought kusum and palash flowers from them for dyeing.

These forest people obtained grains by exchanging forest produce or buying goods with small earnings. Some did odd jobs like carrying loads or building roads, or worked in peasants' fields. When forest produce decreased, they had to seek work as labourers more often. However, some, like the **Baigas** of central India, were reluctant to work for others, considering themselves people of the forest who should live only on its produce. Becoming a labourer was seen as beneath their dignity.

Tribal groups often needed to buy and sell, leading to dependence on traders and moneylenders. Traders sold goods at high prices, and moneylenders gave loans with high interest rates. This often resulted in debt and poverty. Thus, tribals viewed moneylenders and traders as **evil outsiders** causing their misery.

## Some herded animals

Many tribal groups were **pastoralists**, living by herding and rearing animals like cattle or sheep, moving seasonally as needed for pasture. Examples include the **Van Gujjars** of the Punjab hills and **Labadis** of Andhra Pradesh (cattle herders), **Gaddis** of Kulu (shepherds), and **Bakarwals** of Kashmir (goat rearers).

### A time to hunt, a time to sow, a time to move to a new field

Societies have different notions of work and time. The lives of shifting cultivators and hunters were often regulated by a calendar and a division of tasks between men and women. Verrier Elwin, an anthropologist who lived among the Baigas and Khonds, described their calendar:

*   **Chait:** Women cleared harvested stalks; men cut large trees and went on ritual hunts from the east at full moon using bamboo traps. Women gathered fruits (sago, tamarind, mushroom). Baiga women specifically gathered roots (*kanda*) and mahua seeds. Baigas were known as the best hunters in Central India.
*   **Baisakh:** Forests were burned; women gathered unburnt wood. Men continued hunting closer to villages.
*   **Jeth:** Sowing took place, and hunting continued.
*   **Asadh to Bhadon:** Men worked in the fields.
*   **Kuar:** First fruits of beans ripened.
*   **Kartik:** Kutki crop ripened.
*   **Aghan:** All crops were ready.
*   **Pus:** Winnowing took place. This was also a time for dances and marriages.
*   **Magh:** Shifts were made to new *bewars* (shifting cultivation fields); hunting-gathering was the main activity.

This cycle was for the first year. In the second year, with fewer crops to sow and harvest, there was more time for hunting, and men lived in the *bewars*. Only in the third year was the diet supplemented with forest products again.

## Some took to settled cultivation

Before the nineteenth century, some tribal groups began settling down and cultivating land in one place permanently, using the plough. They gradually gained rights over their land. For groups like the **Mundas** of Chottanagpur, the land often belonged to the entire clan, seen as descendants of the original settlers who cleared the land. While all had rights, some individuals gained more power, becoming chiefs and renting out land instead of cultivating it themselves.

British officials preferred settled tribal groups like the **Gonds** and **Santhals**, considering them more "civilised" than hunter-gatherers or shifting cultivators, who were seen as "wild and savage" and needing to be settled and civilised.
