## A Closer Look

Throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, tribal groups across India rebelled against changes in laws, restrictions on their practices, new taxes, and exploitation by traders and moneylenders. Notable revolts included:
*   The Kols (1831-32)
*   The Santhals (1855)
*   The Bastar Rebellion (1910)
*   The Warli Revolt (1940)

The movement led by Birsa was one such significant rebellion.

## Birsa Munda

Birsa was born in the mid-1870s into a poor family. He grew up around the forests of Bohonda, grazing sheep, playing the flute, and dancing. His father moved for work due to poverty. As a youth, Birsa heard stories of past Munda uprisings and leaders urging revolt. They spoke of a **golden age** when Mundas were free from *dikus* and their ancestral rights would be restored. They saw themselves as descendants of the original settlers, fighting for their land (*mulk ki larai*) to regain their kingdom.

Birsa attended a local missionary school, hearing sermons that Mundas could reach the Kingdom of Heaven and regain rights by becoming good Christians and giving up "bad practices". He also spent time with a prominent **Vaishnav** preacher, adopting the sacred thread and valuing purity and piety.

Birsa was influenced by these ideas. His movement aimed to reform tribal society, urging Mundas to stop drinking liquor, clean villages, and abandon witchcraft and sorcery. Crucially, Birsa also turned against missionaries and landlords, seeing them as outside forces destroying the Munda way of life.

In 1895, Birsa called his followers to recover their glorious past – a **satyug** (age of truth) – when Mundas lived well, built embankments, used natural springs, planted trees and orchards, cultivated land, and lived honestly without harming each other. Birsa wanted people to return to working on their land, settle down, and cultivate.

The British were most concerned by the movement's political goal: to expel missionaries, moneylenders, landlords, and the government, establishing a **Munda Raj** with Birsa as leader. These forces were identified as the cause of Munda suffering. British land policies were destroying their traditional system, outsiders were taking land, and missionaries criticised their culture.

As the movement grew, British officials arrested Birsa in 1895, convicting him of rioting and jailing him for two years.

Released in 1897, Birsa toured villages, gathering support. He used traditional symbols and language to inspire people, urging them to destroy "Ravana" (*dikus* and Europeans) and establish his kingdom. Followers attacked symbols of outsider power: police stations and churches, and raided property of moneylenders and zamindars. They raised a white flag, symbolising Birsa Raj.

Birsa died of cholera in 1900, and the movement faded. However, it was significant:
1.  It forced the colonial government to introduce laws making it harder for *dikus* to take tribal land.
2.  It showed tribals' capacity to protest injustice and express anger against colonial rule in their unique ways, using their own rituals and symbols.
