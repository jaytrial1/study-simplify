## Let's imagine

Imagine you are a jhum cultivator living in a forest village in the nineteenth century. You have just been told that the land you were born on no longer belongs to you. In a meeting with British officials you try to explain the kinds of problems you face. What would you say?

> **Answer:** I would explain that our way of life depends on the forest. We move to new patches each year so the soil can rest and the forest can regrow. We get our food, medicines, and materials from the forest. This land is not just dirt; it is our home, our history, and our future. We don't own it individually; it belongs to our community, our ancestors cleared it. If we cannot move, cultivate, and gather, we cannot survive. Ploughing one field year after year will ruin the soil, and we will starve. Taking our land and restricting the forest is taking away our very existence.

## Let's discuss

3. What problems did shifting cultivators face under British rule?
> **Answer:** Shifting cultivators faced several problems: they were forced to settle down and adopt plough cultivation, which was unsuitable for their land and climate; they were restricted from moving freely and using forest resources (like collecting fruits, hunting, and practicing jhum) in Reserved Forests; and they were forced to provide labour for the Forest Department to gain limited access to land.

4. How did the powers of tribal chiefs change under colonial rule?
> **Answer:** Under colonial rule, tribal chiefs lost much of their administrative power and authority. While they were allowed to keep their land titles and collect rent, they were forced to follow British laws, pay tribute to the British, and discipline their own people on behalf of the colonial government. This weakened their traditional position among their people.

5. What accounts for the anger of the tribals against the dikus?
> **Answer:** Tribals were angry with the *dikus* (outsiders like missionaries, moneylenders, and landlords) because these groups exploited them economically (high interest on loans, low prices for produce, high prices for goods), took over their land, interfered with their traditional customs and way of life, and were seen as destroying their culture and causing their suffering and poverty.

6. What was Birsa's vision of a golden age? Why do you think such a vision appealed to the people of the region?
> **Answer:** Birsa's vision of a golden age (*satyug*) was a past time when the Mundas lived a good life, cultivated their own land, managed natural resources (built embankments, used springs, planted trees), lived honestly, and were free from the oppression of outsiders. This vision appealed to the people because it offered a hopeful alternative to their current suffering under colonial rule and exploitation. It reminded them of a time when they were independent, self-sufficient, and in control of their lives and land, providing a powerful goal to fight for and restore.
