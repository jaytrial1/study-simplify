# Tribals, Dikus and the Vision of a Golden Age

In 1895, a man named **Birsa** was seen roaming the forests and villages of Chottanagpur in Jharkhand. People believed he had miraculous powers, able to cure diseases and multiply grain. Birsa himself declared he was appointed by God to save his people from trouble and free them from the slavery of *dikus* (outsiders). Thousands followed him, seeing him as *bhagwan* (God) who would solve their problems.

Birsa belonged to the **Munda** tribe in Chottanagpur, but his followers also included **Santhals** and **Oraons**. These groups were unhappy with the changes and problems brought by British rule. Their traditional ways of life, livelihoods, and religion seemed threatened.

This chapter explores the problems Birsa aimed to solve, identifies the *dikus* and their impact, and examines how tribal lives changed under the British.

Tribal societies often had customs and rituals different from those of Brahmans and lacked the sharp social divisions of caste societies. Members of the same tribe felt a common bond of kinship, though social and economic differences could exist within tribes.
