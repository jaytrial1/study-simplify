# The Khurda Uprising: A Case Study
Long before 1857, another significant uprising occurred in Khurda in 1817, demonstrating the building resentment against British colonial policies since the early 19th century.

Khurda, a small kingdom in south-eastern Orissa built in the late 16th century, was a populated and cultivated area with many villages and administrative units (*garhs*) in the early 19th century. Its king, Raja Birakishore Dev, had been forced by the Marathas to give up four administrative divisions (*parganas*), control of the Jagannath Temple, and administration of fourteen princely states (*garjats*). His successor, Mukunda Dev II, was unhappy with this loss. He tried to negotiate with the British during their conflict with the Marathas to get back his lost territories and temple rights.

After occupying Orissa in 1803, the British did not fulfil his requests. In alliance with other chiefs and with secret Maratha support, Mukunda Dev II tried to assert his rights by force. This led to his removal and the British annexation of his territories. As a consolation, he was only given management rights over the Jagannath Temple with a grant that was just one-tenth of the revenue from his former estate, and his residence was fixed at Puri.

This unfair settlement began an era of oppressive foreign rule in Orissa, leading to a serious armed uprising in 1817.

Soon after taking over Khurda, the British resumed service tenures, severely affecting the *Paiks*, the state's ex-militia. This was worsened by an unreasonable increase in revenue demands and harsh collection methods. Many people left Khurda between 1805 and 1817. Despite this, the British made a series of short-term revenue settlements, increasing demands each time, ignoring the land's capacity to produce or the farmers' ability to pay. No leniency was shown even during natural disasters, which were common in Orissa. Instead, lands of those who failed to pay were sold to corrupt revenue officials or speculators from Bengal.

Jagabandhu Bidyadhar Mahapatra Bhramarabar Rai, popularly known as Buxi Jagabandhu, the hereditary military commander of the deposed king, was one of the dispossessed landholders. He became a beggar and lived on public contributions for two years before deciding to fight for the people's grievances and his own.

Additional grievances included:
*   Introduction of the *sicca* rupee (silver currency).
*   Insistence on paying revenue in the new currency.
*   Dramatic rise in foodstuff and salt prices. Salt became expensive because the British salt monopoly deprived traditional salt makers in Orissa of their livelihood.
*   Auction of local estates in Calcutta, bringing in absentee landlords from Bengal.
*   An insensitive and corrupt police system.

These factors worsened the situation, leading to the uprising.

The uprising began on 29 March 1817 when the Paiks attacked the police station and government offices at Banpur, killing over a hundred men and taking government money. The revolt spread, with Khurda as its centre. Zamindars and farmers enthusiastically joined the Paiks. Those who didn't were punished. A 'no-rent campaign' also started.

The British failed to dislodge the Paiks from their positions. On 14 April 1817, Buxi Jagabandhu, leading five to ten thousand Paiks and Kandh tribesmen, seized Puri and declared the hesitant King Mukunda Dev II as their ruler. The priests of the Jagannath Temple fully supported the Paiks.

Seeing the situation out of control, the British imposed Martial Law. The King and his son were quickly captured and sent to prison in Cuttack. The Buxi and his associate, Krushna Chandra Bhramarabar Rai, tried to cut off communications between Cuttack and Khurda as the uprising spread south and northwest. The British sent Major-General Martindell to clear the area and offered rewards for the arrest of Buxi Jagabandhu and his associates. Hundreds of Paiks were killed, many fled to jungles, and some returned home under an amnesty. By May 1817, the uprising was mostly contained within Khurda.

However, Buxi Jagabandhu sustained the revolt outside Khurda with the help of supporters like the Raja of Kujung and the loyal Paiks until his surrender in May 1825. Afterward, the British adopted a policy of 'leniency, indulgence and forbearance' towards the people of Khurda. The price of salt was reduced, and reforms were made to the police and justice systems. Corrupt revenue officials were dismissed, and former landholders regained their lands. The King's son, Ram Chandra Dev III, was allowed to move to Puri and manage the Jagannath Temple with a grant of twenty-four thousand rupees.

In summary, the Khurda Uprising was the first major popular anti-British armed uprising in Orissa, significantly impacting future British administration there. Calling it just a 'Paik Rebellion' is an understatement.
