# From Meerut to Delhi
The spark of the revolt began with the sepoys' refusal to use the new cartridges and quickly spread from Meerut to Delhi.

On 8 April 1857, a young soldier named Mangal Pandey was hanged in Barrackpore for attacking his officers. A few days later, on 9 May 1857, some sepoys in Meerut refused to use the new cartridges, suspected of being coated with cow and pig fat. Eighty-five sepoys were dismissed and sentenced to ten years in jail.

The response of the other Indian soldiers in Meerut was remarkable. On 10 May, they marched to the jail, released the imprisoned sepoys, attacked and killed British officers, seized weapons, burned British buildings, and declared war on the *firangis*. The soldiers were determined to end British rule and decided that the Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar should rule instead.

The sepoys from Meerut rode through the night and reached Delhi the next morning. When the news spread, the regiments in Delhi also rebelled, killing British officers, seizing weapons, and setting buildings on fire. The soldiers went to the Red Fort, where the Emperor lived, demanding to meet him. Although the ageing Emperor was hesitant to challenge the British, the soldiers insisted, forced their way in, and proclaimed Bahadur Shah Zafar as their leader.

The Emperor accepted their demand and wrote letters to all the chiefs and rulers, urging them to form a confederacy to fight the British. This action had major consequences. The Mughal dynasty had historically ruled a large part of India, and many smaller rulers controlled territories on their behalf. Threatened by British expansion, many felt that if the Mughal Emperor was restored, they could regain control of their own territories under his authority.

The British had not expected this. They thought the cartridge issue would resolve itself. But Bahadur Shah Zafar's support dramatically changed the situation. Seeing an alternative possibility inspired people, giving them courage, hope, and confidence to act.
