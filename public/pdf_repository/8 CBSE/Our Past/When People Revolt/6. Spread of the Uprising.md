# The Uprising Spreads
After the British were driven out of Delhi, the rebellion gained momentum and spread across northern India, attracting various leaders and communities.

After the British left Delhi, there was a brief lull for about a week as news travelled. Then, mutinies erupted across the region.

Regiment after regiment rebelled and joined other troops in key locations like Delhi, Kanpur, and Lucknow. Following the sepoys, people in towns and villages also rose up, gathering around local leaders, zamindars, and chiefs who were ready to fight the British and establish their own authority.

Near Kanpur, Nana Saheb, the adopted son of the late Peshwa Baji Rao, raised an army and expelled the British from the city, proclaiming himself Peshwa and declaring himself a governor under Emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar. In Lucknow, Birjis Qadr, the son of the deposed Nawab Wajid Ali Shah, was made the new Nawab and also acknowledged Bahadur Shah Zafar's authority. His mother, Begum Hazrat Mahal, actively helped organise the uprising.

In Jhansi, Rani Lakshmibai joined the rebel sepoys and fought the British alongside Tantia Tope, Nana Saheb's general. In the Mandla region of Madhya Pradesh, Rani Avantibai Lodhi of Ramgarh led an army of four thousand against the British who had taken over her state's administration.

The British forces were often outnumbered and defeated in many battles. This convinced people that British rule was collapsing and encouraged them to join the uprising. A widespread popular uprising was particularly strong in Awadh. A telegram from Lieutenant Colonel Tytler on 6 August 1857 expressed the British fear: "Our men are cowed by the numbers opposed to them and the endless fighting. Every village is held against us, the zamindars have risen to oppose us."

Many new leaders emerged. Ahmadullah Shah, a maulvi from Faizabad, predicted the end of British rule and gathered a large force of supporters to fight in Lucknow. In Delhi, many *ghazis* (religious warriors) gathered to fight the British. Bakht Khan, a soldier from Bareilly, led a large force to Delhi and became a key military leader. In Bihar, an old zamindar, Kunwar Singh, joined the sepoys and fought the British for months. Leaders and fighters from across the land joined the fight.

## Activity
1. Why did the Mughal emperor agree to support the rebels?
> **Answer:** The Mughal Emperor agreed to support the rebels because the sepoys forced him to and proclaimed him their leader. He likely also saw an opportunity to restore Mughal authority and unite Indian rulers against the expanding British power, which threatened his own position and that of other Indian states.
2. Write a paragraph on the assessment he may have made before accepting the offer of the sepoys.
> **Answer:** Before accepting, Bahadur Shah Zafar likely assessed the strength and determination of the rebel sepoys who had just captured Delhi. He would have considered his own weakened position and the potential threat they posed if he refused. He might have also weighed the possibility of regaining some of his lost prestige and power by becoming the symbolic leader of a widespread anti-British movement, hoping that other Indian rulers would rally behind him.
