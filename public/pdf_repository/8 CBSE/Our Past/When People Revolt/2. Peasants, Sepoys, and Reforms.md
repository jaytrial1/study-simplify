# Peasants, Sepoys, and Responses to Reforms
Discontent grew among peasants and soldiers due to British policies, while the British also introduced social reforms that many Indians viewed with suspicion.

## The Peasants and the Sepoys
In the countryside, peasants and zamindars were unhappy with high taxes and strict revenue collection methods. Many lost their lands because they could not repay loans.

Indian sepoys in the Company's army also had reasons to be unhappy. They complained about their pay, allowances, and service conditions. Some new rules offended their religious feelings. For instance, in 1824, when asked to go to Burma by sea, many sepoys refused because they believed crossing the sea would make them lose their religion and caste. They were severely punished. To prevent this refusal in the future, a new law was passed in 1856 requiring new recruits to agree to serve overseas if needed.

The sepoys were also affected by the situation in the villages, as many were peasants with families there. The anger of the peasants quickly spread among the sepoys.

# Responses to Reforms
The British believed they needed to reform Indian society. They passed laws against *sati* and to allow widow remarriage. They actively promoted English education. After 1830, Christian missionaries were allowed to function freely and own land. A law in 1850 made conversion to Christianity easier by allowing converts to inherit ancestral property. Many Indians felt the British were trying to destroy their religion, customs, and traditional way of life.

However, some Indians did support changing existing social practices.
