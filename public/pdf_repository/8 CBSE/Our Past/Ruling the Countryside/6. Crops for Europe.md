# Crops for Europe

The British also realised that the countryside could provide not just revenue but also the crops required in Europe. By the late eighteenth century, the Company actively promoted the cultivation of opium and indigo. Over the next century and a half, they used various methods to persuade or force cultivators across India to grow specific crops needed in Europe:

*   **Jute** in Bengal
*   **Tea** in Assam
*   **Sugarcane** in the United Provinces (now Uttar Pradesh)
*   **Wheat** in Punjab
*   **Cotton** in Maharashtra and Punjab
*   **Rice** in Madras

To achieve this, the British employed various methods to expand cultivation of these needed crops. The story of indigo provides a closer look at one such crop and method.

### Does Colour Have a History?

Indigo, a rich blue colour, was produced from a plant of the same name. This colour was highly valued by cloth manufacturers in Europe.

### Why the Demand for Indian Indigo?

The indigo plant grows mainly in tropical regions. By the thirteenth century, Indian indigo was used by cloth manufacturers in Italy, France, and Britain. However, only small quantities reached Europe, and it was very expensive.

European cloth manufacturers mainly relied on **woad**, a plant from temperate zones, to make violet and blue dyes. Woad was easily available in Europe (northern Italy, southern France, parts of Germany and Britain). Woad producers, fearing competition from indigo, pressured their governments to ban indigo imports.

Despite this, cloth dyers preferred indigo because it produced a rich blue colour, while woad dye was pale and dull. By the seventeenth century, European cloth producers convinced their governments to ease the ban on indigo imports.

European powers began cultivating indigo in their colonies:

*   French in St Domingue (Caribbean islands)
*   Portuguese in Brazil
*   English in Jamaica
*   Spanish in Venezuela
*   Indigo plantations also started in North America.

By the late eighteenth century, the demand for Indian indigo increased significantly. Britain's industrialisation and dramatic expansion of cotton production created a huge demand for cloth dyes. Simultaneously, existing indigo supplies from the West Indies and America collapsed for various reasons. Between 1783 and 1789, world indigo production halved. British cloth dyers desperately needed new sources, and Britain turned to India.

*Plantation* - A large farm operated by a planter employing various forms of forced labour. Plantations are associated with the production of coffee, sugarcane, tobacco, tea and cotton.

Fig. 7 - The Slave Revolt in St Domingue, August 1791, painting by January Scuhodolski
In the eighteenth century, French planters produced indigo and sugar in the French colony of St Domingue in the Caribbean islands. The African slaves who worked on the plantations rose in rebellion in 1791, burning the plantations and killing their rich planters. In 1792, France abolished slavery in the French colonies. These events led to the collapse of the indigo plantations on the Caribbean islands.
