This section describes how the East India Company gained administrative and financial control over Bengal and its initial approach to collecting revenue, which led to an economic crisis.

# Company Rule and Early Revenue Policies

On 12 August 1765, the Mughal emperor appointed the East India Company as the *Diwan* of Bengal. This event made the Company the chief financial administrator of the territory under its control. Now, it was responsible for administering the land and organising its revenue resources. The goal was to generate enough revenue to cover the Company's increasing expenses. As a trading company, it also needed to ensure it could buy necessary products cheaply and sell its goods.

Initially, the Company saw itself primarily as a trader, not a ruler. It wanted large revenue income but was reluctant to establish a regular system for assessment and collection. The focus was on increasing revenue as much as possible to buy fine cotton and silk cloth cheaply. Within five years, the value of goods bought by the Company in Bengal doubled. Before 1765, the Company imported gold and silver from Britain to purchase goods in India. After becoming Diwan, the revenue collected in Bengal financed the purchase of goods for export.

However, the Company soon realised it needed to act cautiously. As an *alien power*, it had to manage those who had previously ruled the countryside and held authority. These local power holders needed to be controlled but not completely removed.

The Company's policies led to a deep crisis in the Bengal economy.

*   Artisans left villages because they were forced to sell their goods to the Company at very low prices.
*   Peasants struggled to pay the high dues demanded from them.
*   Artisanal production declined.
*   Agricultural cultivation showed signs of collapse.

In 1770, a terrible famine struck Bengal, killing ten million people, wiping out about one-third of the population.
