This section describes the process of manufacturing indigo dye from the plant and highlights the difficulties encountered by both planters (in *nij*) and *ryots* (in *ryoti*) systems.

# Processing Indigo and Problems with Cultivation

After harvest, the indigo plant was taken to the factory vats for processing into dye. Manufacturing the dye required three or four vats, each with a specific function.

*   **Fermenting/Steeper Vat:** Leaves were stripped from the plant and soaked in warm water for hours. Fermentation caused the liquid to boil and bubble. Rotten leaves were removed, and the liquid drained into the next vat below.
*   **Beater Vat:** The liquid was continuously stirred and beaten with paddles. As the liquid turned green, then blue, lime water was added.
*   **Settling Vat:** Indigo separated out in flakes, settling as a muddy sediment at the bottom. Clear liquid rose to the surface and was drained off. The sediment (indigo pulp) was transferred to this vat, then pressed and dried for sale.

*Vat* - A fermenting or storage vessel

### Problems with Nij Cultivation

Planters faced difficulties expanding *nij* cultivation:

*   **Land Availability:** Indigo needed fertile land, which was already densely populated. Large, compact blocks of fertile land for plantations were hard to acquire. Planters tried leasing land and evicting peasants, causing conflict.
*   **Labour Shortage:** Large plantations required many labourers, but labour was needed precisely when peasants were busy cultivating rice.
*   **Ploughs and Bullocks:** *Nij* cultivation on a large scale needed many ploughs and bullocks (two ploughs per *bigha*). Buying and maintaining them was costly. Getting supplies from peasants was difficult as their animals were busy in rice fields at the same time.

Due to these issues, planters were reluctant to expand *nij* cultivation. Less than 25% of indigo-producing land was under this system; the rest was under *ryoti*.

### Problems with Ryoti Cultivation

The *ryoti* system, while enabling expansion, created severe problems for the peasants:

*   **Endless Debt:** When the crop was delivered, the low price received meant a new loan was needed for the next cycle, trapping the *ryot* in perpetual debt.
*   **Forced Cultivation on Best Land:** Planters insisted that indigo be grown on the best soils, which peasants preferred for cultivating rice, their food crop.
*   **Soil Exhaustion:** Indigo has deep roots and rapidly exhausts the soil, making it unsuitable for sowing rice after an indigo harvest.

Peasants who were initially attracted by the loans soon realised the harshness of the system.
