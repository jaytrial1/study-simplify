This section describes the government's response to the rebellion, the findings of the Indigo Commission, and the eventual decline of indigo production in Bengal and its shift to Bihar.

# Aftermath of the Rebellion

Worried by the rebellion, the government deployed the military to protect planters and established the **Indigo Commission** to investigate the system.

*   The Commission found the planters guilty, criticising their coercive methods.
*   It declared that indigo production was not profitable for *ryots*.
*   The Commission instructed *ryots* to fulfill existing contracts but assured them they could refuse to grow indigo in the future.

### Source 3: "I would rather beg than sow indigo"

Hadji Mulla, an indigo cultivator, testified before the Indigo Commission in June 1860. He stated unequivocally that he was unwilling to sow indigo and doubted any new terms would satisfy him. When asked if he would grow it for a higher price, he replied he would rather go to another country or beg than sow indigo. This powerfully illustrates the depth of the *ryots'* suffering and their determination to refuse cultivation.

Following the revolt, indigo production in Bengal collapsed. Planters then moved their operations to Bihar. However, with the discovery of **synthetic dyes** in the late nineteenth century, the demand for natural indigo declined, severely impacting their business.

Despite this, planters in Bihar managed to expand production. When Mahatma Gandhi returned from South Africa, a peasant from Bihar persuaded him to visit Champaran to witness the plight of the indigo cultivators there. Mahatma Gandhi's visit in 1917 marked the beginning of the **Champaran movement** against the indigo planters, highlighting the continued struggle of cultivators against oppressive plantation systems.
