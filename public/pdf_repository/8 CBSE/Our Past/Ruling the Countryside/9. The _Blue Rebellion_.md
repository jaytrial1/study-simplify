This section describes the Indigo Rebellion of 1859 in Bengal, its causes, the actions taken by the *ryots*, and the factors that empowered them to rebel.

# The "Blue Rebellion"

In March 1859, thousands of *ryots* in Bengal openly refused to grow indigo. This resistance escalated into a rebellion:

*   *Ryots* refused to pay rent to the planters.
*   They attacked indigo factories, armed with weapons like swords, spears, bows, and arrows.
*   Women also participated, fighting with household items like pots and pans.
*   Those who worked for the planters were socially boycotted.
*   *Gomasthas* (agents of planters who collected rent) were beaten up.
*   *Ryots* vowed never to take indigo advances again or be intimidated by the planters' *lathiyals* (strongmen wielding *lathis*, or sticks).

While the indigo system was clearly oppressive, people don't always rebel. Several factors gave the indigo *ryots* the power to rise up in 1859:

*   **Support from Local Zamindars and Headmen:** *Ryots* felt they had the backing of local zamindars and village headmen. Headmen, sometimes forced to sign contracts themselves, mobilised peasants and fought the *lathiyals*. Some zamindars also encouraged resistance, unhappy with the planters' growing power and forced land leases.
*   **Hope for Government Support:** The *ryots* believed the British government might support them. The government was wary of another popular rebellion after the Revolt of 1857.
*   **Government Action Misinterpreted:** When news of the revolt spread, the Lieutenant Governor toured the indigo districts in 1859. *Ryots* saw this as a sign of sympathy. In Barasat, magistrate Ashley Eden issued a notice stating *ryots* would not be compelled to accept indigo contracts. Though intended to calm the situation, this was interpreted as a declaration from Queen Victoria that indigo cultivation was optional.

Intellectuals from Calcutta also rushed to the indigo districts, writing about the *ryots*' misery, the planters' tyranny, and the horrors of the system, which helped gain public support for the rebellion.

### Source 2: A Song from an Indigo-Producing Village

Songs sung during the rebellion reflect the feelings and unity of the people. One song from lower Bengal described the planter's *lathis* lying useless and the *babus* (gentlemen) from Kolkata coming to see the fight. It highlighted the *raiyats'* readiness to fight back, refusing to be silently beaten or give up their lives without resisting the *lathiyals*.

### Activity

Imagine you are a witness giving evidence before the Indigo Commission. W.S. Seton Karr asks you "On what condition will ryots grow indigo?" What will your answer be?
> **Answer:** Sir, the *ryots* will not grow indigo under the current system. The price is too low, trapping us in debt year after year. We are forced to grow it on our best land, land we need for rice to feed our families. Indigo ruins the soil. We would only consider growing it if we were paid a fair price that allowed us to live free of debt, if we could choose which land to use, and if it didn't ruin our fields for growing food. But honestly, after what we have suffered, many of us would rather not grow it at all.
