### Let's Discuss

3. Describe the main features of the Permanent Settlement.
> **Answer:** The Permanent Settlement, introduced in 1793, recognised rajas and taluqdars as zamindars. Zamindars were responsible for collecting rent from peasants and paying a fixed amount of revenue to the Company. This revenue amount was fixed permanently and would not be increased in the future. The system aimed to ensure a regular revenue flow and encourage zamindars to invest in land improvement.

4. How was the mahalwari system different from the Permanent Settlement?
> **Answer:** The Mahalwari system, introduced in the North Western Provinces, differed from the Permanent Settlement in several ways. Instead of fixing the revenue permanently with individual zamindars, the Mahalwari system estimated the revenue for each village (*mahal*) based on the fields within it. The revenue demand was subject to periodic revision, not fixed permanently. The responsibility for collecting and paying the revenue was given to the village headman, not the zamindar.

5. Give two problems which arose with the new Munro system of fixing revenue.
> **Answer:** Two problems with the Munro system (Ryotwari) were:
> 1. Revenue officials fixed the revenue demand too high, driven by the desire to increase income.
> 2. Peasants were unable to pay these high demands, causing *ryots* to flee the countryside and villages to become deserted.

6. Why were ryots reluctant to grow indigo?
> **Answer:** Ryots were reluctant to grow indigo because:
> 1. The price they received for the indigo they produced was very low.
> 2. The system of cash advances trapped them in an endless cycle of debt they could not escape.
> 3. Planters forced them to grow indigo on their best, most fertile land, which they needed to grow rice for food.
> 4. Indigo cultivation exhausted the soil, making it difficult to grow rice or other food crops on the same land afterwards.

7. What were the circumstances which led to the eventual collapse of indigo production in Bengal?
> **Answer:** The collapse of indigo production in Bengal was primarily caused by the "Blue Rebellion" of 1859. Thousands of ryots refused to grow indigo, attacked factories, and resisted the planters. The government, worried by the rebellion, set up the Indigo Commission. The Commission exposed the planters' coercive methods and declared indigo production unprofitable for ryots. Following the Commission's report, which allowed ryots to refuse future indigo contracts, production in Bengal rapidly declined.
