This section explores the issues that arose from the Permanent Settlement, affecting both the Company and the cultivators.

# Problems with the Permanent Settlement

The Permanent Settlement, despite its intentions, created significant problems.

*   **Zamindars did not invest:** Company officials soon found that zamindars were generally not investing in improving the land.
*   **High Revenue Demand:** The fixed revenue amount was set so high that zamindars found it difficult to pay. Those who failed to pay lost their *zamindari*, and many were sold off at auctions organised by the Company.
*   **Company loses out on rising income:** By the early nineteenth century, prices in the market rose and cultivation expanded. This increased the income of zamindars, but the Company gained nothing because the revenue demand was permanently fixed.
*   **Zamindars' lack of interest:** Even with rising incomes, many zamindars were not interested in improving the land. Some had lost their estates early on, while others could earn by simply renting out land to tenants without the risk or trouble of investment. As long as they received rent, land improvement was not a priority.

Meanwhile, the system was extremely oppressive for the cultivators in the villages.

*   **High Rent:** The rent paid to the zamindar was very high.
*   **Insecure Rights:** Cultivators' rights on the land were insecure.
*   **Debt Cycle:** To pay the rent, peasants often had to borrow from moneylenders.
*   **Eviction:** If they failed to pay the rent, they were evicted from the land they had cultivated for generations.

### Source 1: Colebrook on Bengal Ryots

H. T. Colebrook, writing in 1806, described the difficult conditions of under-tenants (ryots who rented land from powerful ryots who didn't cultivate themselves) in Bengal. These under-tenants were burdened by excessive rent paid in kind (crops) and high interest on loans for cattle, seeds, and subsistence. This made it impossible for them to escape debt. In such a state, they could not work with spirit, earning only a meager living without hope of improving their situation.
