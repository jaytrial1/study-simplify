This section describes the changes in the composition and training of the East India Company's army, transforming it from a force relying on cavalry to a professionally trained infantry.

# The Company army

Colonial rule relied on military strength. The Mughal army had cavalry (**sawars**: men on horses) and infantry (**paidal**: foot soldiers) trained in archery and swordsmanship. Cavalry dominated, and the Mughal state didn't need a large, professionally trained infantry. Rural areas had armed peasants, and zamindars supplied paidal soldiers.

Changes occurred in the eighteenth century. Mughal successor states like Awadh and Banaras recruited peasants and trained them as professional soldiers. The East India Company adopted this, recruiting for its **sepoy army** (from *sipahi*, meaning soldier).

As warfare technology changed from the 1820s, cavalry needs declined. British forces fighting in Burma, Afghanistan, and Egypt used **muskets** (heavy infantry guns) and **matchlocks** (early guns ignited by a match). Company soldiers had to adapt, making infantry regiments more important.

In the early nineteenth century, the British developed a uniform military culture. Soldiers received European-style training, drill, and discipline, regulating their lives more strictly. This often caused problems because caste and community feelings were ignored. Could individuals easily give up these feelings? Could they see themselves only as soldiers?
