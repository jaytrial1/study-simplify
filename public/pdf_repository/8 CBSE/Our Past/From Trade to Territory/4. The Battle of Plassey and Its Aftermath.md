This section focuses on the Battle of Plassey, its significance as the Company's first major victory, and the subsequent control over Bengal's revenue and the rise of Company officials as "nabobs".

# The Battle of Plassey

When Alivardi Khan died in 1756, Sirajuddaulah became Nawab. The Company wanted a *puppet* ruler (a person controlled by someone else) who would grant concessions. They unsuccessfully tried to help a rival become nawab. An enraged Sirajuddaulah ordered the Company to stop meddling, stop fortifying, and pay revenues.

After negotiations failed, the Nawab marched to the English factory at Kassimbazar, captured officials, locked the warehouse, disarmed Englishmen, and blockaded ships. He then marched to Calcutta to control the Company's fort.

Company officials in Madras sent forces under Robert Clive, reinforced by naval fleets. After prolonged negotiations, Robert Clive led the army against Sirajuddaulah at Plassey in 1757.

The main reason for the Nawab's defeat was that forces led by Mir Jafar, one of his commanders, never fought. Clive had promised to make him nawab after defeating Sirajuddaulah.

### Did you know?

Plassey got its name from Palashi, which came from the *palash* tree known for its red flowers used to make *gulal* for the Holi festival.

## Source 1: The promise of riches

The East India Company's territorial ambitions were viewed with distrust in England. After Plassey, Robert Clive wrote in 1759 about the possibility of gaining "absolute possession of these rich kingdoms" with a yearly income of "two million sterling with the possession of three provinces," asking if this was an object deserving public attention.

## Source 2: The Nawab complains

In 1733, the Nawab of Bengal complained about English traders: They humbly asked for land for a factory but built a strong fort, surrounded it with a ditch, mounted guns, enticed merchants for protection, collected revenue, and "rob and plunder and carry great number of the king's subjects... into slavery".

After Plassey, Sirajuddaulah was assassinated, and Mir Jafar was made nawab. The Company preferred to expand trade through cooperative local rulers rather than direct conquest. However, even puppet nawabs had to maintain dignity to gain respect from their subjects.

When Mir Jafar protested, the Company deposed him and installed Mir Qasim. When Mir Qasim complained, he was defeated at Buxar (1764), driven out, and Mir Jafar was reinstalled. The Nawab had to pay Rs. 500,000 monthly, but the Company wanted more for its wars, trade, and expenses. It wanted more territories and revenue.

By Mir Jafar's death in 1765, the Company's mood changed. Having failed with puppet nawabs, Clive declared, "We must indeed become nawabs ourselves".

In 1765, the Mughal emperor appointed the Company as the **Diwan** (right to collect land revenue) of Bengal provinces. This allowed the Company to use Bengal's vast revenue resources. This solved a major problem: earlier, the Company had to import gold and silver from Britain to buy goods in India, as Britain had nothing to sell there. The outflow of gold slowed after Plassey and stopped after the assumption of Diwani. Now, Indian revenues financed Company expenses: purchasing textiles, maintaining troops, and building forts and offices in Calcutta.

# Company officials become "nabobs" (nawabs)

Becoming nawabs meant the Company gained power and authority. It also meant Company servants envisioned living like nawabs.

After Plassey, Bengal nawabs were forced to give land and large sums as personal gifts to Company officials. Robert Clive amassed a fortune. He came to Madras at 18 in 1743 and left India in 1767 with £401,102. When appointed Governor of Bengal in 1764 to remove corruption, he was cross-examined by the British Parliament in 1772 due to suspicion of his wealth. He was acquitted but committed suicide in 1774.

Not all officials became rich. Many died early from disease and war. Many came from humble backgrounds, aiming to earn enough to return to Britain and live comfortably. Those who returned wealthy led flashy lives and were called "nabobs" (anglicised nawab). They were often seen as upstarts and ridiculed in British society.

## Source 3: How did Clive see himself?

At his Parliament hearing, Clive claimed admirable restraint after Plassey: "A great prince was dependent on my pleasure; an opulent city lay at my mercy; its richest bankers bid against each other for my smiles; I walked through vaults which were thrown open to me alone, piled on either hand with gold and jewels! Mr Chairman, at this moment I stand astonished at my moderation."
