This section describes the East India Company's establishment of a factory in Bengal and the rising tensions with the Nawabs of Bengal over trade concessions and revenue.

# East India Company begins trade in Bengal

The first English factory was set up on the banks of the river Hugli in 1651. This served as a base for the Company's traders, known as "factors". The factory included a warehouse for goods and offices for officials. As trade grew, the Company persuaded merchants and traders to settle near the factory.

*   By 1696, it began building a fort around the settlement.
*   Two years later, it bribed Mughal officials for zamindari rights over three villages, one of which was Kalikata (later Calcutta/Kolkata).
*   It also persuaded Emperor Aurangzeb to issue a *farman* (royal edict/order) granting the Company the right to trade duty-free.

The Company continuously pressed for more concessions and manipulated privileges. Aurangzeb's *farman* only granted the Company duty-free trade, but Company officials engaged in private trade also refused to pay duty, causing enormous revenue loss for Bengal. The Nawab of Bengal, Murshid Quli Khan, protested this.

# How trade led to battles

Conflict intensified between the Company and the nawabs of Bengal in the early eighteenth century. After Aurangzeb's death, the Bengal nawabs asserted their power. Murshid Quli Khan was followed by Alivardi Khan and then Sirajuddaulah. These strong rulers:

*   Refused to grant the Company concessions.
*   Demanded large tributes for trading rights.
*   Denied the Company the right to mint coins.
*   Stopped the Company from extending fortifications.

They accused the Company of deceit, claiming it was depriving the Bengal government of revenue and undermining the nawab's authority by refusing to pay taxes, writing disrespectful letters, and trying to humiliate officials.

The Company, in turn, declared that the local officials' demands were ruining trade, which could only flourish if duties were removed. It also believed that expanding trade required enlarging settlements, buying villages, and rebuilding forts.

These conflicts culminated in the Battle of Plassey.
