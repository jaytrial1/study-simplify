This section examines the Company's methods of territorial expansion between 1757 and 1857, focusing on the use of Residents and the implementation of the Subsidiary Alliance system.

# Company Rule Expands

Analysing the annexation process from 1757 to 1857 shows key aspects. The Company rarely launched direct military attacks. Instead, it used political, economic, and diplomatic methods to extend influence before annexing kingdoms.

After the Battle of Buxar (1764), the Company appointed **Residents** in Indian states. These were political or commercial agents serving Company interests. Through Residents, Company officials interfered in internal affairs, deciding successors and appointments.

Sometimes, states were forced into a **"subsidiary alliance"**. Indian rulers in this alliance could not have independent armed forces. They were to be protected by the Company but had to pay for the "subsidiary forces" maintained for this protection. If payment failed, part of their territory was taken as penalty.

For example, under Governor-General Richard Wellesley (1798-1805), the Nawab of Awadh was forced to give half his territory in 1801 for failing to pay for subsidiary forces. Hyderabad also ceded territories on similar grounds.

## Source 4: What power did the Resident have?

James Mill, the economist and philosopher, wrote about Residents: "We place a resident, who really is king of the country, whatever injunctions of non-interference he may act under. As long as the prince acts in perfect **subservience** (submissiveness), and does what is agreeable to the residents, that is, to the British Government, things go on quietly... but when anything of a different nature happens... then comes clashing and disturbance."
