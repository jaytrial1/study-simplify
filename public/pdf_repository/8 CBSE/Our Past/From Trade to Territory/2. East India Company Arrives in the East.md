This section details how the East India Company obtained its trading rights and faced competition from other European powers in the Indian market.

# East India Company Comes East

In 1600, the East India Company acquired a charter from Queen Elizabeth I, granting it the **sole right to trade with the East**. This meant no other English trading group could compete with it. The Company could buy goods cheaply in the East and sell them at higher prices in Europe, making profit by excluding competition.

However, this charter could not prevent other European powers from entering Eastern markets.

*   By the time the first English ships reached India, the Portuguese had a base in Goa. Vasco da Gama had discovered the sea route to India in 1498.
*   By the early seventeenth century, the Dutch were also exploring trade in the Indian Ocean.
*   Soon, the French traders arrived.

The problem was that all these companies wanted to buy the same goods: fine Indian *cotton* and *silk*, and spices like *pepper, cloves, cardamom*, and *cinnamon*. Competition drove up purchase prices, reducing profits. The only way to flourish was by eliminating rivals. This led to fierce battles. Throughout the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, they regularly sank each other's ships, blockaded routes, and fortified trading posts.

This effort to fortify settlements and carry on profitable trade led to intense conflict with local rulers. The company found it difficult to separate trade from politics.
