This section looks at potential solutions and examples of successful public water management, both internationally and within India.

# In Search of Alternatives

The situation in Chennai, with shortages and acute crises in summer, is common in other Indian cities. The gap in municipal water supply is increasingly filled by private companies selling water for profit. Great inequalities in water use are also common. The standard supply per person in urban India should be about 135 litres per day (seven buckets), but people in slums often get less than 20 litres (one bucket), while those in luxury hotels might use up to 1,600 litres (80 buckets).

A shortage of municipal water is often seen as a government failure. Some argue that since the government cannot supply enough water and many municipal water departments lose money, private companies should take over. They believe private companies can perform better.

Consider the following facts:

1.  Throughout the world, water supply is primarily a function of the government. There are very few examples of fully private water supply systems.

## Public Water Supply in Porto Alegre

Porto Alegre is a city in Brazil with many poor residents. However, it has a much lower number of infant deaths compared to most other cities globally. This is largely because the city's water department has achieved *universal access* to safe water. The average price of water is kept low, and the poor are charged half the basic rate. Any profit made is reinvested to improve the water supply. The department operates transparently, and people have a direct say in deciding projects through public meetings and voting on priorities.

2.  There are areas globally where public water supply has achieved universal access (like Porto Alegre).
3.  In a few cases where water supply was privatized, prices rose steeply, making water unaffordable for many. This led to huge protests and riots, such as in Bolivia, forcing governments to take back the service.
4.  Within India, there are examples of success in government water departments, though limited. Mumbai's water supply department earns enough from water charges to cover its expenses. In Hyderabad, a report showed increased coverage and improved revenue collection. In Chennai, the department has initiatives for rainwater harvesting and uses private companies for transport and distribution under contract, but the government sets the rates and permits operations.
