This section explores the historical relationship of Adivasis with forests, the impact of development projects and policies, displacement, and the resulting challenges they face.

## Adivasis and Development

Historically, forests were vital for empires and civilisations, providing resources like metal ores (iron, copper, gold, silver, coal, diamonds), timber, medicinal herbs, animal products, and animals (elephants). Forests also help recharge rivers and are crucial for air and water quality. Until the 19th century, forests covered most of India, and Adivasis had deep knowledge of, access to, and control over these areas. This allowed them to remain independent of large states and empires, which often depended on Adivasis for forest access.

This historical reality contrasts sharply with the modern image of Adivasis as marginal and powerless. For the past 200 years, economic changes, forest policies, and pressure from the State and private industry have increasingly forced Adivasis to migrate for work in plantations, construction, industries, and as domestic workers. They have lost control and direct access to forest territories.

From the 1830s, many Adivasis from Jharkhand and nearby areas migrated in large numbers to plantations in India (like Assam's tea industry) and abroad (Mauritius, Caribbean, Australia). This migration involved extreme hardship, torture, and death. For example, five lakh Adivasis died during these migrations in the 19th century alone. The song below reflects the migrants' hopes and the harsh reality they faced in Assam:

Come Mini, let's go to Assam
Our country has so much suffering
The country of Assam, oh Mini
Has tea gardens full of greenery...
The Sardar says work, work
The Babu says catch and bring them in
The Saheb says I'll take off the skin of your back
Hey Jaduram, you deceived us by sending us to Assam.

What metals are important in present-day India? Why? Where do they come from? Are there Adivasi populations there?
> **Answer:** Metals like iron, copper, gold, silver, coal, and diamonds are important in present-day India for industry and development. They come from mines, which are often located in areas that were historically and are currently inhabited by Adivasi populations.

List five products that you use at home that come from the forest.
> **Answer:** Five products that might come from the forest include timber (for furniture or paper), medicinal herbs (in medicines), honey, lac, and some spices.

By whom were the following demands being made on forest land?
- timber for construction of houses and railways
- forest land for mining
- forest land for agriculture by non-tribal people
- reserved by government as wildlife parks
> **Answer:**
> - timber for construction of houses and railways: State and private industry
> - forest land for mining: Private industry and State
> - forest land for agriculture by non-tribal people: Non-tribal people
> - reserved by government as wildlife parks: Government

In what ways would this affect tribal people?
> **Answer:** These demands lead to Adivasis losing their land, homes, sources of livelihood (like farming and collecting forest products), access to food, traditions, and way of life. They are often displaced and forced to migrate for low-wage work, leading to poverty and deprivation.

What do you think this poem is trying to convey?
> **Answer:** The poem conveys the difficult reality faced by Adivasi migrants who left their homes hoping for a better life working in Assam's tea gardens but instead experienced suffering, harsh work conditions, and deceit.

*Image description: A photo of Niyamgiri Hill in Odisha.*

This is a photo of Niyamgiri Hill located in Kalahandi district of Odisha. This area is inhabited by Dongarria Konds, an Adivasi community. Niyamgiri is the sacred mountain of this community. A major aluminium company is planning to set up a mine and a refinery here which will displace this Adivasi community. They have strongly resisted this proposed development and have been joined by environmentalists as well. A case against the company is also pending in the Supreme Court.

Adivasis use around 10,000 plant species - approximately 8,000 species are used for medicinal purposes; 325 are used as pesticides; 425 as gums, resins and dyes; 550 as fibres; 3,500 are edible. This entire knowledge system gets wiped out when Adivasis lose their rights over forest lands.

Forest lands have been cleared for timber, agriculture, and industry. Adivasis have lived in areas rich in minerals and resources, which are taken over for mining and industrial projects. Powerful forces often collude to take over tribal land, frequently forcefully and without following proper procedures. Official figures show that over 50% of people displaced by mines are tribals. A recent survey found that 79% of displaced persons in Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Odisha, and Jharkhand are tribals. Huge areas of their land have also been submerged by dams. In the Northeast, their lands are highly militarised. National parks and wildlife sanctuaries, covering large areas, were often created in areas where tribals originally lived and from which they were evicted. If they remain, they are called encroachers.

Losing land and forest access means Adivasis lose their main sources of livelihood and food. Displaced Adivasis migrate to cities for low-wage jobs, getting caught in poverty. 45% of rural tribal groups and 35% of urban tribal groups live below the poverty line. This leads to deprivation in other areas; many tribal children are malnourished, and literacy rates are low.

When displaced, Adivasis lose more than income; they lose traditions, customs, and their way of life. The interconnectedness of economic and social dimensions means destruction in one impacts the other. This dispossession and displacement can be painful and violent.

In your opinion, why is it important that Adivasis should have a say in how their forests and forest lands are used?
> **Answer:** It is important because Adivasis have a deep historical connection to forests, depending on them for their livelihood, food, traditions, and cultural identity. They also possess extensive knowledge about forest resources. Having a say allows them to protect their way of life and ensure their rights are respected when decisions about forest lands are made.
