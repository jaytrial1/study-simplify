This section introduces Adivasis, discusses their diversity, belief systems, languages, and how they are often stereotyped.

## Who are Adivasis?

Adivasis are communities whose name literally means 'original inhabitants'. They traditionally lived, and many still live, closely connected to forests. About 8% of India's population is Adivasi. Many major mining and industrial centres are located in Adivasi areas.

Adivasis are a diverse population with over 500 different groups in India. They are numerous in states like:
*   Chhattisgarh
*   Jharkhand
*   Madhya Pradesh
*   Odisha
*   Gujarat
*   Maharashtra
*   Rajasthan
*   Andhra Pradesh
*   West Bengal
*   North-eastern states (Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, Tripura)

Odisha alone has over 60 different tribal groups. Adivasi societies often have very little hierarchy, making them different from caste-based or kingdom-ruled societies.

Adivasis have unique belief systems, often involving the worship of ancestors, village spirits, and nature spirits (associated with mountains, rivers, animals, etc.). Village spirits are worshipped in sacred groves, while ancestral spirits are worshipped at home. Adivasi religions have been influenced by surrounding religions (Shakta, Buddhist, Vaishnav, Bhakti, Christianity) and have also influenced them (like the Jagannath cult, Shakti, and Tantric traditions). Many Adivasis converted to Christianity in the 19th century.

Adivasis have their own languages, some possibly as old as Sanskrit, which have influenced mainstream Indian languages like Bengali. Santhali is spoken by the largest number of Adivasis and has many publications.

Tribals are also referred to as Adivasis. The Indian government uses the term **Scheduled Tribes** for Adivasis in official documents, often grouped with Scheduled Castes.

## Adivasis and Stereotyping

Adivasi communities are often shown in very stereotypical ways in India, such as in colourful costumes and dancing at events. This limited portrayal leads people to wrongly believe they are exotic, primitive, and backward. Sometimes, they are blamed for their lack of advancement, seen as resistant to change. Stereotyping can lead to discrimination against these groups.
