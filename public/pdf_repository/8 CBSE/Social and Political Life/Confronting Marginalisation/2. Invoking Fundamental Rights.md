# Invoking Fundamental Rights

The Constitution, as you have learnt, lays down the principles that make our society and polity democratic. These are defined through the list of **Fundamental Rights**, which are available equally to all Indians. Marginalised groups have used these rights in two main ways:
1.  By insisting on their Fundamental Rights, they have forced the government to recognise the injustice done to them.
2.  They have insisted that the government enforce these laws.

In some instances, the struggles of the marginalised have influenced the government to frame new laws, in keeping with the spirit of the Fundamental Rights.

**Article 17** of the Constitution states that **untouchability has been abolished**. This means no one can prevent Dalits from educating themselves, entering temples, using public facilities, etc. Practising untouchability is wrong and will not be tolerated by a democratic government; it is now a punishable crime.

Other sections of the Constitution also strengthen the argument against untouchability. For example, **Article 15** states that no citizen of India shall be discriminated against on the basis of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Dalits have used this article to seek equality where it has been denied.

The following poem by Soyrabai, wife of the Bhakti poet Chokhamela from 14th-century Maharashtra (who belonged to the Mahar caste, considered untouchable), questions the idea of purity and pollution:

A body is unclean, they say
Only the soul is untainted
But the impurity of the body
Is born within the body
...By which ritual does the body become pure?
Not a creature has been born except in a bloody womb.
This is the glory of God,
Defilement exists within.
The body is polluted from within,
Be sure of it says the Mahari Chokha

Soyrabai questions the idea of purity, arguing that since every human is born similarly, no one body is less or more pure than another. She suggests that pollution, a tool of the caste system to deny access, occurs not through the nature of work, but "from within" – from thoughts, values, and beliefs.

Therefore, Dalits can 'invoke' or 'draw on' a Fundamental Right (or Rights) when they feel they have been treated badly by an individual, community, or the government. They demand that the government abide by the Constitution and do justice to them.

Similarly, other minority groups have drawn on Fundamental Rights, particularly the **right to freedom of religion** and **cultural and educational rights**. Groups like Muslims and Parsis have the right to be guardians of their culture's content and decide how best to preserve it. By granting different forms of cultural rights, the Constitution ensures **cultural justice** so that the culture of these groups is not dominated or wiped out by the majority community's culture.
