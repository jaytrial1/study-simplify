# Conclusion

As we can see, the existence of a right, law, or policy on paper does not automatically mean it exists in reality. People have had to constantly work and make efforts to translate these into principles that guide the actions of citizens and leaders. The desire for equality, dignity, and respect is not new; it has existed in various forms throughout history. Similarly, even in a democratic society, processes of struggle, writing, negotiation, and organising must continue.

# Exercises

1.  List two Fundamental Rights in the Constitution that Dalits can draw upon to insist that they be treated with dignity and as equals.
    > **Answer:** Two Fundamental Rights Dalits can draw upon are:
    > 1.  **Right to Equality (Articles 14-18):** This includes the abolition of untouchability (Article 17) and the prohibition of discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth (Article 15), ensuring they are treated equally and with dignity.
    > 2.  **Right to Freedom (Article 19-22):** While not directly about dignity in all aspects, the freedom to reside anywhere, practice any profession, etc., is implicitly linked to being treated as an equal citizen without restrictions based on caste. More specifically, the right to live with human dignity is often interpreted as part of the Right to Life (Article 21), which is a fundamental right.

2.  Re-read the story on Rathnam as well as the provisions of the 1989 Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act. Now list one reason why you think he used this law to file a complaint.
    > **Answer:** Rathnam used the 1989 Act because the actions taken against him – forcing him to perform a ritual based on his caste, ostracising his family, and burning his hut – constitute specific 'atrocities' or forms of humiliation and violence listed as punishable offences under this Act against members of Scheduled Castes.

3.  Why do Adivasi activists, including C.K. Janu, believe that Adivasis can also use this 1989 Act to fight against dispossession? Is there anything specific in the provisions of the Act that allows her to believe this?
    > **Answer:** Adivasi activists believe they can use the 1989 Act because it explicitly covers Scheduled Tribes. The Act includes provisions punishing those who wrongfully occupy or cultivate land owned by or allotted to a member of a Scheduled Tribe (provision iv mentioned in Section 5). This specific provision directly addresses the issue of dispossession of tribal lands by non-tribal people, allowing Adivasis to seek legal action against encroachers under this law.
