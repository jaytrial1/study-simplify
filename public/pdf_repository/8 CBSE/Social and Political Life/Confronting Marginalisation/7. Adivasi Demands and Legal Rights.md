The central government passed the **Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006**. This Act aims to undo the historical injustices faced by forest dwelling populations by recognising their rights to land and resources. It recognises their right to homestead, cultivable and grazing land, and non-timber forest produce. The Act also highlights that the rights of forest dwellers include the conservation of forests and biodiversity.

## Adivasi Demands and the 1989 Act

The 1989 Act (Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act) is important for Adivasi activists as well, who refer to it to defend their right to occupy their traditional land. As discussed, Adivasis are often unwilling to move from their land and face forced displacement. Activists demand that those who have forcibly encroached upon tribal lands be punished under this law.

They also point out that this Act confirms what the Constitution already promises: land belonging to tribal people cannot be sold to or bought by non-tribal people. In cases where this has happened, the Constitution guarantees tribal people the right to re-possess their land.

C.K. Janu, an Adivasi activist, notes that state governments are sometimes violators of constitutional rights guaranteed to tribal people. She argues that governments allow non-tribal encroachers (like timber merchants, paper mills) to exploit tribal land and forcibly evict tribal people from their traditional forests by declaring them reserved or sanctuaries. She also states that if tribals have already been evicted and cannot return, they must be compensated. Governments spend large sums on industrial or other projects on land taken from tribals, so they should not be reluctant to spend modest amounts on rehabilitating the displaced.
