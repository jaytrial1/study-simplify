# The Scourge of Manual Scavenging

**Manual scavenging** refers to removing human and animal waste using basic tools from dry latrines and carrying it on the head to disposal grounds. A manual scavenger is the person who performs this job, mainly Dalit women and young girls. According to the Safai Karamchari Andolan, an organisation working with manual scavengers, one lakh persons from Dalit communities still do this job in India, working in 26 lakh private and community dry latrines managed by municipalities.

Manual scavengers work in subhuman conditions and face serious health hazards, constantly exposed to infections affecting their eyes, skin, respiratory, and gastro-intestinal systems. They receive very low wages; those in urban municipalities earn around ₹ 200 per day, while private workers are paid much less.

Despite the abolition of untouchability by the Constitution, manual scavengers like the Bhangis in Gujarat, Pakhis in Andhra Pradesh, and Sikkaliars in Tamil Nadu are still considered untouchable. They often live in separate settlements and are denied access to temples, public water facilities, etc.

In 1993, the government passed the **Employment of Manual Scavengers and Construction of Dry Latrines (Prohibition) Act**, prohibiting manual scavenging and the construction of dry latrines. In 2003, the Safai Karamchari Andolan and 13 other organisations and individuals filed a **PIL (Public Interest Litigation)** in the Supreme Court. The petitioners complained that manual scavenging still existed, even in government undertakings like the railways, and sought enforcement of their Fundamental Rights.

The Supreme Court observed that the number of manual scavengers had increased since the 1993 law. In 2005, it directed every government department/ministry to verify the facts within six months. If manual scavenging was found, the government department had to implement a time-bound program for the liberation and rehabilitation of manual scavengers. The **Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act** came into force on December 6, 2013.

What do you understand by manual scavenging?
> **Answer:** Manual scavenging is the practice of manually cleaning human and animal waste from dry latrines using basic tools and carrying it away. It is a job primarily done by members of Dalit communities and involves working in extremely unhygienic and dangerous conditions.

Re-read the list of Fundamental Rights provided on page 14 and list two rights that this practice violates?
> **Answer:** Based on the typical list of Fundamental Rights (which includes Right to Equality, Right to Freedom, Right Against Exploitation, Right to Freedom of Religion, Cultural and Educational Rights, Right to Constitutional Remedies), the practice of manual scavenging violates:
> 1.  **Right to Equality (Article 14-18):** It is a practice rooted in caste-based discrimination and inequality, forcing specific communities into degrading labour.
> 2.  **Right Against Exploitation (Article 23-24):** It constitutes forced labour and violates human dignity, which falls under the prohibition of forced labour and traffic in human beings.

Why did the Safai Karamchari Andolan file a PIL in 2003? What did they complain about in their petition?
> **Answer:** The Safai Karamchari Andolan filed a PIL in 2003 because manual scavenging still existed despite the 1993 law prohibiting it. Their petition complained that the practice continued, even within government undertakings like the railways, violating the Fundamental Rights of those forced to do this work.

What did the Supreme Court do on hearing their case in 2005?
> **Answer:** On hearing the case in 2005, the Supreme Court observed that the number of manual scavengers had increased since 1993. It directed all government departments and ministries to verify the existence of manual scavenging within six months and, if found, to implement a time-bound program for the liberation and rehabilitation of manual scavengers.
