# Protecting the Rights of Dalits and Adivasis

In addition to policies, our country has specific laws guarding against the discrimination and exploitation of marginalised communities. Let us read a case study, adapted from a real-life account, to understand how Dalits use the protection that laws provide.

### Case Study: Rathnam's Refusal

The villagers of Jakmalgur were preparing for a big festival, held every five years to honour the local deity. Priests from 20 neighbouring villages attended the five-day event. The ceremony traditionally began with a member of the Dalit community washing the feet of all the priests and then bathing in the used water. In Jakmalgur, this task belonged to Rathnam's family; his father and grandfather had performed it before him. Although never allowed to enter the temple, this ritual was seen as a great honour.

Now it was Rathnam's turn. Rathnam, 20 years old and studying engineering, refused to perform the ritual. He stated he had no faith in the practice and that his family was forced into it because they were Dalits. His refusal angered the powerful castes in the village and some families from his own community. The powerful castes were shocked by his courage and believed his education made him think he could compare himself to them.

Rathnam's own caste members feared angering the powerful castes, many of whom they worked for as daily-wage labourers. They worried about losing work and survival. They also feared the wrath of the local deity. Rathnam countered that since no Dalit had ever entered the temple, the deity could not be angry with them.

The powerful castes decided to punish Rathnam. His community was ordered to ostracise him and his family, forbidding anyone from speaking to or working with them. One night, men set his hut on fire. Rathnam and his mother escaped and he went to the local police station to file a case under the **Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989**. Other Dalit families did not support him, fearing a similar fate. The local media picked up the case, leading to journalists visiting the village. Rathnam was presented as a symbol of Dalit action. The ritual was cancelled, but his family was forced to move away due to continued ostracism by the powerful castes.

In your opinion does the force put on Rathnam to perform this ritual violate his Fundamental Rights?
> **Answer:** Yes, the force put on Rathnam to perform this ritual violates his Fundamental Rights, particularly his right to dignity, his right against discrimination based on caste (Article 15), and potentially his right to freedom (Article 19) and freedom of religion (Article 25), as he was forced to participate in a ritual against his will due to his caste identity.

Why do you think the Dalit families were afraid of angering the powerful castes?
> **Answer:** The Dalit families were afraid because they were economically dependent on the powerful castes, often working on their fields as daily-wage labourers. Angering the powerful castes could mean losing their livelihoods and facing social boycott (ostracism), making survival difficult. They also feared physical violence and retaliation, as shown by the burning of Rathnam's hut.

Pandit, Look in your heart for knowledge
Tell me where untouchability
Came from, since you believe in it.
Mix red juice, white juice and air
A body bakes in a body ...
We eat by touching, we wash
by touching, from a touch
the world was born.
So who's untouched, asks Kabir?
Only she
Who is free from delusion

In this poem, Kabir directly challenges the priest, asking him to look within for the origin of untouchability rather than relying on scriptures. He describes the common human origin (blood, air, womb) and argues that everything in the world is created through touch. He redefines 'untouchable' not as a low status, but as a high state of knowledge – being untouched by narrow, limiting ideas. Thus, Kabir turns the idea of untouchability on its head.
