This chapter presents extracts from Ruskin Bond's diary, detailing his experiences and observations during the monsoon season. A diary serves as a personal record of daily events, thoughts, and plans.

## June 24

The author notes the arrival of the first monsoon mist. He observes that birds fall silent as the mist ascends the hill, contributing to a feeling of *melancholy*. The mist not only *blankets* (covers) the hills from view but also smothers the usual birdsong, making the forest feel eerily still, like midnight. Despite the reduced visibility, the author can hear Bijju calling to his sister and running on the hillside, though he cannot see him.

*   **melancholy:** very sad (the mist is called melancholy because it makes people feel melancholy)
*   **blankets:** covers

## June 25

Genuine early-monsoon rain arrives, described as warm and humid, unlike the cold high-altitude rain experienced earlier. The plants respond positively, with the first *cobra lily* emerging from the ferns. The author notes that the mist provides a certain privacy. When asked by a schoolboy to describe the hill station and valley in one sentence, the author responded, "A paradise that might have been."

## June 27

The rains bring seasonal visitors, including a leopard and thousands of leeches. The previous afternoon, the leopard took a dog near the school and later attacked one of Bijju's cows but was scared away by Bijju's mother's screaming *imprecations* (curses). The author anticipates getting used to the daily *bloodletting* (losing blood) from leeches.

Other new arrivals are *scarlet minivets* (bright red birds like a cuckoo; females are yellow), which flit silently among the leaves like jewels. Despite their bright colours, they can sometimes remain unnoticed by staying silent. Aggressive *drongos* (a song-bird with a stout bill) are also observed chasing the minivets away.

A tree creeper is seen moving rapidly up an oak tree trunk, catching insects. The author notes that with the rains, there is no shortage of food for insectivorous birds.

*   **heralded:** announced or brought the news of
*   **imprecations:** curses
*   **bloodletting:** losing blood (Decades ago, leeches were used to remove blood from a patient's body)
*   **scarlet minivet:** bright red bird like a cuckoo
*   **drongo:** a song-bird with a stout bill

This section details the author's experiences during the peak monsoon period in August, focusing on the constant rain, the sounds it makes, and the persistent mist.

# Mid-Monsoon Days

## August 2

The rain drums continuously on the corrugated tin roof throughout the night. It is a steady, tropical downpour without storm or thunder. The sound helps the author stay awake but also allows him to sleep. He finds it a good sound for reading, creating a feeling of being both untouched by and in touch with the rain, despite the potential for leaks in the tin roof.

## August 3

The rain stops, and the clouds begin to break up, allowing the sun to strike the hill on the author's left. Sounds of a woman chopping sticks and cowbells are heard. A crow shakes water from its feathers in the oak tree and caws *disconsolately* (unhappily). Water drips from a drainpipe. Suddenly, the pure song of the whistling thrush emerges from the ravine, described as a "dark sweet secret."

## August 12

The period is marked by endless rain and permanent mist. The sun has not been seen for eight or nine days, making everything damp and soggy. With nowhere to go, the author paces his room and looks out the window at umbrellas. He notes that at least the rain isn't cold. The hillsides are lush, and late-monsoon flowers such as wild balsam, dahlias, begonias, and ground orchids begin to appear.

This section describes the end of the monsoon season in late August, marked by changes in vegetation and the appearance of wildlife seeking shelter, and the transition into early winter rain in October.

# Late Monsoon and Seasonal Shifts

## August 31

It is the last day of August, and the lush monsoon growth is at its peak. The seeds of the cobra lily are turning red, which signifies the approaching end of the rains. The ferns are still firm, green, and upright but will start turning yellow in a few days.

A variety of orchids are in bloom on the grassy slopes of Landour, including *mauve lady's slipper* and *white butterfly orchids*, creating a "fashion display." Wild dahlias in red, yellow, and magenta grow from rocky *crevices* (narrow openings or cracks in rock or wall).

Snakes and rodents are flooded out of their burrows and seek shelter in roofs, attics, and godowns. A *shrew* (a small, mouse-like mammal with a long snout), which has poor eyesight, blunders around the rooms, amusing the children. Their grandmother advises against killing it, believing that *chuchundars* (the Hindi equivalent for shrew) are lucky and bring money. Coincidentally, the author receives a cheque in the mail, though not a large one.

## October 3

The weather transitions directly from monsoon into winter rain. Snow is falling at higher altitudes. After an evening hailstorm, the sky and hills are filled with a beautiful golden light.

# Winter Rains in the Hills

## January 26

In the quiet solitude of the house, after a friend has left, the author experiences the loneliness of winter rain. He sits in a "liquid silence" within, surrounded by the rhythm of rain falling on leaves, lemons, the roof, drenched dahlias, and window panes. The mist holds the house in a "dark caress." As he pauses near a window, the rain briefly stops and then starts again. The trees, now grey instead of green, seem to menace him with their loneliness.

This section describes the weather event marking the end of winter in late March and the subsequent clearing of the sky.

# End of Winter

## March 23

Late March marks the end of winter. A remarkably black cloud settles over Mussoorie, followed by a half-hour hailstorm with hailstones described as being like marbles. The author notes that a hailstorm effectively clears the sky, and as he writes, he observes a rainbow forming.
