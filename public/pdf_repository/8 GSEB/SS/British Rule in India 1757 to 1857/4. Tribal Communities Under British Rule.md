This section explores the conditions of various tribal groups in India during the 19th century and their traditional activities before the significant impact of British policies.

# Tribal Communities Under British Rule

The commercial exploitation policies of the British not only affected peasants but also led to revolts among tribal people in many regions. In the 19th century, India was home to diverse tribal groups, including Khonds, Santhals, Mundas, Koyas, Kols, Gonds, and Bhils.

## Distribution and Activities of Tribes

*   **Santhals:** Found around Hazaribagh in present-day Jharkhand, they often engaged in silkworm rearing, selling raw silk to traders.
*   **Mundas:** Located around Chhota Nagpur, they were typically involved in hunting and gathering.
*   **Khond:** In Central India, the Khond community also practiced hunting and gathering forest produce.
*   **Pastoralist Tribes:** Some groups were involved in animal husbandry.
    *   Van Gujjars (Punjab hills) and Lambadi (Andhra Pradesh) reared cows and buffaloes.
    *   Gaddis (Kullu) and Bakarwal (Kashmir) reared sheep and goats.

## Traditional Activities of Tribes

Tribal groups engaged in various activities for sustenance:

### Food Gathering and Hunting
Some tribes were primarily food gatherers and hunters.
*   Khond community members hunted in groups and gathered fruits, roots, medicinal plants, and herbs from forests.
*   Forest produce was often divided equally among the group and sold in local markets.
*   They sold flowers like Kusum and Palash to artisans for dyeing cloth and leather.

### Animal Husbandry
Some tribal people were associated with animal husbandry. They would migrate seasonally with their animals (sheep, goats, cows, buffaloes) in forests, such as those in Odisha.

### Farming Practices
Tribal groups practiced two main types of farming:

1.  **Shifting Agriculture (Jhum cultivation):** Practiced mostly in hilly and forest areas of Northeast and Central India.
    *   Tribes cleared small plots by cutting trees and grass to allow sunlight to reach the ground.
    *   The cleared vegetation was burned, and the ashes (rich in potash) were spread to fertilize the soil.
    *   Crops were grown on this plot for a few years.
    *   Once soil fertility declined, they would shift to a new plot and repeat the process.
    *   This method is also known as 'slash-and-burn' agriculture.

2.  **Sustainable Farming:** Before the 19th century, some tribal groups began settling down and practicing sustainable, settled agriculture. This allowed them to gain rights over the land they cultivated. Examples include the Santhals, Gonds, and Mundas of Chhota Nagpur.

## British Impact on Tribal Life

The East India Company viewed the settled tribes as a potential source of revenue and preferred them to those practicing shifting cultivation or living in forests. The British feared the independence of tribal communities and sought to curtail the authority of tribal chiefs, forcing them to follow Company rules and offer gifts. This significantly reduced the freedom of the chiefs. The British wanted tribes to settle in one place and cultivate land to become a reliable source of income. They introduced new laws for tribal communities, which were collectively opposed by many groups.
