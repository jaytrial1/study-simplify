This section explains how the British East India Company manipulated agricultural production in India to serve its economic interests, focusing on key cash crops.

# Commercialization of Agriculture

After gaining civil power in Bengal in 1765, the East India Company's main goal was to acquire goods from India that were in high demand in Europe. They focused on prosperous agricultural regions like Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha and aimed to establish a monopoly over India's agricultural markets. Agricultural products were crucial raw materials for export to England. The Company actively promoted the **commercialization of agriculture**, forcing farmers to grow specific cash crops according to their needs. These crops included indigo, cotton, raw silk, opium, and pepper.

## Focus on Key Cash Crops

### Raw Silk
In the 18th century, Britain imported raw silk from Spain and Italy. By controlling silk production in Bengal, Britain could reduce its dependence on these countries. By the late 19th century, the Company dominated silk exports from India, sometimes using oppressive methods against farmers.

### Indigo
Indigo, a natural dye grown in warm climates, was in high demand for dyeing cloth. Indian indigo was particularly valued for its bright color. European countries initially imported indigo from Caribbean countries. When Caribbean production declined around 1790 AD, the demand for Indian indigo surged. The British encouraged Europeans to settle in their Indian territories and increase indigo production.

There were two main systems of indigo production in India:
1.  **Nij System:** Producers used their own resources (plough, oxen, implements) to cultivate indigo. The raw material was delivered to the factory when ready.
2.  **Raiyati System:** Farmers cultivated indigo on their own land and sold the harvest to manufacturers at a fixed price. Manufacturers often provided loans to farmers, making them indebted and forcing them to cultivate indigo.

The Raiyati system was more profitable for manufacturers, who fixed low prices. Farmers were often persecuted and their crops destroyed if they refused to grow indigo. Company officials and their associates engaged in malpractices, including keeping fake accounts and coercing farmers. Since the rulers, bureaucrats, and indigo growers were often complicit, farmers' complaints were ignored. This widespread dissatisfaction led to the **indigo riots of 1859-60 AD**.

### Cotton
Cotton was a major cash crop in West India, similar to indigo in the East. By the 1780s, the East India Company and favored merchants controlled cotton-producing regions. They forced farmers to sell cotton below market prices, leading to the ruin of farmers who could not get fair value for their produce.

Other important cash crops at the time included sugarcane, tea, opium, pepper, and condiments. The British used their political power to buy these crops cheaply. They often gave farmers advance credit, putting them in debt and forcing them to sell their produce at low prices. This made traders wealthy while farmers became poorer. The Company exerted significant influence over the buying and selling of cash crops, ensuring a steady supply of raw materials for industries in England. This process contributed to many farmers becoming landless farm laborers.
