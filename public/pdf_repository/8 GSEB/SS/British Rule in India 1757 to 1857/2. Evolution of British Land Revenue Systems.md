This section details the different land revenue systems introduced by the British in India, aimed at increasing the Company's income.

# Evolution of British Land Revenue Systems

To increase revenue from Bengal, the Company introduced various land revenue systems over time.

## Early Revenue Collection Methods

In 1772 AD, a new system was introduced where contracts were given to 'Izaredars' (revenue collectors) to collect land revenue. The highest bidder received the monopoly for collection. This was known as the **'farming' system**. The Izaredars, focused solely on maximizing income, heavily exploited the peasants. This led to a need for reform. Lord Cornwallis was appointed Governor-General to implement changes.

## Permanent Settlement

In 1793 AD, during Cornwallis's tenure, the **Permanent Settlement** was introduced. Key features included:
*   Landlords were granted rights over the land and its revenue collection. They acted as agents for the government.
*   Landlords had to deposit nine out of ten parts of the collected revenue to the government, keeping one-tenth as their remuneration.

This system severely exploited farmers, who were forced to pay a fixed revenue to the landlord regardless of circumstances. Landlords, focused on meeting their fixed payment to the government, oppressed the farmers. Initially, landlords who failed to pay the fixed amount had their land confiscated, but later, they began acquiring more land. The system primarily favored the landlords and did not benefit the farmers or encourage agricultural progress. This led to numerous farmer revolts in Bengal, transforming the region from a 'granary' into an impoverished area.

## Ryotwari System

Introduced in the early 19th century (1820 AD) by Thomas Munro, the Governor of Madras, the **Ryotwari system** was implemented in the provinces of Mumbai and Madras (Chennai).
*   Under this system, the actual tiller of the land (the 'Ryot') was recognized as the owner.
*   Farmers had to pay land revenue directly to the government based on specific conditions.

However, this system also had drawbacks for the farmers:
*   The land revenue demanded was often excessive.
*   The government reserved the right to increase the land revenue whenever it wished.
*   Farmers were required to pay the fixed revenue even if crops failed or were destroyed.

## Mahalwari System

The **Mahalwari system** was introduced by British officer Holt Mackenzie in 1822 AD, primarily in the North-Western Provinces and parts of Central India.
*   The term 'Mahal' referred to a village or a group of villages in British revenue records.
*   Revenue collection was based on the entire village or its collective land, not individual farms.
*   Land revenue was determined by surveying the village, considering the income or produce of the collective land.
*   The responsibility for collecting the revenue from the village and paying it to the government was assigned to the village headman.

These changes fundamentally altered the traditional Indian land system, disrupting the stability, autonomy, and continuity of Indian villages.
