1_Introduction and Economic Impact of British Rule

This section introduces the economic state of India before British rule, the negative impact of the British on Indian industries and agriculture, and the initial state of cities.

# Introduction and Economic Impact of British Rule

India was economically prosperous until the eighteenth century. However, within 100 years of their rule, the British company significantly worsened the condition of India's **cottage industry**, **handicraft industry**, and **trade**. The East India Company aimed to create a large market for raw materials and finished goods for industries in England. This policy led to the decline of India's cottage industries and agriculture, ultimately causing the collapse of Indian villages. During this period, some cities, including Delhi, continued to flourish alongside these changes.

During the British rule, many old cities underwent significant **transformation**, while new cities developed rapidly. 3_Case Studies of City Development

This section provides specific examples of how cities like Mumbai, Surat, Madras (Chennai), and Kolkata developed under British influence.

# Case Studies of City Development

Today's **Mumbai** was originally an island. The British Emperor Charles II received the Mumbai island as a dowry when he married a Portuguese princess. The East India Company leased it from the British Emperor for a small annual fee of ten pounds. The company developed Mumbai as a **natural port** and city, which is now a major **industrial and commercial hub**.

The history of **Surat** is rich and interesting. Mughal Emperor Akbar conquered Surat in 1573 AD. During the Mughal period, Surat was a significant **commercial center** in Western India and an **international port**. It was known for its jariwork on clothes and was home to people of all religions and castes. The British established their first fort here in the seventeenth century. Surat also had **warehouses and factories** belonging to the Portuguese, the Dutch, and the British during that time. Even today, Surat remains an important industrial and commercial center.

Similarly, a fort was established at **Masulipatnam** on the East coast. Later, the British built **Fort St. George in Madras (Chennai)** in 1640 AD.

4_Delhi: The New Capital

This section focuses on the historical significance of Delhi and its transformation into the new capital under British rule, including the planning and construction of New Delhi.

# Delhi: The New Capital

Present-day **Delhi** is the capital of modern India, having previously served as the capital city under the Sultanate and Mughal rule. The British conquered Delhi from the Marathas in 1803 AD. They later moved their capital from Kolkata to Delhi and began the construction of **New Delhi** in 1911 AD. Today, New Delhi is a beautiful metropolis and the capital of independent India.

The British aimed to make modern Delhi a symbol of their identity. To create a secure city for themselves, they demolished some old structures in Delhi, including forts, buildings, gardens, and mosques. Following the establishment of the railway, Delhi began to expand beyond the old fort walls. The construction of present-day New Delhi started in the **Raisina Hills** area, south of Old Delhi. **Edward Lutyens and Herbert Baker** were responsible for designing New Delhi. Key buildings constructed included the **Viceroy's House** (now Rashtrapati Bhavan), **Rajpath Marg**, **Government Administration Buildings**, and the **Secretariat**. The construction of New Delhi took approximately 20 years.

5_Decline of Indian Cottage Industries

This section explains the state of India's traditional cottage industries and how they suffered and declined due to competition from British manufactured goods and the impact of the Industrial Revolution.

# Decline of Indian Cottage Industries

While many cities developed due to industrial growth during the British era, India's **cottage industries** collapsed because of the rise of large-scale industries, particularly in Britain. Before the arrival of the British, India's cottage industries were thriving. Industries like the production of **Muslin of Dhaka**, **embroidery**, **dyeing**, **printing**, and **combing** were highly developed. The Industrial Revolution led to an increase in the consumption of British goods in India, which directly harmed India's domestic industries. Indian weavers traditionally performed all steps from weaving to dyeing cotton cloth themselves, often using natural dyes like those from the parrot tree (kesuda). They wove cloth on **handlooms**. Sarees like **Bandhni** and **Patola**, made by these weavers, were very popular.

Many other cottage industries also flourished in villages. For instance, pure iron was produced by melting cast iron in furnaces and sold to merchants. These cottage industries contributed to a strong rural economy in India, which unfortunately collapsed due to the impact of the Industrial Revolution and British policies.

6_Challenges and Growth of Modern Industries

This section discusses how traditional Indian industries declined under British rule and how, despite hindrances, some modern industries began to emerge towards the end of the 19th century.

# Challenges and Growth of Modern Industries

With the beginning of British rule, India's traditional industries, such as **cotton textiles**, **sculptures**, **metallurgy**, and **spices**, started to decline. The development of modern factories in England was a major factor in the decline of Indian cottage industries. India's cotton and silk textile industries struggled to survive under the Company regime. The British aimed to expand their control over more Indian territories to support the growth of English industries, viewing India primarily as a **market**. The Company made large profits by obtaining cheap raw materials from India and selling finished goods at higher prices in Europe. Thus, England's industries developed at the expense of India's economy.

However, towards the end of the 19th century, influenced by scientific research, some modern industries like **linen, cotton textile, iron and steel, paper, chemicals, leather making, and shipbuilding** began to grow slowly. The British government often hindered the development of these Indian industries, which slowed their progress. Nevertheless, the textile and iron and steel industries managed to flourish to some extent.

7_The Indian Textile Industry

This section focuses specifically on the textile industry, its historical importance, the challenges posed by British competition, the rise of textile mills in India, and the impact of the Swadeshi movement.

# The Indian Textile Industry

From ancient times, the Indian **textile industry** was renowned globally. Owning Indian muslin and Patola was considered a mark of wealth. However, the Industrial Revolution in Britain changed this situation. England began producing vast quantities of textiles using machines, and the Indian handloom textile industry could not compete. Many handloom weavers in India lost their livelihoods and became unemployed.

## Know This

### Ranchhodlal Rentiawala

The ancestors of Ranchhodlal Rentiawala were originally from Patan, but his father Chhotalal settled in Ahmedabad. **Ranchhodlal Rentiawala** was born on April 24, 1823. He established the **first cotton mill in Ahmedabad** in 1861 AD. The second mill in Ahmedabad was started by Bechardas Lashkari in 1867 AD.

The Industrial Revolution's impact on textiles first became evident with the establishment of mills. The **first textile mill in India** was started in Mumbai in 1854 AD. Following this, textile mills were established in cities like Ahmedabad, Nagpur, Solapur, and Madras (Chennai). Ahmedabad alone saw the establishment of 106 mills. Due to the flourishing textile industry there, Ahmedabad came to be known as the **'Manchester' of India**, referencing the major textile center in England. Ranchhodlal Chhotalal's first mill in Ahmedabad began operation on May 30, 1861, at 4:45 p.m. The textile industry also developed in Solapur (Maharashtra) and Madurai (Tamil Nadu) in South India.

The introduction of modern technology in the textile industry led to unemployment for many Indian artisans. However, India's traditional textile art did not completely disappear. Craftsmanship requiring complex weaving, especially for making attractive saree borders, remained necessary, ensuring a continued need for skilled artisans. Solapur (Maharashtra) and Madurai (Tamil Nadu) were important centers for this kind of work. Textile artisans and the industry faced significant difficulties under British rule, including excessive taxes. British cloth was sold cheaply in Indian markets, forcing Indian producers to sell at prices they could not afford. After the arrival of Mahatma Gandhi, the **Swadeshi movement** became part of the freedom struggle, promoting Indian goods and giving a boost to India's rural industries, handloom weaving, cottage industries, and handicraft industries.

8_The Indian Iron and Steel Industry

This section details the development of the modern iron and steel industry in India, including the pioneering efforts of Jamshedji Tata, the decline of traditional methods, and the establishment of factories in various locations.

# The Indian Iron and Steel Industry

Industrial development in India accelerated partly due to the disturbances in Europe during the First World War. **Jamshedji Tata** established the **first iron factory in Sakchi** (now Jamshedpur). The establishment of this factory marked the beginning of basic industries like **iron and steel** in India. The traditional era of iron smelting, which required large amounts of coal, declined with the advent of innovative production methods. Under new British laws, obtaining coal from forests also became difficult. With the start of iron and steel production, other related industries also began to develop. The establishment of the **'Indian Institute of Science' in Bengaluru** provided new direction for the iron and steel industry. Iron and steel factories were also started at Kulti, Burhanpur, and Bhadravati. Thus, metallurgy gradually developed in India.

## Know This

### Ranchhodlal's Perseverance

Ranchhodlal had previously closed a factory in Bharuch and decided to start a textile mill in Ahmedabad. He ordered looms from England with the help of Dadabhai Navroji. The looms were sent by steamer to Mumbai. Since there was no railway to Ahmedabad at the time, they were transported by sea, but they sank. Adding to the difficulties, the English engineer hired to set up the looms died of cholera. Despite these challenges, Ranchhodlal ordered looms again from England. The 'Ahmedabad Cotton Spinning and Weaving Company' was established in a desolate area of Shahpur in 1859 AD with a capital of one lakh rupees, initially under a thatched roof. The first whistle blew in this mill on May 30, 1861, at quarter to five in the afternoon.

## Know This

### Tipu Sultan's Sword

The sword of **Tipu Sultan** is considered a masterpiece of Indian metallurgy. It was exceptionally sharp, capable of easily cutting through an enemy's armor. This was because it was made using **Woods steel**, a type of steel with high carbon content.

## Know This

### About the Establishment of Jamshedpur

In April 1904, Dorabji, the eldest son of Jamshedji Tata, and American geologist Charles Weld were searching for iron ore deposits in what is now Jharkhand. After a long search, they met people of the **Agaria community** who were carrying baskets of iron ore. The Agaria people directed them to a mountainous area. Dorabji and Weld found the **Razara Hills**, which contained some of the world's finest iron ore. However, this area was difficult to access. Later, an industrial city, known today as **Jamshedpur**, was established by clearing forests on the banks of the **Suvarnarekha river**.

9_Development of Infrastructure: Railways, Post, and Telegraph

This section describes the introduction and initial development of key infrastructure like railways, post, and telegraph services during the British period, particularly under Lord Dalhousie.

# Development of Infrastructure: Railways, Post, and Telegraph

India saw significant improvements in infrastructure during the period of **Lord Dalhousie**. Some English reformers referred to him as the creator of modern India due to these developments. The **first railway line** in India, connecting Mumbai to Thane, began operation in 1853 AD, inaugurated on April 16, 1853. The **Telegraph line** between Kolkata and Peshawar, and Mumbai and Madras (Chennai) also started during Dalhousie's time. Around the same period, the **postal system** was introduced. Initially, mail delivery fees were charged to the recipient rather than the sender. Postal rates also varied by region. Despite many obstacles and hindrances under British governance, crucial infrastructure like cotton textiles, chemicals, iron and steel industries, post and telegraph services, and railway and transport facilities began to develop.
