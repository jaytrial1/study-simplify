# The Indian Textile Industry

From ancient times, the Indian **textile industry** was renowned globally. Owning Indian muslin and Patola was considered a mark of wealth. However, the Industrial Revolution in Britain changed this situation. England began producing vast quantities of textiles using machines, and the Indian handloom textile industry could not compete. Many handloom weavers in India lost their livelihoods and became unemployed.

## Know This

### Ranchhodlal Rentiawala

The ancestors of Ranchhodlal Rentiawala were originally from Patan, but his father Chhotalal settled in Ahmedabad. **Ranchhodlal Rentiawala** was born on April 24, 1823. He established the **first cotton mill in Ahmedabad** in 1861 AD. The second mill in Ahmedabad was started by Bechardas Lashkari in 1867 AD.

The Industrial Revolution's impact on textiles first became evident with the establishment of mills. The **first textile mill in India** was started in Mumbai in 1854 AD. Following this, textile mills were established in cities like Ahmedabad, Nagpur, Solapur, and Madras (Chennai). Ahmedabad alone saw the establishment of 106 mills. Due to the flourishing textile industry there, Ahmedabad came to be known as the **'Manchester' of India**, referencing the major textile center in England. Ranchhodlal Chhotalal's first mill in Ahmedabad began operation on May 30, 1861, at 4:45 p.m. The textile industry also developed in Solapur (Maharashtra) and Madurai (Tamil Nadu) in South India.

The introduction of modern technology in the textile industry led to unemployment for many Indian artisans. However, India's traditional textile art did not completely disappear. Craftsmanship requiring complex weaving, especially for making attractive saree borders, remained necessary, ensuring a continued need for skilled artisans. Solapur (Maharashtra) and Madurai (Tamil Nadu) were important centers for this kind of work. Textile artisans and the industry faced significant difficulties under British rule, including excessive taxes. British cloth was sold cheaply in Indian markets, forcing Indian producers to sell at prices they could not afford. After the arrival of Mahatma Gandhi, the **Swadeshi movement** became part of the freedom struggle, promoting Indian goods and giving a boost to India's rural industries, handloom weaving, cottage industries, and handicraft industries.
