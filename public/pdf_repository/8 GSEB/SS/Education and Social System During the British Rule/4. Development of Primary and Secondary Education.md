This section covers the evolution of primary and secondary education under British rule, including key policies, commissions, and their impact on the spread of education.

# Primary and Secondary Education Under British Rule

The **Charter of 1813 AD** permitted Christian clergy to set up educational institutions in India. A priest named Alexander Duff founded Western schools. This system also allocated **Rs 1 lakh per annum** for education. However, Arabic, Persian, and Hindi educational systems continued.

Some Indian reformers and British officials favored introducing Western education across India, while some Hindus and Muslims preferred education only in traditional schools and madarsas, respectively. Under William Bentick, the East India Company advanced Western education based on the **Charter of 1833 AD**. Consequently, English education began in India in **1835 AD**. From this point, British policy favored providing financial aid primarily to promote English education. The **Wood's dispatch in 1854 AD** recommended adopting a European education system over the existing Indian one.

The credit for initiating English education in India is often given to **William Bentick**. After 1835 AD, English education spread to major areas like Kolkata (Bengal), Mumbai, Madras (Chennai), North India, Punjab, and West India. Despite this, the old traditional education style persisted in villages and towns. As English-educated individuals were favored for jobs, traditional Indian educational institutions saw a decline. Efforts by **Elphinstone** in Mumbai, **Munro** in Madras (Chennai), and **Thomson** in North India significantly increased the number of schools and colleges. Primary education particularly grew in Uttar Pradesh.

The **Hunter Commission of 1882 AD** proposed assigning responsibility for primary and secondary education to local self-governing bodies to broaden educational reach. The commission also recommended allocating a percentage of provincial revenue to education. In secondary education, the government supported non-governmental bodies, relaxed rules, and allowed establishing libraries and collecting fees, contributing to its development.

The **Sadler Commission**, appointed in 1917 AD, recommended imparting education up to the secondary level in regional languages or the mother tongue. After the **Montagu-Chelmsford Act of 1919**, the Department of Education was transferred to elected representatives in the provinces to oversee primary and secondary education development. In 1912 AD, **Gopalkrishna Gokhale** suggested a law for compulsory primary education, but it was rejected by the government. By 1936 AD, the number of primary and secondary schools had increased. However, the British efforts were largely aimed at providing basic education to Indians primarily to secure cheap clerical staff, with insufficient focus on science, technology, and skill development, causing India to lag globally in these areas.
