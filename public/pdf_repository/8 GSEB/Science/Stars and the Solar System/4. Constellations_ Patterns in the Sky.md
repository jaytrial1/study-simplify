This section introduces constellations, explains how they are formed by recognizable star patterns, and describes some famous examples.

# Constellations

Stars forming a group with a recognizable shape is called a **constellation**. Ancient people devised constellations to recognize stars, giving them shapes resembling familiar objects or figures.

You can identify constellations if you know their appearance and location in the night sky.

One famous summer constellation visible in the early night is **Ursa Major** [Fig. 17.11 (a)]. It's also known as the Big Dipper, the Great Bear, or the Saptarshi.

## Ursa Major (Saptarshi)

Saptarshi is associated with seven ancient Indian sages or rishis. According to mythology, these sages preserve and explain the eternal knowledge of the Vedas in every new age.

## Activity: Observing Ursa Major

Observe Ursa Major for a few hours. Does its shape change? Does its position change?

**Q: Do you find any change in its shape [of Ursa Major]?**
> **Answer:** You will observe that the shape of the constellation remains the same.

**Q: Do you find any change in its position [of Ursa Major]?**
> **Answer:** You will find that the constellation appears to move in the sky from east to west.

The constellation appears to move from east to west.

**Q: I have heard that we can locate the Pole Star with the help of Ursa Major.**
> **Answer:** Yes, you can locate the Pole Star with the help of Ursa Major.

## Activity: Locating the Pole Star

On a clear, moonless summer night around 9:00 pm, look north and identify Ursa Major (get help if needed). Look at the two stars at the end of its bowl. Imagine a straight line passing through these stars and extend it northward (about five times the distance between the two stars). This line points to a star that is not very bright – the Pole Star. Observe it; it does not move while other stars drift east to west.

## Activity: Ursa Major and the Pole Star

During a summer night, observe Ursa Major 3-4 times at 2-3 hour intervals. Locate the Pole Star each time. Does Ursa Major appear to move from east to west? Does it appear to revolve around the Pole Star?

In fact, all stars appear to revolve around the Pole Star. The Pole Star is not visible from the southern hemisphere. Some northern constellations like Ursa Major may also not be visible from parts of the southern hemisphere.

**Orion** is another well-known constellation visible during winter evenings. It's magnificent and has seven or eight bright stars [Fig. 17.11(b)]. Orion is also called the Hunter. The three middle stars form the hunter's belt, and four bright stars form a quadrilateral.

The brightest star in the sky, **Sirius**, is near Orion. To find Sirius, imagine a line through Orion's belt stars and look eastward along this line. It leads to very bright Sirius.

**Cassiopeia** is a prominent northern constellation visible in early winter evenings. It looks like a distorted letter W or M [Fig. 17.11(c)].

## Did you know?

A constellation contains a large number of stars, not just 5-10 [Fig. 17.16]. We only see the bright ones with the naked eye. Stars in a constellation are not at the same distance; they just appear in the same line of sight from Earth.
