# Activities on Vision

This section includes activities to demonstrate properties of the eye, such as pupil size change and the blind spot.

Caution: For this activity, never use a laser torch.

## Activity 16.8

Look into your friend's eye. Observe the size of the pupil. Throw light on her eye with a torch. Observe the pupil now. Switch off the torch, and observe her pupil once again. Do you notice any change in the size of the pupil? In which case was the pupil larger? Why do you think it was so? In which case do you need to allow more light in the eye, when the light is dim or bright?

> **Answer:** You will notice a change in the size of the pupil. The pupil was larger when the light was dim. This is because the iris controls the size of the pupil to regulate the amount of light entering the eye. In dim light, the iris makes the pupil larger to allow more light in. You need to allow more light in the eye when the light is dim.

## Activity 16.9

Make a round mark and a cross on a sheet of paper with the spot to the right of the cross (Fig. 16.15). The distance between two marks may be $6-8 \mathrm{~cm}$. Hold the sheet of paper at an arm's length from the eye. Close your left eye. Look continuously at the cross. Move the sheet slowly towards you, keeping your eye on the cross. What do you find? Does the round mark disappear at some point? Now close your right eye. Look at the round mark now and repeat the activity. Does the cross disappear?

## $x$

Fig. 16.15 : Demonstration of blind spot

> **Answer:** Yes, the round mark (or the cross, depending on which eye is closed and which mark you focus on) will disappear at some point as you move the sheet closer. This disappearance demonstrates the existence of the blind spot on the retina, where the optic nerve leaves the eye and there are no photoreceptor cells to detect light.

The impression of an image does not vanish immediately from the retina. It persists there for about $1/16$ th of a second. So, if still images of a moving object are flashed on the eye at a rate faster than 16 per second, then the eye perceives this object as moving.

## Activity 16.10

Get a square piece of cardboard of size $6-8 \mathrm{~cm}$. Make two holes as shown in Fig. 16.16. Thread a string through the two holes. Draw/ paste a cage on one side of the cardboard and a bird on the other side. Twist the string and make the card twirl rapidly. Do you see the bird in the cage?

> **Answer:** Yes, when the card is twirled rapidly, you will see the bird in the cage. This happens because of the persistence of vision; the image of the bird and the image of the cage persist on your retina briefly after you see them, and when they are presented in rapid succession, your brain combines them into a single image.

The movies that we see are actually a number of separate pictures in proper sequence. They are made to move across the eye usually at the rate of 24 pictures per second (faster than 16 per second). So, we see a moving picture.

Nature has provided eyes with eyelids to prevent any object from entering the eye. Eyelids also shut out light when not required.
