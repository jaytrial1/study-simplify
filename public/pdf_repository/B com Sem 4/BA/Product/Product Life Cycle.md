3\. PRODUCT LIFE CYCLE:\*

(a) Definition: The life cycle of a product is similar to the life cycle of a human being. The product, like a human being, is born, grows, attains maturity and then enters its declining phase. Ultimately it disappears from the market. All these stages together constitute a life cycle of the product. Of course the span of life and length of its stages are different for diffe ret products. As Philip Kotler has remarked, The product life cycle is an attempt to recognise distinct stages in the sales history of the product' Cundiff and Others have said, "from the time a product idea is conceived, a product is in various prenatal stages. Its life begins with its market introduction, it then goes through a period during which its market grows rapidly, eventually it reaches market maturity, afterwards its market declines, and finally its life ends." Thus there are four major stages of the life cycle of a product. They are (1) Introduction stage, (2) Growth stage, (3) Maturity

stage and (4) Decline stage. These stages may be visualised as follows: (b) Stages of Product Life Cycle:

Let us describe in brief each of these stages:

(1) Introduction Stage: This is the stage in which a new product is first made available in the market. In the introduction stage, customers are few, competition is less, sales are low, risk is high and profits are low or nil. There are heavy distribution and promotion expenses. This stage is full of risks and uncertainties. There are cases of untimely death of the product in the very introduction stage. For instance, Rushi Ghee, a new product of the Hindustar Lever Ltd. failed to attract buyers and had to be abandoned, though it was pure ghee. Thus, even the most experienced companies sometimes fail to lif their new products from the introduction stage to the growth stage. Notable features of the introduction stages are as follows:

(i) Promotional expenses are very high as compared to sales because of three factors: (1) High advertisement efforts are to be made to inform the potential buyers of the new and unknown product, (2) High costs are incurred to induce trial of the product among customers and (3) High costs are involved in attracting distributors in the retail markets.

(ii) Prices are also high because (1) costs are high due to low level of output. (2) technological problems in production may not have been solved, and (3) high profit margins are required to support the heavy promotion expenditure.

(iii) The introduction stage reveals weaknesses of the product or production techniques. These weaknesses are promptly removed.

(iv) This stage is characterised by a low degree of competition. In the beginning, competitors are not aware of the position of the new product. It is only wher product's worth is established that they enter the field.

(v) at this stage, profits are minimum or losses are maximum.

(2) Growth Stage: During this stage, when buyers are satisfied with the product, sales start rising rapidly. A large number of new buyers begin purchasing the product. Attracted by possibilities of profits, new competitors enter the market. The main features of the growth stages are as follows:

i) It is characterised by an increasing degree of competition. New competitors are attracted by the opportunities for large scale production and profit,

(ii) Prices tend to fall slightly during this stage. Prices are reduced (a) to search out new markets for the product or (b) to induce the existing buyers to purchase more than before or (c) to keep away the rival producers.

(iii) Profit margin is very high during this stage, because expenses are now spread over a larger volume and manufacturing cost per unit falls faster than prices.

(iv) With increasing degree of competition, new features are added to the product. Companies maintain their promotion expenditure at the same or slightly high level. They have to search for new markets or sometimes reduce prices.

(3) Maturity Stage: In this stage sales stabilise at a definite level. The buyers who are satisfied with the product continue purchasing in definite amounts. Sooner or later supply exceeds demand. "The companies take steps to stimulate demand or to face competition through additional advertising and sales promotion. New markets for the product may be developed to prevent the decline. According to Philip Kotler, this stage normally lasts much longer than the previous stages, and it poses some of the most formidable challenges to marketing management. Most products are in the maturity stage of the life cycle, and therefore most of marketing management deals with the mature products.'

Following are the main features of the maturity stage (i) Leading companies get established in the market and their brands have earned popularity. A definite group of buyers gets attached with each brand. Thus every company has monopolised a certain segment of the market. The Hindustan Lever Ltd., the Godrej and the Bata Shoe Company etc. are in this stage of the cycle of their products.

(ii) The sales promotion expenses stabilise at their normal level. The companies try to develop a more effective advertising campaign to attract consumers' attention and interest.

(iii) Prices also stabilise at a certain level. A price cut is now most likely to reduce profit margin. The companies often organise 'reduction sales' to clear their stocks. They may even be prepared to offer a new product in exchange for the old one.

(iv) Producers think of product development at this stage. They try to introduce modifications and developments in the products so that it will attract new customers or more purchases from existing customers

Some authors divide this stage into two phases: maturity stage and saturation stage. Basically there is no difference between the two, except that the features of the first phase are much more glaring than in the second phase,

(4) Decline Stage: Every product ultimately enters the decline stage. The decline may be slow for some products and rapid for others. New products become effective substitutes. The current products lose buyers. A number of firms withdraw from the market and invest their resources in new products. Some companies strive to hold on and they succeed sometimes. For instance the Proctor and Gamble Company in America continued producing when other companies were getting out of the liquid-soap business. Of course, the companies which continue in the same business try to reduce promotional expenses, varieties of the product and prices also.

The main features of the decline stages are as follows:

(i) Sales decline very rapidly.

(ii) Profits decline more rapidly than sales.

(iii) The producers move towards product simplification.

(iv) They try to shift to the production of new products whenever possible.

(v) They are eager to clear their stocks at the earliest.
