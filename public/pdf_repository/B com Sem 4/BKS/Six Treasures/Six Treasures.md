# SIX TREASURES 

Since ancient times, it has been said that having a strong character is crucial. The Six Treasures (Shat Sampatti) refers to the six qualities that enhance a person's character, thus considered as a form of wealth. In order for to qualify for Self-Realization, we need to gather these qualities,

1. Shama
2. Titiksha
3. Dama
4. Vairagya
5. Uparati
6. Viveka

## Shama (Inner Tranquillity of mind)
Shama, in Indian spiritual and philosophical traditions, refers to the cultivation of inner tranquility and control over the mind. It is a state where the mind becomes calm, composed, and free from turbulence, allowing one to experience clarity and balance. Shama is considered an essential virtue for spiritual aspirants and is often mentioned in conjunction with Dama (control of the senses).

### Key Aspects of Shama

1. Inner Calmness : Shama signifies the ability to maintain a peaceful mind, unaffected by external disturbances or internal agitation.
2. Control over Thoughts : It involves mastering the mind so it does not wander into negative or unproductive thoughts, such as anger, greed, or fear.
3. Foundation for Spiritual Progress : Shama is a prerequisite for meditation and self-inquiry, as it allows the mind to focus on higher goals withcut being distracted.
4. Distinct from Dama : While Dama focuses on controlling external actions and sensory inputs, Shama emphasizes internal mental discipline and serenity.

### Benefits of Shama 

- Mental Clarity: A calm mind is better equipped to make thoughtful and wise decisions.
- Emotional Balance: Reduces anxiety, fear, and other negative emotions.
- Spiritual Awareness: Prepares the mind for higher spiritual prictices by creating a conducive inner environment.
- Harmonious Living: Promotes healthier relationships and interactions by reducing reactivity and impulsiveness.
Shama is not about suppressing the mind but training it to remain steady and peaceful amidst life's ups and downs. It is a skill that deepens with regular practice and self-discipline, ultimately leading to a profound sense of inner freedom and joy.

## Dama (Alignment between mind and senses)

Dama is a Sanskrit term that refers to the practice of self-restraint, specifically the control or regulation of the senses and their impulses. It is an essential virtue in Indian spiritual traditions, particularly in Vedanta, Yoga, and other philosophical systems that emphasize inner discipline as a path to self-realization.

### Key Aspects of Dama

1. Alignment of Mind and Senses : Dama involves harmonizing the senses with the mind's higher aspirations. Instead of allowing the senses to lead the mind into distraction or indulgence, one brings them under conscious control to foster inner tranquility.
2. Practical Implementation :

- Moderation: Practicing balance in sensory pleasures, avoiding both indulgence and suppression.
- Mindfulness: Cultivating awareness of sensory inputs and their influence on the mind.
- Discipline: Consciously choosing actions that align with one's spiritual or moral goals.

3. Connection to Shama : Dama is closely related to Shama, which refers to the control of the mind. While Shama focuses

on calming and directing the mind, Dama ensures the senses
4. Role in Spiritual Growth:

- Ethical Foundation: It strengthens self-discipline and moral integrity, creating a foundation for higher spiritual practices like meditation and self-inquiry.
- Freedom from Attachment: By regulating sensory inputs, one minimizes attachment and aversion, leading to greater equanimity.


### Dama in Daily Life
- Dietary Discipline: Eating wholesome food in moderation rather than succumbing to cravings.
- Consumption Habits: Avoiding overindulgence in entertainment, social media, or other distractions.
- Speech and Hearing: Speaking truthfully and kindly, and listening without being overwhelmed by gossip or negativity.
By practicing Dama, individuals achieve a deeper state of selfcontrol, paving the way for inner peace and liberation. It is a lifelong practice that requires consistent effort and awareness.


## Uparati (Interest to do work)
Uparati comes next. It is satiety. Uparati refers to the cessation or withdrawal from external distractions, worldly pursuits, or excessive activity, and developing a focused, disciplined approach toward inner peace, contemplation, and spiritual practice. It is often translated as "disengagement" or "renunciation," but it does not imply complete inaction. Instead, Uparati refers to the cessation of unnecessary or unproductive desires, which allows an individual to engage more effectively in spiritual work, self-discipline, and righteous duties (dharma).

While it may seem like a withdrawal from external activities, Uparati actually involves the cultivation of inner calm andidetachment, so that one can engage in the right actions and duties with a clearer sense of purpose and focus.

### Key Aspects of Uparati
1. Withdrawal from Excessive Activity: Uparati means refraining from indulgence in materialistic or unnecessary actions and desires. This withdrawal is not from all work but from work that is motivated by selfish desires or worldly attachments. It's a process of disengagement from activities that disturb the mind or lead one away from spiritual progress.
2. Engagement in Spiritual Practices: Instead of being consumed by worldly distractions, Uparati encourages a person to direct their energy towards higher pursuits, such as meditation, prayer, study of scriptures, or other spiritual practices that lead to self-realization.
3. Focus and Balance: This quality is about developing the ability to engage in work and daily tasks with balance and mindfulness, without being overly attached or distracted by them. The goal is to perform duties with a sense of purpose while maintaining an inner detachment and mental calm.
4. Dispassion: A key element of Uparati is cultivating a sense of dispassion (virakti). This is not a complete rejection of the world, but rather the development of the ability to engage with it without becoming overly attached to its fleeting pleasures and material concerns.
5. Concentration on the Essential: By practicing Uparati, an individual can focus on what is truly important whether it's fulfilling one's responsibilities, engaging in selfless service, or pursuing spiritual goals without being sidetracked by distractions.

### Uparati in Practice
- Uparati is not about avoiding work or duties, but performing them in a way that is aligned with one's higher purpose. For example, a person may choose to avoid excessive material pursuits or distractions that take away from their spiritual focus, and instead concentrate on their dharma (righteous duty), relationships, or meditation practices.
- It involves finding a balance between worldly responsibilities

and spiritual aspirations. By focusing on the essential and eliminating distractions, Uparati helps a person stay connected to their deeper purpose in life.

## Titiksha (Forbearance)
Titiksha is power of endurance. Titiksha refers to forbearance or patience. It signifies the capacity to withstand things that do not appeal to you. At this point, it's unrealistic to expect everything to unfold according to your preferences. If you often lose your composure and mental clarity when events don't unfold according to your expectations, it indicates a deficiency in Titiksha.

Having Titiksha signifies possessing patience and enduring tolerance. To keep equilibrium and handle undesirable circumstances that occur in life - this indicates the presence of Titiksha.

Imagine that someone is extremely rude to you. Without Titiksha, your mind becomes unsettled and you lose your equilibrium. With Titiksha, you can proceed and maintain equilibrium.

### Key Aspects of Titiksha
1. Patience with Suffering: Titiksha involves accepting pain or difficulty with a calm mind. This does not mean avoiding or denying suffering, but rather accepting that suffering is a part of life and enduring it with equanimity.
2. Endurance in Adversity: It emphasizes the ability to face adversities, challenges, and obstacles without losing one's sense of purpose, self-control, or emotional balance.
3. Emotional Resilience: By cultivating Titiksha, an individual can maintain emotional stability, even in difficult or emotionally charged situations, allowing them to respond rather than react impulsively.
4. Spiritual Discipline: Titiksha is seen as a form of spiritual discipline, where the practitioner consciously works to endure difficulties without becoming attached to them or letting them disturb the peace of the mind.
5. A Tool for Liberation: In many spiritual traditions, including Hinduism, Titiksha is viewed as a necessary quality for

achieving moksha (liberation) because it helps the practitioner detach from the external world, reduces suffering caused by attachment, and fosters a steady mind.

### Titiksha in Practice
- Titiksha is often practiced through meditation, yoga, and mindfulness, where one learns to endure physical discomfort or mental restlessness while remaining focused and grounded.
- It is also a vital quality in dealing with external situations, such as facing criticism, loss, or failure, with a calm and steady deméanor.
In the Bhagavad Gita, for example, titiksha is encouraged as part of the discipline of self-control, showing how one can cultivate inner peace amidst the inevitable challenges of life.

Thus, Titiksha is the spiritual and mental endurance that enables a person to remain steadfast in the face of difficulties, fostering a sense of inner strength and resilience.

## Vairagya (Detachment)
The term vairagya in Sanskrit translates to "detachment." It's a condition of being disconnected from the physical realm. It can also be described as a mental condition in which all ties to the material world are relinquished. Vairagya is associated with releasing emotions such as pride, ego, aversion, feelings of inferiority and superiority, false identities, and fear.

Detachment (vairagya) holds great importance in Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism. Detachment signifies a deficiency in physical or mental connection to the world's objects, your own mind and body, plus your achievements, characteristics, reputation, name, and social standing.

Clinging to material possessions hampers your thinking and leads you to overlook important truths in life. Upon becoming connected, you lose your balance and search for elements that can harm you both physically and spiritually, hindering your progress and achievements. Every habit you form is simply a strong kind of connection. Anything that matters to you, whether it's an individual, a job, a religion, a community, or a circumstance, is a bond. Together, they tie you to this

life and iurn your existence into a major battle since you find it hard to release attachments and progress.

Detachment can help you adjust your expectations and protect you from the emotional impact of setbacks and failures in your personal and professional life. It will also help you think more clearly and make improved decisions by alleviating worry and anxiety from your mind. Focusing on your performance instead of the outcomes can also enhance your likelihood of success. Crucially, detachment enables you to maintain an open mindset and quickly gain insights from your errors.

Consequently, if you desire comfort, practice releasing attachments. Avoid becoming overly attached to your relationships or getting too deeply involved in them. In your relationships, it's often wise to maintain a bit of distance and give individuals some space to breathe. Take all required actions to reach your goals, irrespective of what might happen or not. Focus on your responsibilities, what needs to be completed and the methods to achieve it, and put in your best effort, trusting the results to God, the Supreme Self. Approaching situations with ease, not treating life too earnestly, and practicing detachment are three essential initial steps that can assist you in managing your emotions, life, and relationships effectively.

## Viveka (Conscience)
In Hindu philosophy, Viveka refers to the "discriminative power" or "intellectual discernment." It is the ability to distinguish between what is real and what is unreal, between the permanent and the transient, and between what leads to liberation and what leads to bondage. In a more spiritual sense, Viveka is considered the ability to use one's intellect to make wise decisions and to follow a path of truth and righteousness.

In the context of Conscience, Viveka can be seen as the inner faculty that helps a person determine right from wrong. It is the voice within that guides an individual in moral and ethical matters, helping them act in alignment with their higher self or divine nature. In this sense, Viveka is a mental tool that sharpens the ability to discern one's duties, actions, and the consequences of one's choices, fostering a life of wisdom and virtue.

While Viveka is primarily associated with the intellectual aspect f the mind. it has a moral dimension as well. It leads to the purification f the mind. which in turn allows a person to follow the path of ighteousncss (Dharma) and seek ultimate liberation (Moksha). Thus, Viveka as conscience is not just about knowledoe or intellectual judgment but also about inner clarity and the ability to align one's actions with higher principles. The key characteristics of conscience include:

1. Moral Awareness: Conscience helps individuals recognize moral and ethical obligations, even in complex or ambiguous situations.
2. Self-Reflection: Conscience allows individuals to reflect on their actions, intentions, and the consequences of their choices, leading to self-assessment and improvement.
3. Guilt and Remorse: When people act against their ethical standards, their conscience may cause feelings of guilt or remorse, encouraging them to make amends or avoid repeating the wrongdoing.
4. Guidance for Decision Making: Conscience serves as a guide in making decisions, especially when faced with moral dilemmas or conflicting values.
5. Development through Experience: The sense of conscience often evolves with experience, education, and personal growth. People learn from their mistakes and from the guidance of society, culture, religion, or philosophy to refine their moral judgment.
