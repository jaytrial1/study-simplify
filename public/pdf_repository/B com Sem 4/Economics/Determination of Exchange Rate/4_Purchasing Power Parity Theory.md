4. Purchasing Power Parity Theory

When the two countries are on inconvertible paper currency standard, the rate of exchange is determined according to the respective purchasing power of the two currencies. The theory explaining this phenomenon is known as the purchasing power parity theory, which was first stated by John Wheatley in 1802 in his book 'Remarks on Currency and Commerce'. It was further developed by writers like William Blake, David Ricardo, Thornton, etc. However, the theory in its modern form has been systematically developed by the eminent Swedish economist Gustav Cassel in his illustrious book 'Money and Foreign Exchange After 1914'.

The basic idea underlying the purchasing power parity theory is that every unit of currency possesses a certain degree of purchasing power, and therefore, when the domestic currency of a country is exchanged against a foreign currency, what happens in fact is that the domestic purchasing power is exchanged for foreign purchasing power. It follows, therefore, that the main factor determining the exchange rate is the relative purchasing power of the two currencies. In other words, it is the parity of the purchasing powers of the two currencies which determines the rate of exchange.

# Statement of the Theory
According to the purchasing power parity theory, when the two countries are on inconvertible paper currency standard, the rate of exchange is determined by the ratio of the respective purchasing powers of the two currencies. The rate of exchange tends to rest at that point where the respective purchasing powers of the two currencies are equal. In the words of Cassel,
"In the case of countries on inconvertible paper currency standard, the basic rate of exchange between them is determined by the purchasing power - the ratio between the purchasing powers of two currencies in their respective home markets. The actual rate of exchange at any time may move away from the purchasing power parity due to influences of demand and supply for foreign currency, but the purchasing power par is the point towards which the rate will constantly tend to move and at which it must ultimately come to rest."

Let us take an illustration. Suppose that a certain amount of goods and services can be purchased in America for \$10 and the same amount of goods and services can be purchased in India for Rs. 600/- This means that the purchasing power of \$10 is equal to the purchasing power of Rs. 600. The rate of exchange between dollar and rupee, therefore, will be \$1 = Rs. 60. It should be noted here that the internal purchasing power of a currency depends upon the price level prevailing in the country. If there is a rise in the price level, the purchasing power of the currency will fall and vice-versa.

The rate of exchange, thus, determined in relation to price level is known as the purchasing power parity. This is a norm round which the actual rate of exchange will vary. So long as the relationship between the two price levels remains unchanged, the rate of exchange will tend towards parity. But it should be noted that this parity is not a fixed par like the gold points, it is a moving par - changing with changes in price levels. Changes in the price levels or in the purchasing power of currencies are measured and compared with the help of index numbers.

# Two Versions of the Theory
There'are two versions of the purchasing power parity theory - (i) Absolute Version (ii) Relative Version.

## Absolute Version of the Theory
The absolute version of the theory indicates as how the rate of exchange between the two currencies is determined at any particular time. According to this version, the exchange rate will be determined at that point where the internal purchasing power of the two currencies become equal. Thus, for example, if a certain amount of goods and services can be purchased for one dollar in America and for Rs. 60/- in India, then according to this version of the theory, the rate of exchange between dollar and rupee will be \$1 = Rs. 60, because at this point, the internal purchasing power of the two currencies is equal.

If, however, the market rate of exchange is above or below this par, market forces will come into operation and will bring back the exchange rate once again to its norm. Suppose, for example, the rate of exchange in the open market moves to \$1 = Rs. 65, this means that the purchasing power of dollar in terms of rupee has increased. This will induce people in America to convert their dollars into rupees and import goods from India because it is profitable for them to do so. The result of this will be that the demand for rupees in America will increase, while at the same time the supply of rupees in America will decline. Consequently, the value of rupee in terms of dollar will increase until it reaches the par, that is, \$1 = Rs. 60. This process will move in the reverse direction, if the rate of exchange moves to say \$1 = Rs. 50.

It should be noted here that the absolute version of the theory is based on the assumption that the prices of goods in the two countries do not change. But this, however, is not the case. In real life, the price level or the

internal purchasing power of the currency changes and this brings about a change in the rate of exchange. The limitation of the absolute version of the theory is that it does not take this point into consideration.

## Relative Version of the Theory
The relative version of the purchasing power parity theory has been given by Gustav Cassel. It indicates the effect of changes in the domestic price level on the rate of exchange and the determination of the new exchange rate. Under the absolute version of the theory, the purchasing power of the two currencies is explained with reference to only one commodity, where as under the relative version, the internal purchasing power of the two currencies is considered with reference to a number of goods and services. Thus, the theory in its relative version states that changes in the rate of exchange are governed by changes in the ratio of the respective purchasing powers of the two currencies.

Here, some past exchange rate is adopted at the base rate and changes therein are measured by the ratio of price indices of the respective countries. In other words, according to the relative version of the theory, when the price level in both the countries changes, the new exchange rate will be the old base rate multiplied by the quotient between the degrees of change in the purchasing power of the two currencies.

This can be expressed symbolically as follows:

$$
R_{1}=R o \times \frac{P a_{0}}{P a_{1}} \times \frac{P b_{1}}{P b_{0}}
$$

Here, $R_{1}$ is the new exchange rate

$$
R_{0} \text { is base rate }
$$

$\mathrm{Pa}_{\mathrm{o}}$ is price index of country A (home country) in the base period
$\mathrm{Pa}_{1}$ is price index of country A in the current period.
$\mathrm{Pb}_{\mathrm{o}}$ is price index of country B (foreign country) in the base period.
$\mathrm{Pb}_{1}$ is price index of country B in the current period.
Suppose, for example, the rate of exchange between American dollar and Indian rupee in the base period 1991 is \$1 = Rs. 25 and the price indices in both the countries are 100. Now, if in 2014 the price index in America rises upto 200 and in India it goes upto 500, then the new rate of exchange will be :

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{1} & =25 \times \frac{100}{200} \times \frac{500}{100} \\
& =25 \times \frac{5}{2}=62 \cdot 50
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus, the new exchange rate will be \$1 = Rs. 62 · 50

The purchasing power parity theory has been criticised on the following
grounds :
(1) The theory provides an explanation of the changes in the rate of
exchange and not the process of exchange rate determination:
It has been said that the purchasing power parity theory cannot be said
to be a theory of exchange rate determination, because it does not explain
how exchange rate is determined; it simply states how and why there are
changes in the rate of exchange. The theory only attempts to state that
changes in the internal purchasing power of the currency lead to changes in
the exchange rate, but it fails to explain how exchange rate is determined.
As a matter of fact, exchange rate determination is a part of the process of
value theory and like the value of any other commodity, the rate of exchange
is determined by the demand for and supply of currency. The theory,
however, confines itself only to the purchasing power of currency and tends
to ignore the demand and supply of currency.
(2) Comparisons of the purchasing power of two currencies is a difficult task :
In the second place, in order to compare the purchasing power of two currencies, we have to measure changes in the price level of the two countries which can be done with the help of index numbers. But the problem is, which index numbers should be constructed for this purpose - wholesale price index or cost of living index. Besides, there are certain serious limitations of the techniques of index numbers. They are not a reliable guide and do not provide a correct picutre of changes in the purchasing power of currencies. Moreover, international comparison of the price level is really a very difficult task because the articles used in daily consumption by persons and groups in different countries are in a large number of cases different form one another.

In other words, the difficulty consists in the virtual impossibility of a comparison of price levels as they relate not to identical goods but to different goods.
(3) The theory compares the general price level in the two countries without making any distinction between the price level of domestic goods and the price level of internationally traded goods :
It should be noted that only the internationally traded goods will influence the demand for and supply of foreign currency and consequently only the internationally traded goods, the theory becomes an empty truism, because the prices of internationally traded goods will tend to be the same 

level. There have been cases where exchange rate changes in the first instance for some reasons and, later on brings about changes in the price levels in the two countries. Thus, for example, during the initial stages of inflation, the rising price level tends to affect the exchange rate, but when once inflation has gathered momentum, exchange rate to a large extent affects the price level. Halm has also said that 'The national price levels follow rather than precede the movements of exchange rates :
(6) The theory takes into account only the balance of trade and not the over all balance of payments situation of a country:
It has been said that the theory takes into consideration only the visible items of exports and imports that is, merchandise trade - as factors affecting the demand for and supply of foreign currency, But in fact, apart from visible items, invisible items also affect the exchange rate. Thus, for example, items like shipping, banking and insurance transactions, payment of interest, capital movements etc. although are very little affected by changes in the general price levels, but these items do influence the exchange rates by acting upon the supply and demand for foreign currencies; And their influence may drive the rates in a different way from those shown by a comparison of index numbers. These are important determinants of exchange rate, but the purchasing power patiry theory ignores these factors altogether.
(7) The theory provides an explanation of exchange rate determination in the long run:
The purchasing power parity theory offers an explanation as to how the rate of exchange is determined in the long run. It, however, fails to explain the short term and the day to day fluctuations in the exchange rate. As Paul Einzig points out, the theory deals with the ultimate rather than the immediate process determining the rate of exchange.
(8) Even in the long run, it is doubtful whether the theory can explain the equilibrium rate of exchange, because in order to calulate the new equilibrium rate, we must know the base rate, that is, the old equilibrium rate. But it is very difficult to as certain the equilibrium rate which actually prevailed between the two currencies. As Scammell has put it, 'the theory is a very inadequate tool for calculating the equilibrium rate of exchange.
(9) The theory ignores the influence of terms of trade on the exchange rate.
According to Kindleberger, the terms of trade is an important facter which influences the rate-of exchange, Likewise, Mrs. Robinson and Maclap have also corroborated this view and said that changes in the balance of payments bring about changes in the demarid for and supply of foreign exchange and thereby affect the rate of exchange.

(10) The theory ignores the influence of reciprocal demand and capital movements:
According to Keynes, the two main shortcomings of the purchasing power parity theory are that it fails to take into consideration the influence of reciprocal demand and capital movement on the rate of exchange. In his veiw, foreign exchange rates are determined not only by price movements but also by the elasticities of reciprocal demand and by international movements of capital.
(11) The theory applies at best only to current account transactions, neglecting capital account completely :
Another drawback of the theory is that it fails to take into consideration many items in the balance of payments, other than the merchandise trade. In other words, the theory, at best, is applicable only to current account transactions, neglecting capital account completly. According to Kindleberger, the purchasing power parity theory is designed for trader nations and is of little guidance to country like Switzerland which is both a trader and a banker.
(12) The theory assumes free trade and absence of exchange control for a steady exchange rate based on purchasing power parity :
In reality, however, State interventrion in the free flow of international trade such as export duties, import duties, import quotas, import licensing and exchange control devices etc. cause a permanent deviation from the rate of exchange determined by relative price level. Likewise, temporary deviation from the purchasing power parity may also occur on account of the operations of speculators or due to movements of capital caused by panic.

It was on account of these short-comings and drawbacks of the theory that Benham considered it a 'dubious guide' to practical action in the field of foreign exchange.
