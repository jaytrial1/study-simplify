4. Measures to Correct Disequilibrium in the Balance of Payments

The various measures to correct disequilibrium in the balance of payments can be studied under two broad headings :
(A) Monetary measures (B) Non-monetary measures.

# Monetary Measures:

Monetary measures to correct an adverse balance of payments include the following:

## (1) Deflation or Currency Contraction

Deflation is a classical remedy to correct an adverse balance: it implies contraction of domestic currency through dear money and credit policy, which results in the fall in the costs and prices of domestic goods. It has been held that such a policy, on the one hand, would encourage exports because of a fall in the prices in the foreign markets and, on the other hand, reduce imports because of a fall in the money incomes of the people, which reduces their propensity to import. Thus, a policy of deflation by encouraging exports and restricting imports helps the country to correct its adverse balance of payments.

**Limitations:** There are, however, certain limitations of this policy:

(i) A policy of deflation can be gainfully employed only when countries are on gold standard or on fixed exchange rates, because its workability assumes that exchange rates do not change during its course.
(ii) The degree of deflation necessary to correct an adverse balance of payments depends on the elasticity of demand for exports and imports.
(iii) A more serious limitation of this policy is that it achieves equilibrium in the balance of payments through unemployment, wage-cuts, income-deflation and drastic price fall. A policy of currency contraction may pose a serious economic problem for the country and may even lead to a slump in the economy resulting unacceptable to the policy makers. Thus, deflation, as a remedy to correct an adverse balance of payments may prove worse than the disease and as such has been described as an 'anchronism' in modern times.

## (2) Devaluation

Devaluation implies a deliberate and official reduction in the external value of the currency of the country in relation to the currencies of all the countries or of a few selected countries and/or in terms of gold.

It would be here interesting to note the difference between devaluation of currency and depreciation of currency. A policy of currency depreciation is also indicative of a fall in the external value of a country's currency; However, it is not officially announced but is influenced and determined by the market forces of demand and supply; while devaluation implies official reduction in the external value of a currency, moreover such reduction is in a certain definite proportion. In other words, devaluation of a currency is in a certain definite proportion and is announced officially by the government or the monetary authority of the country, while depreciation of currency is influenced by the free play of market forces. Thus, while devaluation is a reduction of the external value of currency as arbitrarily decided upon by the government, depreciation stands for automatic reduction in the external value of a country's currency by market forces.

Devaluation of currency helps the country to increase its exports by making them cheaper in the foreign markets and in reducing imports by making them expensive in the domestic market. In other words, currency devaluation by a country encourages its exports by lowering the price of its export products in the international markets and by raising the prices of its imported products in the domestic markets. Devaluation is, thus, reflected in expansion of exports and contraction of imports and thereby it tends to exercise a favourable impact on the balance of payments of a country.

It should be noted here that currency devaluation as a measure to correct an adverse balance of payments has been quite often used by the different countries of the world at different times. Thus, for example, during the great economic depression of the thirties of the last century, a number of countries like England, America, etc had devalued their currencies to promote their exports. In 1949 and in 1967 England had devalued her pound-sterling and in 1972 America hand devalued her dollar. likewise, India too had devalued her rupee in September 1949, in June 1966 and in July 1991.

### Essential Conditions for the Success of Devaluation as a Measure to Correct an Adverse Balance of Payments
The success of devaluation as a technique for correcting an adverse balance of payments depends on the following main factors:

#### Elasticity of Demand for Exports and Imports
If the demand for exports and imports of a country which has devalued its currency is fairly elastic, then this measure will prove to be beneficial to the country; but if its demand for exports and imports is relatively inelastic, devaluation will worsen its balance of payments situation by increasing the total value of its imports while at the same time reducing the total value of its exports.

#### Absence of Counter-Devaluation Measures
The efficacy of devaluation measure adopted by a country will also depend upon the extent of counter devaluation measures taken by other countries. If other nations also devalue their currencies in the same or greater proportion, then the gain to the country devaluing its currency will be nullified. It should be noted here that competitive devaluation does not help any country, instead it creates uncertainty in the sphere of international trade and tends to reduce its volume. During the period of the great economic depression of the thirties of the last century, a number of countries of the world had entered into competitive devaluation, which is known as 'beggar thyneighbour' policy and almost all of them had a bitter experience of the same.

#### Constant Cost-Price Structure
The success of devaluation requires that there will be no change in the structure of costs and prices in the country which devalues its currency. If there is a rise in cost and prices, the beneficial effects of devaluation will be lost, because as a result of an increase in cost and prices, export prices too will rise and so the measure would prove to be ineffective. Not only this, failure to maintain relative stability in costs and prices again necessitates devaluation.

And frequent currency devaluation causes a loss of confidence in the currency of that country which may ultimately lead to flight of capital from that country.

#### Extent of Import-content in Exports
One of the objectives of devaluation is to encourage exports and for this it is necessary that the domestic production of export goods must increase. But if the import content say like raw materials, spare parts and components etc in the production of export goods is quite substantial, it will tend to increase the cost of production of export goods by making these import contents expensive and thereby nullify the advantages of devaluation.

#### Availabillty of Exportable Surplus
In order that devaluation may prove to be effective in achieving its objectives, it is equally essentiail y that the supply of exportable surplus should be fairly elastic. If exportable surplus is not available in adequate quantity, curency devaluation will not be beneficial. Besides, it is also essential that the exportable surplus must be of standard quality and made available at competitive prices in the international markets.

#### Level of Money Incomes in the Foreign Countries
To what extent curency devaluation will help in increasing exports also depends on the level of money incomes abroad. Many a times, it so happens that the exports of the country which devalues its currency, are more income - eleastic than price - eleastic. In such cases, rise in exports would depend more on the level of income than the price factor. Thus, for example, devaluation may cause a fall in the prices of export products, but if the level of money incomes in the importing country does not show any improvement, devaluation may not turn out to be so beneficial.

#### Inflation
Normally, devaluation breeds inflation; this is because, on the one hand, there is a rise in the price of imports and on the other hand increase in exports due to devaluation creates shortage of such products in the domestic market which leads to a rise in their prices. Thus, devaluation causes inflation and if it is not kept under control, it is likely to offset the benefits of devaluation. Not only this, continuance of inflationary pressures in the economy in the post - devaluation period may further necessitate devaluation. Looked at from this point of view, it may be said that devaluation is both a cause and effect of inflation.'

Besides, devaluation also increases the burden of external debt; a country with a large foreign debt has to bear the brunt of devaluation in the form of high burden of its external debt.

In short, the success or otherwise of devaluation as a measure to correct an adverse balance of payments depends on the interaction of the various factors discussed above and the efficacy of the follow-up measures initiated in the post-devaluation period.

## (3) Exchange Control
Another important monetary measure to correct disequilibrium in the balance of payments is exchange control. It refers to government regulation of exchange rate as well as restriction on the conversion of local currency into foreign currency. Under the system of exchange control all the exporters of the country have to surrender their earnings of foreign exchange to the central bank in exchange for domestic currency at a predetermined rate. The foreign exchange, then, is rationed out amongst the licensed importers on the basis of some predetermined scheme of priority. Thus, under exchange control system, the government acquires full command over the entire foreign exchange earnings of the country. Usually, licensing and import quota system are integral parts of exchange control regime. The aim of exchange control is to bring about an equality between the demand for and supply of foreign exchange through state intervention and control. The balance of payments is sought to be rectified by keeping imports well within the limits of export earnings and foreign exchange reserves.

**Limitations:**

The following are the main limitations of exchange control measures:
(a) Exchange control system deals with an adverse balance of payments by suppressing the deficit which is only a symptom of the disease; it does not go to the root of the problem. In other words, exchange control measures deal only with the deficit in the balance of payments and not its causes.
(b) Exchange controls cannot increase the supply of foreign exchange. All that they can do is to allocate scarce foreign exchange resources amongst the various competing sectors. But such an allocation may prove to be arbitrary and may not be in the best interests of the country.
(c) A system of exchange control adversely affects free trade and thus restricts the advantages of international trade and specialisation cost.
(d) Exchange control measures tend to divert the flow of international trade from its natural course to artificial course, because these measures do not allow the free operation of the principle of comparative cost.
(e) These measures create uncertainty and thereby lead to a shrinkage in the volume of international trade. In short, exchange control deficit in the balance of payments. At best, these measures can be fundamental adjustments are made to restore equilibrium in the balance of payments.

# Non-monetary Measures
Non-monetary measures to correct an adverse balance of payments

## (1) Export Promotion Measures
In order to remove the deficit in the balance of payments, it is necessary piscase exports. The following measures may be suggested to this effect:
(i) Reduction in export duties and offer of various tax concessions and tax incentives.
(ii) Making provision for cash assistance, cash subsidies and bouties for exports.
(iii) Concession in railway freights to export goods.
(iv) Technical upgradation and modernisation of export industries this will reduce cost of production and improve the quality of export goods.
(v) Adequate and timely availability of the imports of capital equipment and other inputs need for the export industries.
(vi) Special emphasis on market research, market surveys and other sales promotion measures.
(vii) Greater awareness of quality control in respect of export products.
(viii) Participation in international fairs and exhibitions.

## (2) Measures to Restrict Imports
It is equally essential to reduce imports in order to meet with the deficit in the balance of payments. The following measures may be suggested to restrict imports:
(i) Prohibition of imports of less essential items: In the context of an adverse balance of payment situation, the goverment may impose a total ban on the imports of less essential items like luxurious and semi-luxurious goods.
(ii) Imposition of new import duties and increasing the rates of existing import duties: This will make imported goods more expensive and in course of time will lead to contraction in their demand.
(iii) Fixation of import quotas: Under this system the government may fix and permit the maximum quantity or value of a commodity to be imported during - given period. By restricting imports through the quota system, the deficit in the balance of payments is sought to be reduced or eliminated.
