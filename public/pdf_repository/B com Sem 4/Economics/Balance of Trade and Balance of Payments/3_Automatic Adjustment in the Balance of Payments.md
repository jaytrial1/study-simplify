3. Automatic Adjustment in the Balance of Payments

We have seen earlier that the balance of payments of a country always balances. The deficit, if any, in the balance of payments on current account is made good out of capital account. However, if a country cannot get long term capital and investment to meet its deficit on current account, in that case then, it has to take special measures to correct its adverse balance, But it has been said that even if no such measures are taken, the balance of payments tends to balance itself automatically. This automatic adjustment, according to the classical view, is brought about through price changes, while according to Keynes and other writers, it is brought about through income changes.

# Automatic Adjustment through Price Changes
David Ricardo and other classical writers held the view that automatic adjustment in the balance of payments of a country was brought through price changes. According to them, under the international good standard mechanism, disequilibrium in the balance of payments was automatically corrected through free international movements of gold. Thus, for example, if there was a persistent deficit in the balance of payments of a country on the current account, then under the gold standard, it would be obliged to make payments through export of gold. Now, since the quantity of money under the gold standard was directly related to the stock of gold with the country (that is, with the monetary authority), the country exporting gold had to contract or reduce the quantity of money. This reduction in money supply led to a fall in price level which encouraged exports and discouraged imports and thereby restoring the balance in the balance of payments.

The above process worked in a reverse direction in the case of a surplus country having a favourable balance which led to an inflow of gold. The increase in the quantity of gold reserves led to an increase in the quantity of money and consequently increase in the price level. The rise in price level caused reduction in exports and increase in imports thus wiping off the surplus in the balance of payments and restoring equilibrium.

In short, according to the classical view, free international movements of gold through its effects on the supply of money and the price level brought the necessary adjustments in the economy and restored equilibrium in the balance of payments of a country.

Limitations
The above classical analysis of automatic adjustment in the balance of payments through price changes suffers from the following main limitations:

(i) The analysis assumed automatic relationship between movements of gold and money supply. Thus, for example, an increase in the quantity of gold reserves through imports caused an increase in money supply and a reduction in gold reserves through exports caused a decrease in money supply. But this need not necessarily happen. The central bank could keep the increased gold reserves as idle and inactive and may not increase the money supply; in the reverse case, the central bank may not reduce the money supply due to fall in the stock of gold reserves. Under these circumstances, gold movements failed to exercise any effect on the quantity of money and the price level.
(ii) The classical analysis assumed the validity of the quantity theory of money, that is to say, an increase in the quantity of money led to an increase in price level and vice versa. But we know that the cause and effect relationship between the quantity of money and price level is very loose, very uncertain and dangerous to depend upon.
(iii) The classical analysis also assumed that changes in the gold stock and in the quantity of money influence only the price level and the cost of production. But in fact changes in gold reserves and money supply also influence the volume of investment, output, employment and income; this has been ignored in the classical analysis.

# Automatic Adjustment through Income Changes
According to Keynes and other writers, automatic adjustment in the balance of payments of a country is brought about not through price changes but through income changes. The incomes of the different sectors of the economy of a country are inter - dependent on each other. Thus, for example, incomes in the industrial sector are dependent on the agricultural sector; likewise income from foreign trade affects agricultural and industrial incomes. In other words, changes in the income of one sector of the economy brings about changes in the other sectors, because the incomes derived by the different sectors are inter - related and inter dependent on each other.

Now, suppose if there is a surplus in the balance of payments of a country, it implies that there is an increse in the employment and income of the factors enagaged in export industries. A part of this increased income will be spent on the domestic goods and a part of it on the imported goods. How much of the increased income shall be spent on imported goods will depend on the marginal propensity to import. Thus, a rise in exports leads to a rise in the level of employment and income in the export sector which tends to an increase in imports. The Keynesians point out that the increase in the over all money incomes of the different sectors of the economy will be many times more than the original increase in the export sector because of multiplier and acceleration process, and therefore the total increase in imports too will be much more than the original imports. Thus, an increase in exports causes an increase in imports through income changes which ultimately wipes out the surplus in the balance of payments and restores its equilibrium position.

Likewise, in the case of a country having deficit in its balance of payments, the process works in the reverse direction. Thus, for example, a fall in exports leads to a fall in employment and income of the factors engaged in export industries. This affects the incomes of the other sectors of the economy, because as said earlier, incomes derived by the different sectors are interdependent on each other. Thus, there is an over-all decline in the money incomes in the entire economy; this downward trend in income leads to a fall in the demand for imports which ultimately wipes out the deficit in the balance of payments and restores its euilibrium position.

In this way, according to Keynesians, equilibrium in the balance of payments of a country is brought about through income changes.

It should, however, be noted that both the classical and the Keynesian versions of automatic adjustment mechanism are complementary approaches. The actual adjustment in the balance of payments is attained through both the price changes and the income changes.

The pertinent question now is that if the automatic adjustment through price changes and income changes brings about equilibrium in the balance of payments of a country, then why does a country need to adopt a deliberate policy and special measures to correct its adverse balance? There are three main reasons for it:

(a) When equilibrium in the balance of payments is brought about through automatic adjustment, it affects the level of employment and income in the economy. It is to prevent a fall in employment and income that special measures are needed.
(b) Reduction in imports during the process of automatic adjustment may not be in the best interests of the country. It is just possible that instead of non-essential imports, essential items of import may fall and if this happens, the country will have to face various other difficulties. Particularly, in the context of planned economic development, it is essential to regulate imports according to set picorities and therefore special measures are adopted to correct an adverse balance.
(c) Besides, the process of automatic adjustment is so lengthy time consuming - painful and full of hazards that no country would be willing to completly rely on it for correcting its adverse balance. Hence, there is the need for deliberate policy measures to correct an adverse balance of payments.
