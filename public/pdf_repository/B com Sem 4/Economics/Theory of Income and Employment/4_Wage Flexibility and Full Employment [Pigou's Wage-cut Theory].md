The classical theory of employment was evolved and developed on the basis of Say's law of markets. Pigou—the chief exponent of the classical theory—interpreted Say's law in terms of supply of and demand for labour. A freely competitive economy, according to the classical economists, ensures full employment of labour. Full employment indicates absence of involuntary unemployment. If some workers are found to be unemployed, competition for jobs will force wages down. And if workers are prepared to accept lower wages, employers will be induced to engage more labour in place of other factors of production. This means that substitution of labour for other factors will eventually lead to full employment. Thus, according to classical writers, wage flexibility will ensure full employment equilibrium in a free economy.

In this context, Pigou has tried to explain the existence of unemployment in the economy in terms of wage rigidity. According to him, the main causes of unemployment are factors that prevent appropriate wage adjustments; for example, the trade unions may not agree to accept lower wages for their member workers or the government may also come in the way of acceptance of lower wages by labour through minimum wage legislation. A complete flexible wage rate will remove fluctuations of employment. To quote him, "With perfectly free competition, there will always be at work a strong tendency for wage rates to be so related to demand that everybody is employed. The implication is that such unemployment as exists at any time is due wholly to the fact that changes in demand conditions are continuously taking place and that frictional resistence prevents the appropriate wage adjustments from being made simultaneously." According to Pigou, therefore, the economy will tend towards full employment of labour via wage cut, whatever may be the state of demand for labour.

Pigou, thus, holds the view that a general wage-cut will ensure full employment of labour. Wage reduction will ultimately lead to fall in prices and interest. The fall in prices consequent on wage-cut will increase the real value or purchasing power of cash balances with the people. This will lead to an increase in effective demand which will stimulate cash-balance effect.'

The following are the main points of criticisms levelled by Keynes and the classical theory and Pigou's wage-cut policy:

1.  Keynes does not agree with the Say's law of market that supply centers its own demand. All incomes earned by the factors of production during the process of producton need not necessarily be spent on purchasing goods. A part of the income may be saved too. Besides, income which is not spent on consumption goods by the consumers need not necessarily be spent on investment goods by producers.

2.  According to Keynes, the classical assumption of full employment is highly unrealistic. The classicists considered full employment as a normal phenomenon, while Keynes regarded it as a rare phenomenon. According to Keynes, under-employment equilibrium seems to be more realistic.

3.  Equilibrium between saving and investment is not brought about by changes in the rate of interest but through changes in the level of income.

4.  Keynes has criticised wages-cut theory of Pigou on both theoretical and practical grounds.

5.  On theoretical ground, Keynes observed that a general wage-cut would reduce the purchasing power in the hands of the workers which means a cut in their consumption, that is, the aggregate effective demand for goods and commodities will decline. This will obviously lead to decline in production and employment. Thus, a policy of general wage-cut in stead of increasing employment would reduce the volume of employment. According to Keynes, unemployment cannot be removed by cutting down wages but by increasing the effective demand for goods and commodities.

6.  On practical count, Keynes points out that labour unions are an integral part of the modern industrial system and they would certainly resist a wage-cut policy. Besides, in a modern Welfare State, the government prescribes welfare legislation regarding minimum-wage and unemployment insurance for the working class. In this connection Dillard very rightly remarks, "it is bad politics even if it should be considered good economics to object to labour unions and to liberal labour legislation." Thus, in modern times it is not feasible to implement a policy of wage-cut as a cure for removing unemployment.

Thus, viewed as a whole, according to Keynes a policy of wage-cut instead of attaining full employment in the economy is more likely to result in increasing unemployment. Later on, however, Pigou too had accepted this view and held that a policy to increase effective demand is superior to a policy of wage-cut. To qutoe him, "I am in favour of attacking the problem of unemployment by manipulating demand rather than by manipulating wages" [Pigou: Lapses from Full Employment]
