## 3. Keynes' Psychological Law of Consumption

The functional relationship between consumption and income has been explained by Keynes in the form of a law. This law is based on empirical observation and is validated by empirical data. Since the law has its roots in the psychology of human behaviour, it is known as the psychological law of consumption. Keynes states this law as, "The normal psychological law holds that when the real income of the community increases or decreases, its consumption will increase or decrease, but not so fast."

Thus, as income increases, consumption increases but by less than the increase in income. The proprotion of income spent on the purchase of goods and services diminishes as income increases. In other words, as income increases, consumption also increases but not in the same proportion as increase in income, and vice-versa. This is so, because as one adds to one's consumption with expansion of one's income, he comes closer to the point of saturation beyond which consumption is less likely to be stretched. To put it differently, we may also say that the proportion of income saved increases as income increases.

It will thus, be seen that Keynes' law of consumption is related to three propositions :-

(a) When the total income of a community increases, its consumption expenditure also increases, but by a somewhat smaller amount. Thus, as income increases, consumption expenditure also increases but not in the same proportion in which income increases; that is to say, the proportion of income saved increases with an increase in income.
(b) It follows from this that an increase in income will be divided in some proportion between spending and saving.
(c) An increase in income will, thus, lead to an increase in both consumption and saving. It is unlikely that an increase in income will lead to either less spending or less saving than before, Thus, rising income will often be accompanied by increased saving and falling income by decreased saving.

# Assumptions of Keynes Psychological Law of Consumption

The law of consumption given by Keynes is based on the following assumptions :
(i) The existing psychological and institutional complex influencing consumption expenditure remains constant. In other words, it is assumed that in the short run, habits, tastes, customs, price level, size of population, pattern of income distribution etc. do not change and that consumption expenditure depends on income only.
(ii) The conditions in general are normal and that the economy is not faced with any abnormal or extraordinary circumstances such as war, floods or famines, inflation, etc.
(iii) The existence of a wealthy capitalistic economy based on laissezfaire policy, that is, it is a free economy in which there are no government restrictions on consumption and savings.

# Implications of the Law
The following are some of the main implications of the law :
(a) The law invalidates Say's law of markets. According to the law of consumption, as income increases, consumption also increases, but not in the same proportion; it increases at a slower rate. In other words, demand does not increase to the same extent as output Hence, it is not correct to say that supply creates its own demand.
(b) The law underlines the importance of investment in any programme of full employment. Since the proportionate increase in consumption is less than increase in income, there arises a gap between increase in income and increase in consumption. This gap is to be filled by increasing the volume of investment or else it will lead to depression and unemployment.

(c) The law also underlines the need for State intervention. Since consumption increases in less proportion than income, there arises a situation of over-production and general unemployment. State intervention, therefore, becomes necessary to avert over production and mass unemployment through appropriate monetary and fiscal policies.
(d) The law also explains the turning points of a business cycle. The downward turning point from a boom is explained by the fact that although people's income increases, the consumption expenditure does not increase in the same proportion. Likewise, the upper turning point from a depression is explained by the fact that although people's income decreases the consumption expenditure cannot be reduced in the same proportion; a minimum level of consumption expenditure has to be incurred.
