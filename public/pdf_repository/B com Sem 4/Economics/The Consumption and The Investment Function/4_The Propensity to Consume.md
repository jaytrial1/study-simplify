The propensity to consume or the consumption function expresses a relationship between two variables—income and consumption. Actually it is called the schedule of propensity to consume and indicates the proportion of total income that is spent on consumption at various levels of income. If consumption is represented by C and income by Y, then the propensity to consume = C(Y). Thus, as said earlier, consumption is a function of income. The propensity to consume indicates a functional relationship between income and consumption.

# Difference Between Consumption and Propensity to Consume

It would be useful here to note the difference between consumption and propensity to consume (consumption function). Consumption means the amount spent on consumption at a given level of income, while consumption function or the propensity to consume refers to consumption expenditure at various levels of income. Thus, for example, out of a total income of Rs. 5,000 crores, if Rs. 4,000 crores are spent on consumption, then Rs. 4,000 crores represent consumption. But if we draw up a consumption schedule showing changes in consumption as a result of changes in income, then this schedule is called consumption function or the propensity to consume. In short, consumption means amount spent on consumption at a given level of income, while propensity to consume refers to change in the level of consumption expenditure as a result of change in income.

# Types of Propensity to Consume

The propensity to consume is of two types :
(1) Average propensity to consume (2) Marginal propensity to consume


## **Average Propensity to Consume**

The average propensity to consume may be defined as the ratio of total consumption to total income. If C denotes consumption and Y denotes income, then :

average Propensity to Consume (APC) = $\frac{\mathrm{C}}{\mathrm{Y}}$

Thus, for example, if the aggregate income of the community is Rs. 5,000 crores and the expenditure on consumption is Rs. 4,000 crores.
then, APC = $\frac{4000}{5000}$ or $\frac{4}{5}$ or 80 percent.
This means that the community spends 80 percent of its income on consumption.

## **Marginal Propensity to Consume**

The marginal propensity to consume may be defined as the ratio of change in consumption to the change in income. It is calculated by dividing the absolute change in consumption by the absolute change in income and is expressed as :

Marginal Propensity to Consume = $\frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Y}$
Where C = consumption, Y = income and $\Delta$ indicates change in consumption or income.

If, for example, the aggregate income increases by Rs. 100 Crores and the aggregate consumption expenditure by Rs. 80 Crores, then:

$$
\text { MPC }=\frac{80}{100} \text { or } 0.8
$$

The marginal propensity to consume, thus, measures the rate of change in aggregate consumption expenditure relative to the rate of change in aggregate income.

The marginal propensity to consume may be equal to unity, greater than unity or less than unity. Thus, for example,
(a) The marginal propensity to consume is equal to one (1) or equal to unity, when additional consumption expenditure is exactly equal to additional income. If, for instance, additional income ( $\Delta \mathrm{Y}$ ) is Rs. 100 and additional consumption expenditure ( $\Delta \mathrm{C}$ ) is also Rs. 100

$$
\text { then, } M P C=\frac{100}{100}=1
$$

This, however, is hardly the case. The marginal propensity to consume may be equal to one by sheer incident rather than by a deliberate design.

(b) The marginal propensity to consume is more than one or unity, when additional consumption expenditure is greater than additional income. If, for example, additional income is Rs. 100 and additional consumption expenditure is Rs. 120, then,

$$
\mathrm{MPC}=\frac{120}{100}=1.2
$$

When marginal propensity to consume is greater than one, people draw upon their past savings or indulge in borrowing. It, therefore, leads to dis. savings.
(c) The marginal propensity to consume is less than one or unity, when additional consumption expenditure is less than additional income. If, for example, additional income is Rs. 100 and additonal consumption expenditure Rs. 70, then :

$$
\mathrm{MPC}=\frac{70}{100}=0.7
$$

# Marginal Propensity to Consume tends to be Less than Unity or less than One
According to Keynes, the marginal propensity to consume tends to be less than one, because as income increases, consumption also increases but less than proportionately. Of course, at very very low levels of income marginal propensity to consume tends to be high. Likewise, during war period when demand is suppressed by measures like price controls, rationing, forced savings etc. and when in the post war period, these are lifted, the marginal propensity to consume tends to be more than unity. In such a situation, incremental consumption is more than incremental income.

However, during normal times, increase in consumption expenditure is less than in proportion to increase in income, that is, with an increase in income, the marginal propensity to consume tends to be less than unity

# Poor have High Marginal Propensity to Consume than the Rich

It should be noted here that the marginal propensity to consume is higher in the case of poor than in the case of rich people. This is because, greater is the income of an individual, he will be able to satisfy almost all his basic needs and requirements and therefore, greater will be the tendency on his part to save more in order to provide for the future. On the other hand, income of the poor people being low, most of their basic human needs remain unsatisfied and as such an increase in their income leads to increase in consumption (and not savings) and so their marginal propensity to consume is high.

According to Keynes, in a capitalist society with a large unequal distribution of income, the total propensity to consume of the society is low. This is because, a very large part of the national income is appropriated by the rich who have low propensity to consume. Due to this over all low propensity to consume, the level of aggregate effective demand remains low resulting in low level of employment. Thus, in order to increase employment it becomes necessary to increase the propensity to consume which can be done by a policy of income redistribution in favour of the poor. For this, taxes may be imposed on the rich who have low propensity to consume and by spending that revenue through a public expenditure policy in a manner that would increase the income of the poor and consequently their propensity to consume. This would lead to an increase in the aggregate effective demand and ultimately result in more employment.

# Propensity to Consume and Employment

Let us discuss in brief as to what extent propensity to consume affects the level of employment in the economy.

We know that as income increases, consumption also increases but not in the same proportion, and as a result there arises a gap between income and consumption. As Keynes has put it, when income increases,

consumption also increases, but increase in consumption is less than in proportion to increase in income. A basic problem which arises from this fact is how to bridge the gap between income and consumption, because if this gap is not bridged it would mean that consumption in the economy is low which would lead to fall in effective demand and consequently to a fall in the level of employment. It is, therefore, necessary to bridge the gap between income and consumption. To maintain a certain level of employment in the economy, this gap can be bridged in two ways :
(i) By increasing the propensity to consume; for this purpose Keynes has advocated a policy of income redistribution in favour of the poor whose marginal propensity to consume is high by taxing the rich whose marginal propensity to consume is low. Likewise, reduction in taxes on articles of mass consumption and the supply of essential goods to the poor at the subsidised rates too would help in increasing their propensity to consume.
(ii) However, according to Keynes, the propensity to consume remains more or less stable in the short run and therefore he has advocated increase in investment expenditure to bridge the gap between income and employment. The increase in investment expenditure would help in increasing total effective demand and consequently employment in the economy.

# Determinants of Consumption Function
Factors Affecting Propensity to Consume:
The factors affecting propensity to consume can be studied under two heads :
(A) Subjective factors (B) Objective factors.
## (A) **Subjective Factors**

The subjective factors determining propensity to consume are purely personal reactions to consumption and are also known as endogenous or internal factors. According to Keynes, subjective factors are the main determinants of the shape and position of the consumption curve. The subjective factors refer to psychological motives and attitudes of people as also of institutions. Keynes has listed eight such motives :
(i) The motive of precaution, that is, the desire to hold a reserve against unforeseen contingencies.
(ii) The motive of foresight, that is, the desire to provide for anticipated future needs like old age, family education, etc.
(iii) The motive of calculation, that is, the desire to enjoy interest and capital appreciation.

(iv) The motive of improvement, that is, the desire to enjoy a gradually increasing expenditure.
(v) The motive of independence, that is, the desire to enjoy a sense of independence and power to do things.
(vi) The motive of enterprise, that is, the desire to carry out speculation of business projects.
(vii) The motive of pride, that is the desire to possess or to bequeath a fortune.
(viii) The motive of avarice, that is, the desire to satisfy sheer miserliness.

To this, Keynes has also added the corresponding list of motives on consumption such as enjoyment, short-sightedness, generosity, miscalculation, ostentation and extravagance.

Besides, subjective motivations also apply to the behavioural pattern of business corporations and governmental bodies. These include the following :
(i) Enterprise - the desire to do big things - to expand.
(ii) Liquidity - the desire to face emergencies successfully.
(iii) Rising incomes-the desire to demonstrate successful management.
(iv) Financial prudence - the desire to ensure adequate financial provision against depreciation and obsolescence and to discharge debt.
The above factors persuade individuals and institutions to save more and consume less.

However, these subjective factors are not likely to change substantially at least in the short run.

## (B) **Objective Factors**

The objective factors determining the propensity to consume are also known as exogenous or external factors and are subject to rapid changes even in the short period. The important objective determinants of consumption function are as follows :
(i) Changes in Fiscal Policy : Fiscal ploicy relates to government taxing and spending policy. Progressive taxation adopted by modern welfare states to reduce inequality of income, tend to shift consumption upwards by transferring purchasing power from the rich to the poor. Progressive public expenditure financed by taxes on rich as also loans subscribed by the rich tend to stimulate consumption.Controls, rationing and such other restrictions on spending imposed during war-time or during the period of planned development depress overall consumption in the economy.

(ii) Windfall Gains or Losses : Windfall gains consequent upon stock market fluctuations or any other factor will stimulate consumption. The opposite effect will follow in case of windfall losses. Unexpected increase in incomes shifts consumption upwards while unexpected cut in incomes shifts it downwards.
(iii) Changes in Expectations : Changes in the economic outlook of persons also shifts consumption upwards or donwwards.

If people expect prices to rise in future, there will be a heavy rush for buying in the present, shifting consumption upwards. Expectation of decline in prices in future will depress consumption. Similarly, when people expect income to increase, they may save less and consume more out of current income. Thus, expectations about future income also affects their consumption.
(iv) Changes in the Rate of Interest : Changes in the rate of interest influence consumption through their effects on the market values of securities. A fall in the rate of interest by raising the market prices of securities is likely to increase consumption. A rise in the rate of interest may shift. consumption downwards by reducing the market prices of securities. However, substansital changes in the rate of interest are not likely to occur in the short period. Therefore, this objective factor may be regarded as only of secondary importance in the short run.
(v) Changes in Wage Level : If wage rate is increased, there will be an increase in consumption expenditure, because workers have a high propensity to consume. In other words, when wage rate is increased consumption curve shifts up. But if the rise in wage rate is followed by a more than proportionate increase in prices, the real wage rate will fall and consumption curve will shift in the downward direction.
(vi) Financial Policy of the Corporations : If the joint stock companies retain a larger amount of profits for business expansion, dividend income of the shareholders will be low and consumption function will shift down. Conversely, if the joint stock companies retain a smaller of profits, consumption function will shift up.
(vii) Pattern of Income Distribution : Marginal propensity to consume is different for different groups of people in the society. Generally the poor have a higher marginal propensity to consume than the rich. Hence if income is transfered from the rich to the poor, total consumption in the economy will increase and consumption curve will shift up. Conversely, if income is transfered from the poor to the rich, consumption curve will shift down.
(viii) Amount of Wealth : People with large amount of wealth in the form of land and buildings etc. are less worried about future uncertainties

of life. Hence they save less and consume more out of current income. Conversely, people who possess no wealth save more and consume less out of current income.

It follows that under normal conditions, sharp changes in the propensity to consume or consumption function are not likely to occur. In the words of Prof. Hansen, "In general the conclusion is reached that except for quite abnormal" or revolutionary changes in certain objective factors-expectations caused by unusual events such as wars, earthquakes, strikes, revolutions, etc. major changes in the tax structure, quite exceptional windful losses or gains-apart from such drastic changes, shifts in the propensity to consume out of a given income are not likely to be of more than secondary importance.

It should, however, be noted at this stage that Keynes did not quite clearly differentiate between the cyclical (short-run) and secular (long-run) consumption function. The validity of his analysis of consumption function in the long run requires to be empirically examined.

# Significance of the Concept of Consumption Function

Keynesian concept of consumption function occupies a very important place in modern economic literature and is considered an outstanding contribution to modern economic thought. In the words of Hansen, "Keynes' analysis of the consumption function is a major landmark in the history of economic doctrine". It is a major break- through in modern economics. The main points of significance of this concept may briefly be mentioned as follows :
(i) The concept of consumption function has disproved Say's law of markets that supply creates its own demand.
(ii) Since in the short-run, consumption function is assumed to be a stable phenomenon, it underlines the importance of investment in the theory of income and employment.
(iii) The concept provides a satisfactory explanation of the 'up-turns' and 'down-turns' in business cycles.
(iv) The concept helps to explain the long run over-saving phenomenon and the declining trend of marginal efficiency of capital in rich countries.
(v) The conept of marginal propensity to consume is very useful in the understanding the theory of multiplier.
To quote Hansen once again, "It has been my conviction for many years that the great contribution of Keynes' general Theory was the clear and specific formulation of consumption function. This is an epoch-making contribution to the tools of economic analysis, analogous to, but even more important than Marshall's discovery of the demand function."
