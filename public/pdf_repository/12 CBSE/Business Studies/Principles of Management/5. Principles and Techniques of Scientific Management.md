This section details the core principles proposed by F.W. Taylor for scientific management and the techniques developed to implement these principles in practice.

# Principles and Techniques of Scientific Management

## Principles of Scientific Management

Taylor's Scientific Management is based on the following four principles:

### 1. Science not Rule of Thumb

This principle states that management practice should be based on scientific inquiry rather than traditional 'rule of thumb' methods.

*   Taylor believed there was only one best method to maximise efficiency, which could be developed through study and analysis.
*   This scientifically developed method should replace the rule of thumb throughout the organisation.
*   The scientific method involves investigating traditional methods through work-study, unifying the best practices, and developing a standard method of work.
*   *Example:* Scientifically analysing the process of loading iron pigs into boxcars, even a small activity, can lead to significant savings in energy, time, and materials. Establishing a standard time or method for a task through analysis, not estimates or past experience, is another example.

### 2. Harmony, Not Discord

Under the factory system, potential conflict often existed between management and workers (class-conflict), with workers feeling underpaid and management feeling workers didn't put in enough effort. Taylor recognised this conflict was unproductive.

*   This principle advocates for complete harmony and proper understanding between management and workers, who should work together for organisational goals.
*   To achieve this, Taylor stressed 'Mental Revolution', a complete change in attitude from competition to cooperation.

*   'Mental Revolution' means both management and workers should have positive feelings for each other. Management should share company gains with workers, and workers should work hard and contribute their best.
*   Taylor believed the true interests of management and workers are the same, and the prosperity of the organisation depends on the prosperity of both employer and employees.
*   *Example:* Japanese management often practices a paternalistic style with openness between management and workers, reflecting this principle.

### 3. Cooperation, Not Individualism

This principle, an extension of 'Harmony, not discord', advocates for complete cooperation between management and workers instead of individualism.

*   Competition should be replaced by cooperation. Both parties must recognise their mutual need and importance.
*   Management should encourage constructive suggestions from employees through rewards and take workers into confidence before making important decisions. Workers should avoid strikes and unreasonable demands.
*   Taylor believed there should be an almost equal division of work and responsibility between workers and management.
*   Management should adopt a 'Paternalistic style', caring for employee needs. Open communication and goodwill can reduce the need for trade unions.
*   *Example:* Involving workers in setting work standards can increase their involvement and efficiency, demonstrating cooperation.

### 4. Development of workers to their greatest efficiency and prosperity

Scientific management insists on the development of workers, as industrial efficiency depends on their competencies. This principle states that management should aim to develop workers to their greatest efficiency and prosperity.

*   Concern for efficiency should start with a scientific selection process. Selected workers should be assigned jobs based on their physical, mental, and intellectual capabilities.
*   To increase efficiency and reach maximum potential, workers should receive scientific training. This benefits both the company (efficiency) and the workers (prosperity).
*   *Example:* Companies sending employees to seminars by management experts for mental growth aligns with developing workers.

## Techniques of Scientific Management

Taylor suggested the following techniques to apply scientific principles of management:

### 1. Functional Foremanship

In a factory, the 'Foreman' is the key figure interacting daily with workers. Taylor aimed to improve the foreman's role. He identified qualities of a good foreman but found no single person possessed them all. This led him to propose 'Functional Foremanship'.

*   This technique divides the supervision task into several specialised functions, each assigned to a specialist foreman.
*   Taylor advocated separating planning and execution functions, with each managed by a group of four foremen, totalling eight specialists guiding a worker.
*   This structure extends to the shop floor, with a Planning Incharge and a Production Incharge under the Factory Manager.

*   Functional foremanship is an extension of the principle of 'Division of Work' and specialisation to the shop floor. Each worker receives orders from these eight foremen related to their process.

**Under Planning Department:**
1.  **Instruction Card Clerk:** Drafts instructions for workers.
2.  **Route Clerk:** Specifies the production route.
3.  **Time and Cost Clerk:** Prepares time and cost sheets.
4.  **Disciplinarian:** Ensures discipline.

**Under Production Department:**
1.  **Speed Boss:** Ensures timely and accurate job completion.
2.  **Gang Boss:** Keeps machines, tools, etc., ready for workers.
3.  **Repair Boss:** Ensures proper working condition of machines and tools.
4.  **Inspector:** Checks the quality of work.

**Why did Taylor stress on the need for multiple foremen?**
> **Answer:** Taylor stressed the need for multiple foremen because he believed no single person possessed all the necessary qualities (intelligence, education, tact, grit, judgment, special knowledge, manual dexterity, energy, honesty, good health) required for effective supervision. By dividing the foreman's role into eight specialised functions, each entrusted to a specialist, he aimed to leverage individual strengths according to their qualities.

**'Functional Foremanship' is opposite to 'Unity of Command'**
Taylor's functional foremanship directly contrasts with Fayol's principle of unity of command. Unity of command requires a subordinate to report to only one superior, while functional foremanship involves a worker receiving orders and being accountable to eight different specialists.

### 2. Standardisation and Simplification of Work

**Standardisation** refers to setting standards for every business activity.

*   This involves fixing standards for quality and quantity of goods, using standardised equipment, materials, production methods, etc.
*   These standards serve as benchmarks during production. Working conditions (temperature, lighting, etc.) should also be standardised.
*   Objectives of standardisation:
    (i) Reduce a product line to fixed types, sizes, and characteristics.
    (ii) Establish interchangeability of manufactured parts and products.
    (iii) Establish standards of excellence and quality in materials.
    (iv) Establish performance standards for men and machines.
*   *Example:* Fixing a standard time to produce a certain output level. If workers take longer, corrective measures are needed.

**Simplification** involves eliminating superfluous or unnecessary varieties, sizes, and grades of products.

*   It aims to remove unnecessary diversity in products.
*   It leads to reduced inventories, better equipment utilisation, and increased turnover. This saves costs on labour, machines, and tools and improves control.
*   *Example:* Hindustan Unilever Limited reducing soap varieties resulted in efficient and effective outcomes.

### 3. Method Study

Method study focuses on finding the 'one best way' of doing a job.

*   Various methods exist for a job. Method study systematically analyses different parameters to find the best way, from raw material procurement to final product delivery.
*   Critical analysis of plant layout, product design, material handling, and work processes is done. Techniques like process charts and operations research are used.
*   The objective is to minimise production cost and maximise quality and customer satisfaction.
*   *Example:* Designing a car assembly line involves deciding the sequence of operations and placement of men, machines, and materials – all part of method study.

### 4. Motion Study

Motion study involves a detailed analysis of the various body movements a worker performs while doing a task.

*   It studies movements like lifting, putting objects, sitting, and changing positions.
*   By closely examining body motions, productive, incidental, and unproductive movements can be identified.
*   The purpose is to detect and eliminate unnecessary movements and find the best method for a job, saving time and increasing production.
*   Taylor and Frank Gilbreth reduced brick layering motions from 18 to 5, increasing productivity significantly.
*   Taylor used tools like stopwatches and movie cameras to study motions. This helped design suitable equipment and train workers.
*   *Example:* If motion study shows a worker frequently moves to get tools, keeping the toolbox closer eliminates unnecessary movement, increasing productivity and efficiency.

**Objectives of Motion Study:**
(i) Determine worker movements during a typical job.
(ii) Differentiate productive, incidental, and unproductive movements.
(iii) Reduce unproductive movements.
(iv) Design suitable equipment and tools to minimise unproductive movements.

### 5. Time Study

Time study is a technique to determine the standard time a worker of reasonable skill and ability takes to perform a well-defined job.

*   It helps determine how much work an employee should complete in a given time.
*   The job is broken into elements, and the time for each element is recorded using devices like stopwatches.
*   Standard time for the whole task is fixed based on several readings.
*   The method depends on task volume, frequency, cycle time, and measurement costs.
*   Objectives of Time Study:
    (a) Determine the number of workers needed.
    (b) Frame suitable incentive schemes.
    (c) Determine labour costs.
*   *Example:* If time study determines a worker takes 30 minutes to make one box, the standard task is 14 boxes in a 7-hour working day (8-hour shift minus 1 hour for rest/lunch). Wages can be based on this standard.

**Comparison of Time Study with Motion Study**

| Basis                       | Time Study                                           | Motion Study                                            |
| :-------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1. Purpose                  | Aims to determine standard time required to do a particular work. | Aims to eliminate unnecessary movements in doing a particular work. |
| 2. Development of new methods | Does not develop new methods of doing the work.      | Helps in developing new methods by eliminating unnecessary movements. |
| 3. Tools of study           | Conducted with the help of a stop watch.             | Conducted through a movie camera.                       |

### 6. Fatigue Study

Fatigue study determines the amount and frequency of rest intervals required to complete a task.

*   Fatigue is tiredness from physical or mental work. Continuous work without breaks causes fatigue. Taylor suggested rest intervals to regain stamina and increase productivity.
*   The study observes workers to note when their efficiency decreases, determining the necessary rest intervals.

All four studies (Method, Motion, Time, and Fatigue Study) fall under **Work Study**, which is the scientific study of any work.

### 7. Differential Piece Wage System

This system pays efficient and inefficient workers at different rates based on their productivity.

*   Taylor believed financial incentives motivate extra effort. He developed this system linking incentives directly to productivity.
*   First, a standard task is set using time and motion study. Then, two wage rates are fixed: a higher rate for workers meeting or exceeding the standard output, and a lower rate for those below standard.
*   The goal is to differentiate and reward efficient workers.
*   *Example:* If the standard is 10 units, with rates of $50/unit for 10+ units and $40/unit for <10 units. A worker producing 11 units gets $550 (11 * $50). A worker producing 9 units gets $360 (9 * $40). The difference ($190) motivates the inefficient worker to improve.

## Modern Techniques of Scientific Management (Just for Reference)

1.  **Just In Time Manufacturing:** Inventory strategy to reduce inventory and costs, improving return on investment.
2.  **Lean Manufacturing:** Focuses on reducing seven wastes (waiting, transportation, processing, motion, inventory, scrap, manufacturing process) to improve quality, reduce cost, and production time.
3.  **Kaizen:** Japanese term for 'change for better'. Focuses on improving productivity by eliminating waste, using approaches like just-in-time delivery, standardised work, and right-sized equipment.
4.  **Six Sigma:** A technique to reduce inefficiencies and save time/money by reducing 'quality variations'. It is customer-oriented and data-driven for process improvement.
