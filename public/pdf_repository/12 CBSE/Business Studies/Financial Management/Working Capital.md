# WORKING CAPITAL 

Working Capital refers to that part of total capital, which is required for holding current assets. It may also be defined as excess of current assets over current liabilities.
Before we proceed further with the concept of Working Capital, let us first understand the meaning of Current Assets and Current Liabilities.

## Meaning of Current Assets

Current Assets refer to those assets, which are expected to get converted into cash or cash equivalents within a period of one year.

- Investment in current assets helps in day-to-day operations of the business.
- Examples of Current Assets in order of liquidity:

1. Cash in hand/Cash at Bank
2. Marketable securities
3. Bills Receivable
4. Debtors
5. Finished goods inventory
6. Work-in-progress
7. Raw materials
8. Prepaid expenses

An asset is more liquid if it can be converted into cash quicker and without reduction in value.

## Meaning of Current Liabilities

Current Liabilities refer to those liabilities which are payable within a period of 1 year. For example, bills payable, creditors, outstanding expenses, advances received from customers, etc.

## Working Capital affects both Liquidity and Profitability

Working Capital affects both liquidity as well as profitability of a business:

- Current assets help in day-to-day operations of the business and are expected to be converted into cash within one year. Hence, it provides liquidity to the business.
- However, such assets attract very nominal earnings. They contribute less to the profits as compared to fixed assets.
- It means, current assets are usually more liquid but contribute less to the profits than fixed assets. Thus, it is rightly said that working capital affects both liquidity as well as profitability of a business and a balance needs to be struck between liquidity and profitability.


## Types of Working Capital

There are two types of Working Capital:

1. Gross Working Capital (GWC): It refers to investment in all the current assets taken together, i.e. Gross Working Capital = Sum Total of all Current Assets.
2. Net Working Capital (NWC): It refers to excess of current assets over current liabilities, i.e. Net Working Capital = Current Assets - Current Liabilities.

- NWC is positive when Total Current Assets > Total Current Liabilities.
- NWC is negative when Total Current Assets < Total Current Liabilities.

## Example to Differentiate between Gross and Net Working Capital 

Suppose Current Assets (CA): Cash = ₹ 50,000; Debtors = ₹ 70,000; and Inventory = ₹ 1,00,000 Gross Working Capital (GWC) $=50,000+70,000+1,00,000=\$ 2,20,000$
Further, suppose Current Liabilities (CL) are:
Bills Payable = ₹ 30,000; Creditors = ₹ 1,00,000;
Net Working Capital (NWC) $=2,20,000-1,30,000=\$ 90,000$
Current Assets are financed through both short and long-term sources
Some part of current assets is usually financed through short-term sources, i.e., current liabilities. In the given example, out of the total current assets ( $\$ 2,20,000$ ), some part is met through current liabilities ( $\$ 1,30,000$ ) and rest ( $\$ 90,000$ ) has to be financed through long-term sources.

## Concept of Operating Cycle

There is always a time gap between the sale of goods and receipt of cash. Working Capital is required for this period in order to maintain the sales activity. This time gap is technically termed as 'Operating Cycle' of the business. Operating Cycle is the time duration starting from the procurement of goods or raw materials and ending with the sales realisation. The length and nature of the operating cycle may differ from one firm to another depending upon the size and nature of the firm.
In a manufacturing company, operating cycle is the length of time needed to complete following events:

1. Conversion of cash into raw materials;
2. Conversion of raw materials into work-inprogress;
3. Conversion of work-in-progress into finished goods;
4. Conversion of finished goods into accounts receivable;
5. Conversion of accounts receivable into cash.

In a trading concern, the operating cycle will include the length of time taken for:
(i) Procurement of goods, and
(ii) Realisation of sales revenue.

It must be noted that the length of operating cycle directly affects the requirements of working capital. Longer the operating cycle, more is the working capital requirement.

## Factors Affecting Working Capital Requirements

The working capital requirements of a concern depend on the following factors:

1. Nature of Business: The basic nature of a business influences the amount of working capital required.

- A Manufacturing Concern needs substantial working capital as raw material needs to be converted into finished goods before any sales become possible.

- On the other hand, a Trading Concern requires less working capital as there is no processing, i.e., sales are generally made immediately upon receipt of materials.
- Similarly, a Service Firm also needs less working capital as it sells more on cash basis and do not have to maintain inventory.

2. Scale of Operations: The firms, which operate at large-scale require more working capital as their inventory level, debtors, etc are generally high. However, firms operating at smallscale need less working capital due to low level of activity.
3. Business Cycle: The requirements of working capital are also determined by different phases of business cycle.

- During Boom conditions, the market is flourishing, which means more demand, more production, more inventory, more debtors, i.e. more amount of working capital is needed.
- However, during Depression or Recession, less working capital is needed as there is low demand, need for lesser inventories and less debtors.

4. Seasonal Factors: If a firm is operating in seasonal goods and services, then working capital requirement will also fluctuate with change in season.

- In Peak Season, higher amount of working capital is required due to higher level of activity.
- In the Lean Season, less working capital is required because of low level of activity.

5. Production Cycle: Production cycle is the time span between receipt of raw materials and conversion of same into finished goods.

- Some businesses have a longer production cycle while some have a shorter one. Duration and length of production cycle affects the requirement of working capital.
- In case of firms with longer processing cycle, more working capital is required. On the other hand, firms with shorter production cycle can manage with less working capital.

6. Credit Allowed: Credit policy of the company determines the average time period needed to collect the sale proceeds. Different firms allow different credit terms to their customers. It depends on competition level and credit worthiness of customers.

- A company following liberal credit policy needs more working capital due to higher amount of debtors.
- However, in case of strict credit policy, less working capital is needed.

7. Credit Availed: The requirements of working capital also depend on credit facilities enjoyed by the company.

- A company enjoying liberal credit facilities from its suppliers will need less working capital.
- However, if company avails strict credit terms (i.e. suppliers are giving only short period credit), then company will require more working capital.

8. Availability of Raw Material: If the raw materials and other required materials are available freely and continuously, then less working capital will be needed as less stock has to be maintained.

- However, if the business does not have easy access to raw material (like in case of imported goods), then more working capital is required as higher inventory level would have to be maintained.
- In addition, the time gap between placing an order and actual receipt of material (also called lead time) is also relevant. Higher the lead time, higher the quantity of material to be stored and higher is the amount of working capital requirement.

9. Operating Efficiency: Operating efficiency of a company also determines the requirements of working capital.

- Efficient management of raw materials (reflected by higher inventory turnover ratio) enables the firm to manage with less stock.
- Similarly, a better debtors turnover ratio will reduce the amount tied up in receivables.
- Better sales effort may reduce the average time for which finished goods inventory is held.
- Such efficiencies may reduce the level of raw materials, debtors and finished goods, resulting in lower requirement of working capital.
- On the other hand, a less efficient firm needs more working capital due to more investment in inventory and debtors.

10. Growth Prospects: The firms which have sufficient possibilities of growth require more working capital to meet higher production and sales target whenever required. However, for companies with less growth prospects, less working capital is needed.
11. Level of Competition: The extent of competition influences the working capital needs. More working capital is required in highly competitive market as firms need to maintain higher level of inventory of finished goods to meet urgent orders from customers. Competition may also force them to extend liberal credit terms. However, in case of less competition, firms can manage with less working capital.
12. Inflation: Changes in the price level also affect the requirements of working capital.

- Rising prices would necessitate the use of more funds to maintain an existing level of production and sales. Thus, working capital requirement of a business becomes higher with higher rate of inflation.
- It must also be noted that rising prices (say, an inflation rate of 5\%) does not mean that every component of working capital will rise by the same proportion.
- The actual requirement depends on the rates of price change of different components (i.e. raw material, labour cost, etc.) as well as their proportion in the total requirement.
- However, if the company can raise their prices proportionately, then there will be no serious problem regarding working capital.
