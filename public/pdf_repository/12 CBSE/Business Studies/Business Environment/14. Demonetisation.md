This section explains the concept, features, and impact of demonetisation as implemented in India.

# Demonetisation

## Concept

On November 8, 2016, the Government of India announced the demonetisation of the two highest value currency notes, ₹500 and ₹1,000, effective immediately. They ceased to be legal tender, except for a few specified purposes like paying utility bills. **Demonetisation** is the act of stripping a currency unit of its status as **Legal Tender**. (Legal Tender Money is currency that can be legally used to settle debts or other obligations).

According to an RBI report, almost 86% of the country's total currency in circulation became invalid. The scheme required people to deposit the invalid currency in banks, and restrictions were placed on cash withdrawals. This placed restrictions on the convertibility of domestic money and bank deposits.

The objectives of demonetisation included curbing corruption, counterfeiting, the use of high-denomination notes for illegal activities, and especially the accumulation of 'black money' (untaxed income).

*Note: On May 19, 2023, RBI announced the withdrawal of ₹2,000 currency notes from circulation, encouraging the public to deposit or exchange them.*

## Features of Demonetisation

1.  **Tax Administration Measure:** Demonetisation is seen as a measure to improve tax compliance. Cash from declared income could be easily deposited and exchanged. However, holders of black money had to disclose their unaccounted wealth and pay taxes with penalties.
2.  **Shift in Government Stance:** It signaled the government's stance that tax evasion would no longer be tolerated.
3.  **Channelizing Savings:** Demonetisation aimed to channel savings into the formal financial system. While much of the deposited cash was expected to be withdrawn, some new deposit schemes offered by banks could provide a base for loans at lower interest rates.
4.  **Less-Cash Economy:** It aimed to move towards a less-cash or cash-lite economy, increasing financial savings through the formal system and improving tax compliance.
    *   However, digital transactions require internet connectivity and devices like cell phones and Point-of-Sale (PoS) machines.
    *   These challenges are offset by the understanding that it helps bring people into the formal economy, potentially increasing financial savings and reducing tax evasion.

# Impact of Demonetisation

|     | Impact Area              | Effects                                                                                                                               |
| :-- | :----------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1.  | Money/Interest rates     | i. Decline in cash transactions. <br> ii. Increase in bank deposits. <br> iii. Increase in financial savings.                         |
| 2.  | Private wealth           | Declined as some demonetised high-value notes were not returned, and real estate prices fell.                                         |
| 3.  | Public sector wealth     | No significant direct effect mentioned.                                                                                               |
| 4.  | Digitisation             | Increased digital transactions among new users and increased use of RuPay Cards and Aadhar Enabled Payment System (AEPS).             |
| 5.  | Real estate              | Prices declined.                                                                                                                      |
| 6.  | Tax collection           | Rise in income tax collection due to increased disclosure.                                                                              |
