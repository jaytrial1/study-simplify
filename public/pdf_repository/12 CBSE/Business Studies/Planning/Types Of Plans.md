# TYPES OF PLANS 

Plan is a document showing detailed scheme, program and strategy, worked out in advance for fulfilling an objective.
Every organisation has to prepare a plan before making any decision related to business operation or undertaking any project. Plans can be classified into several types depending on the use and the length of the planning period. To provide guidance to managers for making decisions and solving problems, various kinds of plans are drawn. These plans can be classified into 'Single-use Plans' and 'Standing Plans'.

## Single use Plans

Single use plan is one time plan which is specifically designed to achieve a particular goal. For example, budget.

- Single use plans are made for handling non-recurring problems, i.e. they can't be used again and again.
- The length of a single use plan differs depending on the type of the project. It may span a week or a month. A project may sometimes be of only one day, such as organising an event or a seminar or conference.
- These plans include budgets, programmes and projects.
- They are also known as 'Specific Plans' as they are formulated to fit the specific situations.


## Standing Plans

A standing plan is one which is used for activities that occur regularly over a period of time.

- It is designed to ensure that internal operations of an organisation run smoothly. Such a plan greatly enhances efficiency in routine decision-making.
- It is a plan that is developed once but is modified from time to time to meet business needs as required.
- Standing plans include policies, procedures, methods and rules.
- Standing plans help in decision-making on problems which are of recurring nature. They are also known as 'Multi-use Plans' or 'Repeated-use Plans'.
There are other types of plans which are usually not classified as single use or standing plans. For example, strategy and objectives as they are usually set by the top management and serve as a guide for overall planning. On the other hand, single use and standing plans are part of the operational planning process.
Single use Plans Vs Standing Plans

| Plans | Single use Plans | Standing Plans |
| :--: | :--: | :--: |
| 1. Meaning | Single use plans refer to plans formulated to meet the requirements of a particular situation and to accomplish specific objectives. | Standing plans refer to plans, which are used for activities that occur regularly over a period of time. |
| 2. Objectives | Single use plans are developed for activities which are not likely to be repeated in future. | Standing plans are developed for activities, which are of recurring nature. |
| 3. Period | These plans are generally formulated for a short period. | These plans are formulated for a long period. |
| 4. Stability | Single use plans have less stability as they <br> discarded when the specific situation or <br> project is over. | Standing plans are relatively stable as they are <br> used over and over again. |
| 5. Scope | Single use plans generally have a narrow <br> scope as they are formulated to fit the specific <br> situation. | Standing plans have a wide scope as they <br> involve more than one department of business <br> function. |
| 6. Example | Cash Budget to show expected inflow and <br> outflow of cash for a specific period in advance. | Procedure for processing an order or <br> recruitment of employees. |

Based on what the plans seek to achieve, plans can be classified as Objectives, Strategy, Policy, Procedure, Method, Rule, Programme, Budget. Let us discuss them one by one.

## Objectives (Neither Single use nor Standing Plan) 

The first step in planning is setting objectives. Objectives are the ends which the management seeks to achieve within a given time period by its operations.

- So, objectives can be said to be the desired future position that the management would like to reach.
- They represent the end point of planning. All other managerial activities are also directed towards achieving these objectives.
- They are usually set by top management and different departments may have their own objectives.
- Objectives define the future state of affairs and serve as a guide for overall business planning.
- Objectives should be expressed in specific terms, i.e., they should be measurable in quantitative terms.
Example:The objective of a mobile company can be to increase the mobile users by $10 \%$ in 2 years


## Strategy (Neither Single use nor Standing Plan)

Strategy is a comprehensive plan for accomplishing an organisation objectives.

- Strategy provides the broad contours of an organisation's business. Strategy also refers to the future decisions defining the organisations direction and scope in the long run.
- The three dimensions of strategy are:
(i) Determining long-term objectives.
(ii) Adopting a particular course of action.
(iii) Allocating resources necessary to achieve the objective.
- A strategy is formulated after considering the changes taking place in the business environment. The changes in economic, political, social, legal and technological environment will affect an organisations strategy. Strategy involves preparing oneself for meeting unforeseen factors.
- Major strategic decisions will include decisions like whether the organisation will continue to be in the same line of business or combine new lines of activity with the existing business or seek to acquire a dominant position in the same market.

- A company's marketing strategy may relate to following questions:
(i) Who are the customers?
(ii) What is the demand for the product?
(iii) Which channel of distribution to use?
(iv) What is the pricing policy?
(v) How do we advertise the product?

Example: Pepsi and Coca Cola are competitors in the soft drink market. If Pepsi reduces the price of its products in the market, then counter-plan of Coca-Cola, to maintain its market share may be considered as strategy.

## Features of Strategy 

1. It is formulated by top management due to its relative importance.
2. It is formulated after considering both internal and external environment.
3. It is dynamic as it depends on ever changing business environment.
4. It provides guidelines for thinking and action.

## Policies (Standing Plan)

Policies are general statements that guide thinking or channelise energy towards a particular direction.

- Policies are derived from objectives. They define the broad parameters within which a manager may use his discretion to apply the policy.
- There are policies for all levels and departments in the organisation ranging from major company policies to minor policies.
- Policies provide a basis for interpreting strategy. They are guides to managerial action and decisions in the implementation of strategy.
- The policies pre-decide some issues, avoid confusion and it becomes easier to resolve problems or issues. Policy acts as a guide to take decisions in an unexpected situation. In short, policy is the general response to a particular problem or situation.
Example: Policy of a company may be not to employ any person who is less than 18 years of age.


## Difference between Policies and Objectives

| Basis | Policies | Objectives |
| :--: | :--: | :--: |
| 1. Purpose | Policies aim to determine how the work is to be done. | Objectives aim to determine what is to be done. |
| 2. Period | Policies do not have a time dimension. | Organisation may have short and long-term objectives. |
| 3. Need | Although they are needed to achieve objectives, but an organisation can exist without them. | Objectives are basic and critical for the existence of an organisation. |
| 4. Formulation | They are formulated by the managers of all levels. | They are generally determined by the top level. |
| 5. Source | They are derived from objectives. | They are derived from goals. |

Difference between Policies and Strategy

| Basis | Policies | Strategy |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1. Purpose | Policy aims to deal with repetitive issues. | Strategy aims to counter unforeseen <br> problems or environmental threats. |
| 2. Validity | Policies remain valid for the situations or <br> problems which are recurring in nature. | Strategies are single use plans and designed <br> to suit specific situations. |
| 3. Role of <br> Competitors | Policy may not be formulated on the move <br> of competitors. | It is formulated after considering activities of <br> the competitors. |
| 4. Hierarchy of <br> plans | Policies, which are supportive in nature, <br> occupy lower place in hierarchy of plans. | Strategies are considered to be superior and <br> occupy higher place. |

## Procedures (Standing Plan) 

A procedure is a chronological sequence of various steps to be taken in order to perform an activity in an efficient manner.

- A procedure is generally established for repetitive activity, so that same steps are followed, whenever that activity is performed. They are generally meant for insiders to follow.
- The sequence of steps to be taken is generally to enforce a policy and to attain pre-determined objectives.
- Policies and procedures are related to each other as procedures are steps to be carried out within a broad policy framework.
- An enterprise may have different procedures, like procedure for processing an order, recruitment of employees, collection of payments and so on.
- Procedures are very rigid and do not leave any scope for individual judgment.

Example: An enterprise may have following procedure for purchase of goods: (i) First, identify the various suppliers; (ii) Invite quotations from them; (iii) Compare the quotations; (iv) Place order to the supplier, who offers the best quotation; (v) Receipt and inspection of goods; and (vi) Make payment to the supplier.

Difference between Policies and Procedures

| Basis | Policies | Procedures |
| :--: | :--: | :--: |
| 1. Need | Policy is needed for achieving objectives. | Procedure is needed for the implementation of policy. |
| 2. Scope for Discretion | Policies are flexible and managers have discretion within prescribed limits. | Procedures are more rigid and there is little scope for discretion to be used by managers. |
| 3. Expression | Policy is expressed as general statement. | Procedure is expressed in specific terms. |
| 4. Area of application | Policies have broad areas of application. | Procedures have limited area of application. |

## Methods (Standing Plan)

Method is a prescribed process in which a particular operation or an activity is performed considering the objective of the organisation.

- It deals with a task comprising one step of a procedure and specifies how this step is to be performed. The methods may vary from task to task.

- Selection of proper method saves time, money and effort and increases efficiency.
- For imparting training to employees at various levels (from top level to supervisory level), different methods can be adopted. For example, for top level management, orientation programmes, lectures and seminars can be organised, whereas at the supervisory level, on the job training methods and work oriented methods are appropriate.
- Methods should be stated in clear and precise terms to improve organisational efficiency and to bring orderliness to the work place.
Example: There are different methods available for valuation of stock like FIFO (First in first out), LIFO (Last in first out), etc. Business can select any method for valuation of its stock. Selection of a particular method avoids confusion and helps in smooth functioning.


## Difference between Procedures and Methods 

| Basis | Procedures | Methods |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1. Scope | Procedure has a wide scope as it lays down <br> sequence of all activities to be completed. | Methods have limited scope as they are <br> confined to one step of procedure. |
| 2. Flexibility | They are more rigid as compared to methods. | Methods are flexible statements. |
| 3. Purpose | Procedure aims to develop steps for <br> completing an activity. | Method aims to standardise the way of <br> completing a task. |

## Rules (Standing Plan)

Rules are specific statements that inform what is to be done.

- Rules reflect a managerial decision that a certain action must or must not be taken. Employees are expected to comply with the rules while performing an activity.
- Rules are rigid and demand strict compliance. They do not allow for any flexibility or discretion.
- Rules are usually the simplest type of plans as there is no compromise or change unless a policy decision is taken.

A company may have rule of 'No Smoking' in the factory premises.

Rules are enforced to maintain discipline and to avoid disorder and chaos in the organisation. Their violation is generally associated with some sort of disciplinary action.

## Difference between Procedures and Rules

| Basis | Procedures | Rules |
| :--: | :--: | :--: |
| 1. Nature | They indicate broad guidelines within which management can exercise little discretion. | Rules strictly define the guidelines and there is no scope for discretion. |
| 2. Flexibility | Procedures are more flexible as compared to rules. | Rules are rigid statements. |
| 3. Effect of violation | There is no specific penalty for violation of procedure. | The guilty has to face penalty in case of violation. |
| 4. Example | Procedure established for selection of employees by a company. | Rule of 'No Smoking' in the organisation. |

Difference between Policies and Rules

| Basis | Policies | Rules |
| :--: | :--: | :--: |
| 1. Nature | Policy is a general statement. | Rule is the most specific statement. |
| 2. Purpose | Policy aims to guide in decision-making. | Rules aim to guide to human behaviour. |
| 3. Flexibility | Policies are generally flexible. | Rules are rigid and there is no scope for deviations. |
| 4. Effect of violation | There is no penalty specified for violation of policies. | The guilty has to face penalty in case of violation. |
| 5. Example | Company may have Policy "To make payments more than one lakh by cheque". | Rule of "No Smoking" in the company. |

Difference between Rules and Methods

| Basis | Rules | Methods |
| :--: | :--: | :--: |
| 1. Purpose | Rules are designed to achieve order and discipline. | Methods are designed to achieve efficiency and economy. |
| 2. Effect of violation | The guilty has to face penalty in case of violation. | There is no penalty specified for violation of methods. |
| 3. Flexibility | Rules are rigid and there is no scope for deviations. | Methods are flexible statements. |
| 4. Application | Rules are applied to human beings to make them behave in a particular manner. | Methods relate to manual and mechanical ways of doing a task. |

## Programme (Single use Plan) 

Programme is a detailed statement about a project which outlines the objectives, policies, procedures, rules, tasks, human and physical resources required and the budget to implement any course of action.

- Programmes will include the entire gamut (i.e. complete range) of activities as well as the organisation's policy and how it will contribute to the overall business plan.
- It gives a step-by-step approach to guide the action necessary to reach a pre-determined goal. The minutest details are worked out, i.e., procedures, rules, budgets, within the broad policy framework.
- Programme can be of different types, like production programme, training programme, sales promotion programme, etc.
Example: A company may have programme with respect to 'Construction of new Factory Premises.


## Budget (Single use Plan)

Budget is the statement of expected result expressed in numerical terms over a specific period of time.

- It is a plan which quantifies future facts and figures. For example, a sales budget may forecast the sales of different products in each area for a particular month. - Budget may also be prepared to show the number of workers required in the factory at

- As budget is expressed in numerical terms, it becomes easier to compare actual figures with expected figures and take corrective action subsequently.
- Thus, budget is also a control device from which deviations can be taken care of. However, making a budget involves forecasting. So, it is a fundamental planning instrument in many organisations.
- Some important budgets used in an organisation are: Sales Budget, Production Budget, Cash Budget, Revenue and Expense Budget, etc.
Example: Cash Budget is prepared to show the expected inflow and outflow of cash for a period in advance. Cash inflow comes from sales and cash outflows in the form of expenses. The net cash position can be determined by subtracting cash outflow from cash inflow. Management needs to hold adequate cash balances for various purposes. But at the same time, it should avoid excess cash balance as it gives little or no return. So, business must assess and plan its need for cash with a degree of caution.
