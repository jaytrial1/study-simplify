*   E.T. Elbourne: "Sole proprietorship is a business initiated, and operated by one individual who carries all financial and administrative responsibilities, employing such assistants as may be necessary."
*   Edward Elbourne: "Sole proprietorship is a business unit whose ownership and management are vested in one person. The individual assumes all risks of loss or failure of the enterprise and receives all profits from its successful operations."

## Characteristics of Sole Proprietorship

As 'sole' means single and 'proprietor' means owner, this type of business is a one-person show exhibiting following features:

1.  **Individual ownership:** Exclusively owned by a single person.
2.  **Individual management and control:** All affairs are managed and controlled by the sole proprietor, though assistants can be employed.
3.  **Individual financing:** All investment is made by the proprietor, with access to loans if required.
4.  **No separate legal entity:** Legally, the proprietor and proprietorship are one. The business's existence is tied to the owner.
5.  **Unlimited liability:** The proprietor is responsible for all losses. Personal property can be used to pay business debts if assets are insufficient.
6.  **Sole beneficiary:** The sole proprietor is entitled to all profits and losses, motivating them to maximize profits.
7.  **Easy formation and closure:** Subject to minimum legal formalities and regulations for starting and closing.
8.  **Limited area of operation:** Generally limited due to limited finance availability and managerial abilities.

## Suitability of Sole Proprietorship Form of Business

Success depends on the entrepreneur's intelligence, competence, and decision-making capacity. This form is suitable when:

1.  Capital requirement is limited.
2.  Confidentiality/secrecy is important.
3.  Market is local.
4.  Goods are artistic or require customization.
5.  Quick decision-making is necessary.
6.  Size of the venture is small.

## Legal Formalities Involved

### Sole Proprietorship Registration Procedure

A sole proprietorship does not need to be registered and is therefore inexpensive to start. However, industry-specific licenses may be required. General factors include:

1.  **Business name:** No obligation to select a trade name, but free to do so.
2.  **Service tax registration:** Form ST 1 required if taxable services exceed 10 lakh per financial year.
3.  **VAT/CST registration:** VAT for intrastate sales (threshold varies), CST for interstate sales (imperative).
4.  **Others:** PAN Card of proprietor, business bank account, Shops & Establishment License, EPF Registration, Importer Exporter Code (if applicable).
5.  **Payment of taxes:** Sole trader bears the taxes of the business as they are a single legal entity.

## Case Study-I: Satya Brata Dey: SHREE LEATHERS

### Satya Brata Dey

There are long lines outside Satya Brata Dey's Shree Leather Shoe Stores in Calcutta.

### From Rags to Riches

In November 1986, Satya came to Calcutta from Jamshedpur with little money. He wore shoes made by his father. His mother gave him a few hundred rupees. Today, Satya Brata Dey is a millionaire, providing jobs in West Bengal, a state with high unemployment.

### Parents had a strong influence on his life

Satya's mother was strong and encouraged him to "Always win." Satya learned this lesson well, winning against great odds. His father owned a small shoe shop in Jamshedpur and made sturdy shoes. Satya helped him as a teenager, learning the art of making good-looking, durable shoes at a reasonable cost. This apprenticeship taught him a priceless craft that would later make him a millionaire and job creator. He remembers his father's hands with love and pride. Satya is proud that he can still make shoes with his own hands.

From his father, Satya also learned the dignity of labour. He has great respect for working people and treats his employees like family, receiving their loyalty in return. This is one secret of his success.

### What? Start your own business? That's despicable

In Satya's Bengali community, running one's own business was looked down upon as risky, with businessmen seen as potentially exploiting others. A secure government job ("babu") or even 'service' (job in private business) was preferred for stability, marriage prospects, and pension.

### Making shoes was low in the prestige ladder

Doing business was risky, but making shoes was considered very low prestige. Satya faced this prejudice. He remembered his father's friend questioning why he wasted time helping in the shop instead of learning skills for a job. This comment stuck with Satya as he searched for a place to start his shoe business in Calcutta. He was grateful he had learned the trade, as there were no jobs, and he never considered looking for one anyway.

### Satya dreamed big but reality hit hard

Since childhood, Satya wanted his own business, dreaming of a factory like TISCO. Hot, tired, and hungry in Calcutta, he found a rundown showroom for rent on a deserted street. Using his mother's money for rent, it became his home and 'showroom'. It was infested and dirty, but for Satya, it was his dream showroom, his place.

He cleaned and fixed it himself, sweeping, mopping, cleaning windows, and painting walls until it looked bright and ready. "Never be afraid to dream big," he advises. "You can make your dreams come true."

### Satya Starts his business

Satya started by buying six pairs of ladies' chappals, determined not to borrow. He sold them at Rs. 69 each, made a small profit, and reinvested. After a few months, a profit of Rs. 300 thrilled him; he was really in business.

### Satya's business philosophy develops

Satya's showroom was in New Market. While others closed by 6 pm, Satya opened early and stayed open late, even without customers. He reasoned, "What if there was one customer who needed shoes late in the day when the other shops were closed?"

Gradually, Satya started manufacturing his own shoes, positioning them as "poor man's shoes" – good quality and inexpensive. He kept margins low to attract many customers. Even ministers and businesspeople bought his shoes. Satisfied customers brought friends and family, and his business grew rapidly due to the winning combination of quality and price. He also kept competitors' products for comparison, highlighting Sreeleathers' value.

### Customer service is of prime importance

Customer service is always important at Sree leathers. Satya offers tea, coffee, or coke so customers spend time and buy more. He tells a story of a woman waiting for another shop to open who was invited in, offered coffee, and ended up buying 12 pairs of shoes without pressure. "We don't just sell shoes. We make our customers feel good. That's what brings them back," says Satya Brata Dey.

A man in a hurry for a train bought shoes, returned to buy four more pairs, and continues to be a loyal customer from out of town. About 15% of his customers are from out of town.

### Cut out the middleman

Manufacturing his own shoes eliminates middlemen, allowing lower prices compared to companies like Bata with high overheads. He imports materials directly from manufacturers, further avoiding intermediaries.

### He keeps his employees happy and motivated

Satya believes motivating staff is critical. A disgruntled employee won't provide good customer service, which drives success. He treats long-term staff like family, talks with them, and helps solve problems. They feel like owners.

He has a small team of dedicated managers working long hours. Paying vendors and suppliers on time is another key to success; he gets angry if they aren't paid promptly.

### Sources of stress

Government authorities are a major source of stress. Small bureaucrats harass for no reason. He recounted a story where policemen and municipal employees demanded bribes during construction of a new showroom, despite having legal permissions. Satya refused to pay bribes, contacting higher authorities to resolve the issues, though it caused delays and stress.

### Growing, growing, franchising

Sree leathers has about 15 outlets in Eastern India. Only one is his own; the rest are franchises. Demand for franchises is high, with people seeking recommendations from high-ranking officials. Satya is selective to protect quality and reputation. His shoes are sold abroad (Greece, Austria, Netherlands, Germany, UK, USA, Singapore, Saudi Arabia) as non-brand name shoes.

Expansion now means creating jobs, which is important to him, especially in West Bengal where jobs are scarce. He feels it would be selfish not to expand. He employs about 150 people directly and estimates another 150 through his franchises, creating employment for at least 300 people. It's a win-win situation. Sree leathers is worth several crores today. The young man who came with little has come a long way. His secret: hard work, dignity of labour, and big dreams.
