This section explains the concept of working capital, its need for day-to-day operations, the operating cycle, and different definitions of working capital.

# Working Capital

Successful business operation requires adequate capital, in addition to a good idea and plan.

## Need for Capital

Capital is needed for:
a) Investing in **longer-term assets** (Fixed Assets) like land, building, machinery. These are used for main business activities and don't directly generate cash inflow from sale (unless retired).
b) Funding **day-to-day operations** (Working Capital) like buying raw materials, paying rent, wages, utility bills.

**Working Capital** is the money needed to fund the normal, day-to-day operations. It ensures liquidity to pay debts and expenses as they become due, especially during start-up before profitability is reached.

Our discussion focuses on Working Capital: computation, sources, etc.

## The Operating Cycle

The need for working capital arises from the time lag between spending money on inputs and receiving money from sales.

For manufacturing: buy raw materials -> convert to product -> pay expenses during conversion -> sell product -> receive cash from customer.
For trading: buy goods -> sell goods -> receive cash.

The duration between buying raw material (or goods) and receiving cash from the customer is the **"Operating Cycle"** or **Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)**. It's the time cash is tied up in operations.

Different products/businesses have different operating cycles. Longer cycles require more working capital; shorter cycles require less.

## Gross and Net Working Capital

### Financial Concept

**Gross working capital** (current capital or circulating capital) is the sum of all **current assets**. Current assets are items expected to convert to cash within the operating cycle (or generally within a year), including:

*   Cash
*   Inventory (raw materials, WIP, finished goods, spares)
*   Accounts Receivable (trade debtors)
*   Short-term investments

**Current liabilities** represent short-term funding sources, due within a short period (generally a year), including:

*   Payables to vendors and service providers
*   Employee dues
*   Short-term borrowings
*   Provisions

| Current Assets:        | Current Liabilities: |
| :--------------------- | :------------------- |
| Stock                  | Trade creditors      |
| Debtors                | Short term loans     |
| Cash                   | Outstanding expenses |
| Short term investment  |                      |
