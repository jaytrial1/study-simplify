This section defines inventory and inventory control, discusses characteristics of inventory items, introduces ABC analysis based on Pareto's Principle, and explains the concept and calculation of Economic Order Quantity (EOQ).

# Inventory Control and Economic Order Quantity

## Inventory

**Inventory** generally means a detailed list of items. In business, it refers to raw materials, semi-finished goods, consumables, spare parts, finished goods, etc., required directly or indirectly for sales or representing stages in production.

Inventory, in this context, deals with **tangible items** in manufacturing or trading businesses.

In accounting, inventory refers to the **value** of items owned by the business, regardless of location. Valuation methods differ for accounting purposes.

## Control

**Inventory control** systems help achieve the "desirable behavior" of inventory items. Desirable behavior includes avoiding stock-outs while minimizing costs. Achieving both simultaneously is the challenge. A well-designed system helps manage this. Control has physical and fiscal (monetary) aspects.

# Items that Make Up the Inventory

Understanding item characteristics is key to designing a good inventory control system.

*   **Stock Keeping Unit (SKU) code:** A unique code identifying each item based on characteristics (color, size, weight, etc.). SKUs are basic units for data collection, analysis, and tracking (e.g., using Bar Codes, RFID).
*   **Motley crowd:** Inventory comprises diverse SKUs classified into Raw Materials, Packing Materials, Spare Parts, WIP, Finished Goods, Consumables, etc. Treatment differs for each class.
*   **Space:** Space needs vary and aren't proportional to cost (bulky low-value vs. high-value low-volume).
*   **Value:** SKUs have different values (see Pareto Principle).
*   **Lead time:** Time to manufacture or procure, influenced by various factors and can vary.
*   **Standard vs. made to order:** Some items are commodities with many suppliers; others are custom-made with limited sources.
*   **Seasonality of supply:** Agricultural products have seasonal supply, impacting inventory planning.
*   **Demand not uniform or predictable:** Demand can be seasonal or difficult to forecast (e.g., spare parts).
*   **Shelf life:** Perishable items require special storage and have expiry dates, imposing constraints.
*   **Safety aspects:** Hazardous items require special storage precautions.
*   **Obsolescence:** Technology changes can reduce demand for certain items.

These characteristics require different control systems for different item categories.

## Pareto's Principle and ABC Analysis for Control

**Pareto's Principle** (the "80/20 rule") states that a small percentage of causes produce a large percentage of effects. In business, 20% of customers might account for 80% of sales, or 20% of inventory items for 80% of inventory value. The principle highlights the "vital few" and "trivial many". Its value is focusing efforts on the 20% that matter most.

### Examining Inventory

Businesses often see a Pareto distribution in inventory value: a small percentage of items account for a large percentage of total inventory value. Focusing control efforts on these high-value items is efficient.

## ABC Analysis

**ABC analysis** is an inventory control technique based on Pareto's Principle, classifying SKUs into three categories based on value or consumption:

*   **"A" items:** High consumption value. Typically 10-20% of SKUs account for 70-80% of consumption value.
*   **"B" items:** Moderate consumption value. Typically 15-25% of SKUs account for 10-20% of consumption value.
*   **"C" items:** Low consumption value. Typically 65-75% of SKUs account for 5-10% of consumption value.

Control strategy focuses effort:
*   **"A" items:** Tightly controlled (close monitoring, careful forecasting, planned ordering to avoid stock-outs or excess).
*   **"B" items:** Watched closely, but ordering reviewed less often.
*   **"C" items:** Minimal controls, can be ordered in bulk. Focus is simply on not running out.

"A" items are outstandingly important, "B" average, "C" relatively unimportant for control focus. More attention is given to A, less to B, least to C.

### Suggested Policy Guidelines for A, B & C Classes

| (A) items (High Consumption Value) | (B) items (Moderate Consumption Value) | (C) item (Low Consumption Value) |
| :--------------------------------- | :------------------------------------- | :------------------------------- |
| Very strict consumption control    | Moderate control                       | Loose control                    |
| No/very low safety stock           | Low safety stock                       | High safety stock                |
| Phased delivery (Weekly)           | Once in three months                   | Once in 6 months                 |
| Weekly control report              | Monthly control report                 | Quarterly report                 |
| Maximum follow up                  | Periodic follow up                     | Exceptional                      |
| As many sources as possible        | Two or more reliable                   | Two reliable                     |
| Accurate forecasts                 | Estimates on past data                 | Rough estimate                   |
| Central purchasing /storage        | Combination purchasing                 | Decentralised                    |
| Max. efforts to control LT         | Moderate                               | Min. clerical efforts            |
| To be handled by Sr. officers      | Middle level                           | Can be delegated                 |

ABC (Always Better Control) analysis helps improve inventory control.

# Economic Order Quantity (EOQ)

Considering monetary implications, an effective inventory control system aims to avoid stock-outs while minimizing costs associated with ordering and holding inventory.

Assuming future demand is known and uniform, we need to determine **when to place an order** (Reorder Point) and **how much to order** (Order Quantity).

**Reorder Point** considers lead time and demand during lead time.
Reorder formula = Average daily usage rate × Lead time in days

To account for variability in demand or lead time, **safety stock** is used. Reorder level = (Average daily usage rate × Lead time) + Safety Stock. Safety stock doesn't impact EOQ calculation but is vital for control.

## Costs

Two main costs:
1.  **Ordering Cost (P):** Cost of placing and receiving one order (paperwork, receiving, inspection, handling).
2.  **Inventory Carrying Cost (C):** Cost of holding one unit of inventory per period (cost of tied-up money, space, insurance, perishability, pilferage, obsolescence). Often expressed as a percentage of unit price.

## Determining EOQ (Q*)

The **Economic Order Quantity (Q*)** is the order quantity that minimizes the total annual cost (ordering cost + carrying cost).

Assumptions for the basic EOQ model:
1.  Future demand (D) is known and uniform.
2.  Unit price is constant (no quantity discounts).

Let:
*   D: Annual demand (units)
*   P: Cost of placing one order (₹)
*   C: Inventory carrying cost per unit per year (₹)
*   Q: Order quantity (units)

Total number of orders per year = D / Q
Total annual ordering cost = (D / Q) * P

Average inventory level (excluding safety stock) = Q / 2
Total annual inventory carrying cost (excluding safety stock) = (Q / 2) * C

Total annual cost (excluding safety stock cost) = (P * D / Q) + (C * Q / 2)

The total cost is minimized when ordering cost equals carrying cost:
P * D / Q = C * Q / 2
2 * P * D = C * Q²
Q² = (2 * P * D) / C
**Q (EOQ) = √((2 * P * D) / C)**

*For those with calculus background:* Differentiating the total annual cost equation with respect to Q and setting the derivative to zero finds the minimum cost point (EOQ).
d(Total Cost)/dQ = d((PD/Q) + (CQ/2))/dQ = -PD/Q² + C/2
Setting derivative to 0: -PD/Q² + C/2 = 0
PD/Q² = C/2
2PD = CQ²
Q² = 2PD/C
Q = √((2PD)/C)

Formula for calculating EOQ:

$$
Q = \sqrt{\frac{2PD}{C}}
$$

Where:
*   Q: Economic Order Quantity
*   D: Annual Demand for the item (SKU)
*   P: Cost of placing and receiving one Order (does not include purchase price)
*   C: Inventory carrying cost per unit per year.

## Numerical Examples

**Example 1:**
Annual jeans sales = 1,200 units (₹ 100/month, so 12 * 100 = 1200 per year is incorrect interpretation of rate, it means 1200 units/year at some price). Let's assume D=1200 units/year. Cost of placing order (P) = ₹ 500. Inventory holding cost (C) = ₹ 30 per annum. What is EOQ?
D = 1,200
P = 500
C = 30
EOQ = √((2 * 500 * 1200) / 30)
EOQ = √((1,200,000) / 30)
EOQ = √(40,000)
EOQ = 200 jeans.

> **Answer:** The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) for jeans is **200 units**.

**Example 2:**
Annual pen sales = 30,000 units (D). Purchase cost = ₹ 6 per pen. Holding cost per annum = 20% of purchase cost (C). Ordering cost (P) = ₹ 500 per order. What is EOQ?
D = 30,000
P = 500
C = 20% of ₹ 6 = 0.20 * 6 = ₹ 1.20
EOQ = √((2 * 500 * 30000) / 1.20)
EOQ = √((30,000,000) / 1.20)
EOQ = √(25,000,000)
EOQ = 5,000 pens.

> **Answer:** The Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) for pens is **5,000 units**.

## Final Note on Inventory Control and EOQ

Inventory control involves monetary, physical, safety, and other aspects. Systems like ABC analysis are important. Other systems include Just-in-Time (JIT) and perpetual inventory.

EOQ is a key factor in inventory management, but the formula presented is simplified. More advanced formulas exist for seasonal demand, fluctuating lead times, and price breaks, but are beyond the scope here.
