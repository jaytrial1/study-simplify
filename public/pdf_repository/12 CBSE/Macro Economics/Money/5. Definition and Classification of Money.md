# Definition and Classification of Money

Money can be defined and classified in several ways based on its functions, legal status, liquidity, and scope.

## Definition of Money

A comprehensive definition incorporates money's functions: **Money is anything which is generally accepted as a medium of exchange, measure of value, store of value and means for standard of deferred payment.**

The term 'money' covers items like coins, currency notes, and cheques used for transactions and settling claims. Money can be any commodity chosen by common agreement to facilitate exchange.

## Legal Definition of Money

Legally, money is what the law declares it to be. Based on legal recognition, money is of two types:

1.  **Legal Tender Money:** Money that must be legally accepted for payment of debts. A creditor cannot refuse it.
    *   **Limited Legal Tender:** Legally accepted only up to a certain amount. Beyond this limit, acceptance is optional, and refusal doesn't allow legal action. In India, coins are limited legal tender (e.g., ₹1 coins up to ₹1,000 as per Coinage Act, 2011).
    *   **Unlimited Legal Tender:** Legally accepted for any amount. Refusal can lead to legal action. In India, paper notes are unlimited legal tender.
2.  **Non-Legal Tender Money or Optional Money:** Generally accepted but not legally required to be accepted. Examples include cheques, bank drafts, and bills of exchange. Their acceptance is voluntary.

## Bank Money

**Bank money** refers to demand deposits in commercial banks. These can be withdrawn on demand using cheques. Bank money is considered **Non-Legal Tender Money** or **Optional Money**.

## Functional Definition of Money

This definition states that money is anything that performs the four basic functions: medium of exchange, measure of value, standard of deferred payments, and store of value.

## Definition on the Basis of Liquidity

Liquidity refers to the ease and speed with which an asset can be converted into money without loss of value.
*   **Money (Liquid form):** Assets like coins, currency notes, and bank money are highly liquid and generally accepted.
*   **Near Money:** Financial assets easily convertible into money but not as liquid as cash and not legal tender. Examples include certain savings deposits, fixed deposits, Treasury Bills, and bonds. They cannot be used directly for purchases but can be quickly converted to cash.

## Definition on the Basis of Scope

*   **Narrow Definition of Money:** Includes highly liquid forms that perform all four main functions: currency notes, coins, and demand deposits.
*   **Broad Definition of Money:** Includes narrow money plus less liquid assets like time deposits in banks and post offices, which can be converted into chequeable deposits relatively easily. Broad Money = Money Assets + Near Money.

## Classification of Money

Money can be classified based on the relationship between its face value (value as money) and its intrinsic value (value as a commodity).

1.  **Full-bodied Money:** Money where the face value equals the intrinsic value (Money Value = Commodity Value). Historically, silver coins in the British period might have fit this.
    *   **Face Value:** The value printed on the money unit (e.g., ₹10 on a note).
    *   **Intrinsic Value:** The value of the material (e.g., metal) the money unit is made of.
2.  **Representative Full-bodied Money:** Usually paper money whose face value is much higher than its commodity value. It is accepted because it is fully backed by a metallic reserve (gold or silver) and can be exchanged for it.
    *   **Convertible Paper Money:** Notes freely exchangeable for full-bodied money (gold/silver) at the holder's option. Full backing isn't always necessary as not all notes are converted simultaneously.
    *   **Inconvertible Paper Money:** Paper money that cannot be converted into full-bodied money by the holder. It circulates based on government regulation and trust, not metallic backing (e.g., the Indian one-rupee note historically).
3.  **Credit Money:** Money whose intrinsic value is significantly lower than its face value (Money Value > Commodity Value). Examples include modern currency notes, credit cards, and bank deposits.
    *   **Token Coins:** Small coins whose metal value is less than their face value (e.g., modern Indian coins like ₹1, ₹2, ₹5, ₹10, ₹20).
    *   **Representative Token Money:** 100% backed and redeemable in a commodity like gold/silver, but the market value of the commodity offered is less than the paper's face value.
    *   **Circulating Promissory Notes (Central Bank Issued):** Currency notes issued by the central bank (like RBI notes in India). They are promises to pay the bearer the stated sum, and their commodity value (as paper) is much less than their face value.
    *   **Demand Deposits in Banks:** Deposits that can be withdrawn or transferred by cheque. These are claims against the bank, not backed by bullion. Their commodity value (of the cheque) is low compared to the transaction value. They are convenient for large transactions, reducing the need for cash.
