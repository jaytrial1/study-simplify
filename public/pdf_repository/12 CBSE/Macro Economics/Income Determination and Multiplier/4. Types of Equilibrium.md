This section discusses the different possibilities for the equilibrium level of income in the Keynesian framework relative to the full employment level, including underemployment and over full employment equilibrium, and the concept of inflationary pressure.

# Types of Equilibrium

According to the classical economists, the equilibrium level of income is always attained at the full employment level, implying the absence of involuntary unemployment. However, as per the Keynesian theory, the equilibrium level can be achieved at three possibilities:

1.  **Full employment level:** Equilibrium where all available resources are fully utilized.
2.  **Underemployment level:** Equilibrium at a level of output and employment less than the full employment level.
3.  **Over full employment level:** Equilibrium at a level of output that the economy cannot sustain in the long run without inflationary pressures.

Let us discuss these three possibilities of equilibrium level.

## Full Employment Equilibrium

This refers to a situation when the aggregate demand is equal to the aggregate supply precisely at the full employment level of output.

## Underemployment Equilibrium

This refers to a situation when the aggregate demand is equal to the aggregate supply at a level where the resources are not fully employed. It occurs at an output level prior to the full employment level.

## Over Full Employment Equilibrium

This refers to a situation when AD is equal to AS at an output level that is beyond the full employment level. This occurs when aggregate demand is so high that it would require resources beyond the economy's full capacity to produce the demanded output.

## Over Full Employment Equilibrium Creates Inflationary Pressure

*   Over Full Employment Equilibrium signifies that planned expenditure (AD) is equal to planned output (AS) at a level higher than the full employment level.
*   However, in reality, actual output cannot increase beyond this level because the economy is already operating at full employment, and there is no idle capacity or unemployed resources available to increase production further in the short run.
*   So, any increase in AD beyond the full employment output level will lead to an increase in the general price level (i.e., inflation) because the increased demand cannot be met by increased supply. There will be no real increase in output, only a rise in prices.

## Short-run Fixed Price Analysis of Product Market

In the short run, prices are assumed to be fixed. This is because producers take some time to adjust prices in response to excess supply or demand. They first tend to update production plans. For instance, with excess supply, firms plan to produce less in the next cycle to avoid inventory accumulation. Also, an individual firm is often too small to influence the overall market price and must accept the prevailing price.

As a result, the price level remains constant in the short run and is assumed to change only when the economy is unable to eliminate the effect of excess demand or supply through output adjustments. So, it is assumed that prices remain constant in the short run and vary in the long run.

To derive aggregate demand under fixed prices of final goods (i.e., in the short run), it is assumed that the **elasticity of supply is infinite**. This means suppliers are willing to supply whatever amount consumers demand at the given constant price. This assumption is necessary because if quantity supplied exceeded or fell short of quantity demanded at this price, the price would change due to excess supply or demand.

So, when aggregate supply (AS) is assumed to be infinitely elastic at the fixed price, equilibrium is solely determined by aggregate demand (AD). This is known as the **'Effective Demand Principle'**. Effective Demand refers to the total demand of the community which is met by a corresponding supply at the fixed price.
