This section details the AD-AS approach to determining equilibrium, explaining the components of AD and AS, using a schedule and diagram, and describing the adjustment process when AD is not equal to AS.

# Aggregate Demand-Aggregate Supply Approach (AD-AS Approach)

According to the Keynesian theory, the equilibrium level of income in an economy is determined when aggregate demand, represented by the C+I curve, is equal to the total output (Aggregate Supply or AS).

Aggregate demand in the two-sector model comprises two components:

*   **Consumption expenditure (C):** This component varies directly with the level of income; consumption rises as income increases.
*   **Investment expenditure (I):** This is assumed to be independent of the level of income, meaning it is autonomous.

Thus, the AD curve is represented by the (C+I) curve in this analysis.

Aggregate supply is the total output of goods and services in the national income. It is typically depicted by a 45° line in a diagram, representing the points where planned aggregate expenditure equals total income/output. Since income received is either consumed or saved, the AS curve can also be represented by the (C+S) curve.

The determination of the equilibrium level of income using the AD-AS approach can be understood with the help of the following schedule and diagram:

In the figure, the AD or (C+I) curve shows the desired level of expenditure by consumers and firms corresponding to each level of income. The economy is in equilibrium at point 'E' where the (C+I) curve intersects the 45° line (representing AS).

*   'E' is the equilibrium point because at this point, the level of desired spending on consumption and investment exactly equals the level of total output.
*   OY is the equilibrium level of output corresponding to point E.
*   In the table, the equilibrium level of income is ₹ 400 crores, where AD (or C+I) = AS = ₹ 400 crores.

This situation at equilibrium is referred to as **'Effective Demand'**. Effective demand refers to that level of AD which becomes 'effective' because it is equal to AS.

If there is any deviation from the equilibrium level of output, i.e., when planned spending (AD) is not equal to planned output (AS), then a process of readjustment will start in the economy, and output will tend to adjust up or down until AD and AS are equal again.

## Adjustment When AD is More Than AS

When planned spending (AD) is more than planned output (AS), the (C+I) curve lies above the 45° line. This means that consumers and firms together plan to buy more goods than firms are currently willing to produce.

As a result, the *planned inventory* would fall below the desired level. Firms experience a reduction in their stock of unsold goods.

To bring the inventory back to the desired level, firms would increase production. This increase in production leads to increased employment and output, which in turn raises the income level. This process continues until the economy returns to the output level OY, where AD equals AS, and there is no further tendency to change.

*Note: Planned Inventory is the unsold stock of goods anticipated based on expected sales, while Unplanned Inventory refers to unanticipated changes in stock due to unexpected sales levels.*

## Adjustment When AD is Less Than AS

When planned spending (AD) is less than planned output (AS), the (C+I) curve lies below the 45° line. This means that consumers and firms together plan to buy fewer goods than firms are currently willing to produce.

As a result, *planned inventory* would rise above the desired level. Firms experience an unwanted accumulation of unsold goods.

To clear the unwanted increase in inventory, firms plan to decrease production. This decrease in production leads to decreased employment and output, which in turn reduces the income level. This process continues until the economy returns to the output level OY, where AD equals AS, and there is no further tendency to change.

It must be noted that the equilibrium level may or may not be at the level of full employment. Equilibrium is possible even at a level lower than the full employment level. For instance, in the table, the employment level is 40 lakhs corresponding to the equilibrium income of ₹ 400 crores. This is not necessarily the full employment level, as employment might increase further with higher output levels.
