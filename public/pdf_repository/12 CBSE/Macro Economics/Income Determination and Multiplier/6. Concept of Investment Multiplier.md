This section introduces the concept of the investment multiplier, explaining how an initial change in investment leads to a larger change in income and deriving the algebraic relationships between the multiplier, MPC, and MPS.

# Concept of Investment Multiplier

The concept of 'Investment Multiplier' is an important contribution of Prof. J. M. Keynes. Keynes believed that an initial increment in investment increases the final income by many times. The multiplier expresses the relationship between an initial increment in investment and the resulting increase in aggregate income.

In practice, it is observed that when investment is increased by a certain amount, the change in income is not restricted to the extent of the initial investment, but it changes several times the change in investment. In other words, the change in income is a multiple of the change in investment. The multiplier explains how many times the income increases as a result of an increase in investment.

Multiplier (k) is defined as the ratio of the increase in national income ($\Delta Y$) due to an increase in investment ($\Delta I$).

$$
k=\frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta I}
$$

Suppose an additional investment ($\Delta I$) of ₹ 4,000 crores in an economy generates an additional income ($\Delta Y$) of ₹ 16,000 crores. The value of the multiplier (k), in this case, will be:

$$
k=\frac{16,000}{4,000}=4
$$

This means that income increased 4 times with a single increase in investment.

## Multiplier and MPC

There exists a direct relationship between the Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC) and the value of the multiplier. Higher the MPC, the more significant will be the value of the multiplier, and vice-versa.

The concept of the multiplier is based on the fact that **one person's expenditure is another person's income**. When investment is increased, it directly increases the income of those involved in the investment project. These recipients then spend a part of this increased income on consumption. The amount of this increased income spent on consumption depends on the value of the MPC.

*   In case of a **higher MPC**, people will spend a large proportion of their increased income on consumption. This consumption expenditure becomes income for others, who in turn spend a large proportion of that income, and so on. This leads to a larger cumulative increase in income, resulting in a higher value of the multiplier.
*   In case of a **low MPC**, people will spend a lesser proportion of their increased income on consumption, saving more instead. This reduces the subsequent rounds of spending and income generation. In such a case, the value of the multiplier will be comparatively less.

Thus, the value of the multiplier depends significantly upon the MPC.

## Algebraic Relationship between Multiplier and MPC

The algebraic relation between the Multiplier and MPC can be derived from the equilibrium condition. We know that, at equilibrium, income (Y) is the sum of consumption (C) and investment (I):

$$
Y=C+I
$$

If there is a change in investment ($\Delta I$), it will lead to a change in income ($\Delta Y$). This change in income will induce a change in consumption ($\Delta C$). So, any change in income ($\Delta Y$) will also be equal to the sum of the induced change in consumption ($\Delta C$) and the initial change in investment ($\Delta I$):

$$
\Delta Y=\Delta C+\Delta I
$$

We know that the change in consumption is determined by the MPC and the change in income:

$$
\Delta C = MPC \times \Delta Y
$$

Substitute this into the equation for $\Delta Y$:

$$
\Delta Y = (MPC \times \Delta Y) + \Delta I
$$

Rearrange the equation to isolate $\Delta I$:

$$
\Delta I = \Delta Y - (MPC \times \Delta Y)
$$

$$
\Delta I = \Delta Y (1 - MPC)
$$

Now, recall the definition of the multiplier, $k = \frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta I}$. We can rearrange the equation above to find this ratio:

$$
\frac{\Delta Y}{\Delta I} = \frac{1}{1 - MPC}
$$

Therefore, the algebraic relationship is:

$$
k=\frac{1}{1-M P C}
$$

## Multiplier (k) in terms of MPS

We know that the multiplier is given by:

$$
k=\frac{1}{(1-M P C)}
$$

We also know that the sum of MPC and MPS is equal to 1 (since income is either consumed or saved):

$$
MPC + MPS = 1
$$

Rearranging this gives:

$$
1 - MPC = MPS
$$

Substituting this into the multiplier formula, we get the multiplier in terms of MPS:

$$
k=\frac{1}{M P S}
$$

It means the value of the multiplier can be calculated if either the MPC or the MPS is known.

## Maximum Value of Multiplier

The maximum value of the multiplier is **infinity** when the value of MPC is 1. MPC = 1 indicates that the economy decides to consume the whole of its additional income, saving nothing. In this theoretical scenario, each round of spending is as large as the previous one, leading to a continuous and infinitely large increase in aggregate demand and income.

Proof:
We know; $k=\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{MPC}}$
When MPC = 1, then:

$$
k=\frac{1}{1-1}=\frac{1}{0}=\infty
$$

(as any number, when divided by 0, gives infinity)

## Minimum Value of Multiplier

The minimum value of the multiplier is **one** when the value of MPC is zero. MPC = 0 indicates that the economy decides to save the whole of its additional income, and nothing is spent as consumption expenditure. In this case, the initial increase in investment leads to an equal increase in income, but because none of that additional income is consumed, there are no further rounds of spending and income generation. So, the total increase in income ($\Delta Y$) will be equal to the initial increase in investment ($\Delta I$), i.e., $\Delta Y = \Delta I$. Here, the value of the multiplier is equal to 1.

Proof:
We know; $k=\frac{1}{1-\mathrm{MPC}}$
When MPC = 0, then:

$$
k=\frac{1}{1-0}=\frac{1}{1}=1
$$
