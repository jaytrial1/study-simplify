This section explains the central bank's role in managing the money supply and credit in the economy using quantitative monetary policy tools.

# Controller of Money Supply and Credit

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) regulates the money supply through its **Monetary Policy**. This policy is adopted by the Central Bank to control credit or money supply in the economy. RBI's monopoly on currency issue gives it control over credit and money supply. RBI uses various instruments for this:

1.  **Repo (Repurchase) Rate:**
    *   Rate at which the central bank lends money to commercial banks for short-term needs, against approved securities.
    *   **Increase in Repo Rate:** Increases commercial banks' borrowing cost from RBI $\rightarrow$ Banks increase their lending rates $\rightarrow$ Borrowers are discouraged $\rightarrow$ Reduces commercial banks' credit creation ability $\rightarrow$ Reduces money supply.
    *   **Decrease in Repo Rate:** Opposite effect, encourages lending and increases money supply.

    | Increase in Repo Rate (or Bank Rate) by RBI |                                             |
    | :------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------ |
    | Increases                                   | $\rightarrow$ Cost of Borrowings for Banks  |
    | Increases                                   | $\rightarrow$ Lending Rates by Banks        |
    | Discourages                                 | $\rightarrow$ Borrowers from Taking Loans   |
    | Reduces                                     | $\rightarrow$ Credit Creating Power         |
    | Reduces                                     | $\rightarrow$ Money Supply in the Economy   |

    | Decrease in Repo Rate (or Bank Rate) by RBI |                                              |
    | :------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------- |
    | Decreases                                   | $\rightarrow$ Cost of Borrowings for Banks   |
    | Decreases                                   | $\rightarrow$ Lending Rates by Banks         |
    | Encourages                                  | $\rightarrow$ Borrowers to Take more Loans   |
    | Increases                                   | $\rightarrow$ Credit Creating Power          |
    | Increases                                   | $\rightarrow$ Money Supply in the Economy    |

2.  **Bank Rate (or Discount Rate):**
    *   Rate at which the central bank lends money to commercial banks for long-term needs.
    *   Used to control credit and has a similar effect to the Repo Rate.
    *   **Increase in Bank Rate:** Increases commercial banks' long-term borrowing cost $\rightarrow$ Banks increase lending rates $\rightarrow$ Discourages long-term borrowing $\rightarrow$ Reduces credit creation.

3.  **Reverse Repo Rate (or Reverse Repurchase Rate):**
    *   Rate at which commercial banks can deposit their surplus funds with the Central Bank for a short period. It's the rate RBI pays to commercial banks for parking funds with it.
    *   **When Reverse Repo Rate is raised:** Encourages banks to deposit funds with RBI $\rightarrow$ Reduces funds available for lending $\rightarrow$ Reduces lending capability.
    *   **Lowering Reverse Repo Rate:** Opposite effect, encourages banks to lend more.
    *   During periods like Covid-2019, banks with surplus deposits parked funds with RBI earning interest via the reverse repo rate.

    **Always Remember:** Repo Rate and Reverse Repo Rate work oppositely in terms of direction of funds (RBI lending vs. RBI borrowing/accepting deposits) but are used together to manage the lending capacity of commercial banks. To reduce credit creation, both are generally increased; to increase credit creation, both are generally reduced.

4.  **Open Market Operations (OMO):**
    *   Refers to the buying and selling of government securities by the Central Bank in the open market, involving the public and commercial banks.
    *   **Sale of securities by Central Bank:** Banks or public buy securities, paying money to RBI $\rightarrow$ Reduces reserves of commercial banks $\rightarrow$ Limits banks' ability to create credit $\rightarrow$ Decreases money supply.
    *   **Purchase of securities by Central Bank:** RBI buys securities, paying money to banks or public $\rightarrow$ Increases reserves of commercial banks $\rightarrow$ Raises banks' ability to give credit $\rightarrow$ Increases money supply.

    ## Sale of Securities by RBI
    | Reduces   | $\rightarrow$ Cash Reserves of Commercial Banks |
    | :-------- | :---------------------------------------------- |
    | Reduces   | $\rightarrow$ Credit Creating Power             |
    | Reduces   | $\rightarrow$ Money Supply in the Economy       |

    ## Purchase of Securities by RBI
    | Increases | $\rightarrow$ Cash Reserves of Commercial Banks |
    | :-------- | :---------------------------------------------- |
    | Increases | $\rightarrow$ Credit Creating Power             |
    | Increases | $\rightarrow$ Money Supply in the Economy       |

    ## Conditions Necessary for Success of Open Market Operations
    *   A well-developed and organized security market must exist.
    *   The sale/purchase of securities must effectively impact commercial banks' reserves.
    *   The central bank must hold sufficient securities to influence money supply.
    *   The value of government securities should be relatively stable.

5.  **Legal Reserve Requirements (Variable Reserve Ratio Method):**
    *   Commercial banks are legally required to maintain reserves, a direct method to control credit creation.
    *   **(i) Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR):** Minimum percentage of net demand and time liabilities (deposits) that banks must keep with the central bank.
        *   **Increase in CRR:** Reduces excess reserves of commercial banks $\rightarrow$ Limits their credit creating power.
        *   **Decrease in CRR:** Increases excess reserves $\rightarrow$ Expands credit creating power.
    *   **(ii) Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR):** Minimum percentage of net demand and time liabilities that banks must maintain *with themselves* in the form of designated liquid assets (like excess reserves, unencumbered* government/approved** securities, balances with other banks).
        *   **Increase in SLR:** Reduces the funds available for lending and limits banks' ability to sell securities or borrow against them $\rightarrow$ Reduces credit creation.
        *   **Decrease in SLR:** Increases funds available for lending $\rightarrow$ Expands credit creation.
    *   RBI can influence credit creation by changing CRR and/or SLR.

    *   *Unencumbered securities* are those not used as security for loans from the Central Bank.
    *   *Approved securities* are those whose repayment is guaranteed by the government.
