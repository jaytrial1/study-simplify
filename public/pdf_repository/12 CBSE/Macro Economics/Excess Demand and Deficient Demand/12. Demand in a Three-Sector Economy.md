# Excess and Deficient Demand in Three-Sector Economy

In a three-sector economy, with households, firms, and government, AD is the sum total of consumption (C), investment (I), and government expenditure (G). With the introduction of the government sector, the new $\mathrm{AD}_{1}$ curve $(\mathrm{C}+\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{G})$ lies above the old AD curve $(\mathrm{C}+\mathrm{I})$.

For simplicity, it is assumed that government expenditure is a constant amount. The new $\mathrm{AD}_{1}$ curve is parallel to the old AD curve because at every level of output, the vertical distance between the $(\mathrm{C}+\mathrm{I})$ curve and the $(\mathrm{C}+\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{G})$ curve is the constant amount of government expenditure (G). *Here's a detailed description of the image/graph, focusing on the economic insights:

**Description**

The image depicts a Keynesian Cross diagram, a tool used in macroeconomics to illustrate the relationship between aggregate demand and income/output/employment.

**Axes:**

*   **Y-axis:** Represents Aggregate Demand.
*   **X-axis:** Represents Income, Output, or Employment (which are often used interchangeably in this context).

**Lines:**

*   **45-degree line:** This is a reference line that shows where aggregate demand equals income/output. It represents the equilibrium condition.
*   **AD (C + I):** This is an aggregate demand curve that includes Consumption (C) and Investment (I). It shows the planned level of aggregate spending at each level of income. Note the slope of this curve is less than the slope of the 45-degree line.
*   **AD1 (C + I + G):** This is another aggregate demand curve that includes Consumption (C), Investment (I), and Government Spending (G). This curve is positioned higher than the AD (C + I) curve. It is shown by a dashed line.

**Key Elements:**

*   **"G" Vertical Arrow:** The vertical distance between the AD (C + I) curve and the AD1 (C + I + G) curve is marked with a double-headed arrow labeled "G". This represents the increase in aggregate demand due to government spending (G). This shows how government spending leads to an increase in the total spending in the economy.

**Insights:**

1.  **Components of Aggregate Demand:** The graph illustrates the components of aggregate demand (C, I, and G). It highlights that aggregate demand is the sum of these individual spending components.

2.  **Impact of Government Spending:** The inclusion of government spending (G) shifts the aggregate demand curve upward. This shift indicates that at any given level of income, there is now more aggregate demand in the economy when the government spends.

3.  **Keynesian Multiplier Effect:** While not explicitly shown, the diagram is a foundation for understanding the Keynesian multiplier effect. An increase in government spending will lead to a larger increase in equilibrium income/output. This is because the initial spending creates income for others, who then spend a portion of that income, and so on. The graph suggests that adding government spending into the economy causes an increase in aggregate demand, but doesn't specify how much higher the new equilibrium income/output would be. The multiplier effect is determined by the slope of the AD curves.

In summary, this graph presents a simplified model of aggregate demand and how government spending can influence it, which is a fundamental concept in Keynesian economics.*
