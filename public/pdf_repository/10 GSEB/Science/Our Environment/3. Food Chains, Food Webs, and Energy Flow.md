# Food Chains and Webs

A **food chain** is a series of organisms in which one organism feeds on another, transferring energy from one trophic level to the next.

Each step or level of the food chain forms a **trophic level**.
*   **First Trophic Level:** Autotrophs or **producers** (e.g., green plants) are at the first trophic level. They capture solar energy and make it available for heterotrophs or consumers.
*   **Second Trophic Level:** Herbivores or **primary consumers** come at this level.
*   **Third Trophic Level:** Small carnivores or **secondary consumers** are at this level.
*   **Fourth Trophic Level:** Larger carnivores or **tertiary consumers** form this level.

The interactions among various components of the environment involve the flow of energy from one component to another. Autotrophs capture sunlight energy and convert it into chemical energy, which supports all living activities. This energy then flows to heterotrophs and decomposers. However, as energy is converted from one form to another, some is lost to the environment as unusable heat.

Key findings about energy flow in ecosystems:
*   Green plants in a terrestrial ecosystem capture about **1%** of the energy of sunlight that falls on their leaves and convert it into food energy.
*   When green plants are eaten by primary consumers, a significant amount of energy is lost as heat to the environment, some is used for digestion and work, and the rest contributes to growth and reproduction. On average, only about **10%** of the food eaten is converted into the consumer's own body mass and becomes available for the next level of consumers. This is known as the **10% Law**.
*   Because so little energy is available for the next level of consumers, food chains generally consist of only three or four steps. The energy loss at each step is so great that very little usable energy remains after four trophic levels.
*   There are generally a greater number of individuals at the lower trophic levels of an ecosystem, with the greatest number being producers.
*   The length and complexity of food chains vary greatly. Each organism is often eaten by two or more other kinds of organisms, which in turn are eaten by several others. This complex, branching network of feeding relationships is called a **food web**.

From the energy flow diagram (Fig. 13.4), two important aspects become clear:
1.  **Unidirectional Flow:** The flow of energy is unidirectional. Energy captured by autotrophs does not revert back to solar input, and energy passed to herbivores does not return to autotrophs. As it moves progressively through trophic levels, it is no longer available to previous levels.
2.  **Progressive Diminishment:** The energy available at each trophic level diminishes progressively due to energy loss at each transfer.
