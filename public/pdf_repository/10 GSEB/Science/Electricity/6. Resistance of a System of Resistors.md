## Resistors in Series

What happens to the value of current when a number of resistors are connected in series in a circuit? What would be their equivalent resistance? Let's try to understand these with the help of activities.

### Activity 11.4

1.  Join three resistors of different values in series (e.g., $1 \Omega, 2 \Omega, 3 \Omega$).
2.  Connect them with a battery (e.g., 6 V), an ammeter, and a plug key, as shown in Figure 11.6.
3.  Plug the key and note the ammeter reading.

This observation means that in a series combination of resistors, the current is the same in every part of the circuit, or the same current flows through each resistor.

5.  Similarly, measure the potential difference across the other two resistors ($R_2$ and $R_3$) separately. Let these values be $V_2$ and $V_3$, respectively.

> **Observations for Activity 11.5:**
> You will observe that the total potential difference $V$ is equal to the sum of the potential differences across the individual resistors: $V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3$.

This means that the total potential difference across a combination of resistors in series is equal to the sum of the potential differences across the individual resistors. That is:

$$
V=V_{1}+V_{2}+V_{3}
$$

In the electric circuit shown in Figure 11.8, let $I$ be the current through the circuit. As observed in Activity 11.4, the current through each resistor ($R_1, R_2, R_3$) is also $I$.

It is possible to replace the three resistors joined in series by an equivalent single resistor of resistance $R_s$, such that the total potential difference $V$ across it and the total current $I$ through the circuit remain the same. Applying Ohm's law to the entire circuit, we have:
$$
V=I R_{s}
$$

On applying Ohm's law to the three resistors separately, we get:
$V_{1} = I R_{1}$
$V_{2} = I R_{2}$
$V_{3} = I R_{3}$

Substituting these into the equation for total potential difference ($V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3$):
$I R_{s} = I R_{1} + I R_{2} + I R_{3}$

Dividing by $I$ (since $I$ is common and non-zero):
$$
R_{s}=R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}
$$

We can conclude that when several resistors are joined in series, the **equivalent resistance ($R_s$) of the combination equals the sum of their individual resistances**. This means the total resistance in a series circuit is always greater than any individual resistance.

## Example 11.7

An electric lamp, whose resistance is $20 \Omega$, and a conductor of $4 \Omega$ resistance are connected to a 6 V battery (Figure 11.9). Calculate (a) the total resistance of the circuit, (b) the current through the circuit, and (c) the potential difference across the electric lamp and conductor.



---
**Image:**
public/images/10 CBSE/Science/Electricity/6. Resistance of a System of Resistors/img_img-9.png
---



*Figure 11.9: An electric lamp connected in series with a resistor of $4 \Omega$ to a 6 V battery. This is a series circuit.*

### Solution

Given:
Resistance of electric lamp $R_1 = 20 \Omega$
Resistance of conductor $R_2 = 4 \Omega$
Battery voltage $V = 6 \mathrm{~V}$

(a) **Total resistance of the circuit ($R_s$)**:
Since the lamp and conductor are connected in series, the total resistance is the sum of individual resistances:
$R_s = R_1 + R_2$
$R_s = 20 \Omega + 4 \Omega$
$R_s = 24 \Omega$

(b) **Current through the circuit ($I$)**:
Using Ohm's Law, $I = V / R_s$:
$I = 6 \mathrm{~V} / 24 \Omega$
$I = 0.25 \mathrm{~A}$

(c) **Potential difference across the electric lamp ($V_1$) and conductor ($V_2$)**:
In a series circuit, the current is the same through all components ($I = 0.25 \mathrm{~A}$).
For the electric lamp:
$V_1 = I \times R_1$
$V_1 = 0.25 \mathrm{~A} \times 20 \Omega$
$V_1 = 5 \mathrm{~V}$

For the conductor:
$V_2 = I \times R_2$
$V_2 = 0.25 \mathrm{~A} \times 4 \Omega$
$V_2 = 1 \mathrm{~V}$

*Verification*: The sum of potential differences across individual components should equal the total potential difference supplied by the battery: $V_1 + V_2 = 5 \mathrm{~V} + 1 \mathrm{~V} = 6 \mathrm{~V}$, which matches the battery voltage.

> You will observe that the total current $I$ (measured before the parallel combination) is equal to the sum of the separate currents through each branch of the combination. That is:
> $I = I_1 + I_2 + I_3$

This means that in a parallel circuit, the total current supplied by the source divides among the various branches, and the sum of the currents in the individual branches is equal to the total current. Also, the potential difference across each resistor in a parallel combination is the same.

Let $R_p$ be the equivalent resistance of the parallel combination of resistors. By applying Ohm's law to the entire parallel combination, we have:
$$
I=V / R_{p}
$$

On applying Ohm's law to each individual resistor, since the potential difference $V$ is the same across all of them:
$I_{1}=V / R_{1}$
$I_{2}=V / R_{2}$
$I_{3}=V / R_{3}$

Substituting these into the equation for total current ($I = I_1 + I_2 + I_3$):
$V / R_{p} = V / R_{1} + V / R_{2} + V / R_{3}$

Dividing by $V$ (since $V$ is common and non-zero):
$$
1 / R_{p}=1 / R_{1}+1 / R_{2}+1 / R_{3}
$$

Thus, we can conclude that the **reciprocal of the equivalent resistance of a group of resistances joined in parallel is equal to the sum of the reciprocals of the individual resistances**.

## Example 11.8

In the circuit diagram given in Figure 11.10, suppose the resistors $R_1, R_2,$ and $R_3$ have the values $5 \Omega, 10 \Omega, 30 \Omega$, respectively, which have been connected to a battery of 12 V. Calculate (a) the current through each resistor, (b) the total current in the circuit, and (c) the total circuit resistance.

### Solution

Given:
$R_1 = 5 \Omega$
$R_2 = 10 \Omega$
$R_3 = 30 \Omega$
Battery voltage $V = 12 \mathrm{~V}$

In a parallel circuit, the potential difference across each resistor is the same as the battery voltage, so $V = 12 \mathrm{~V}$ for all resistors.

(a) **Current through each resistor**:
Using Ohm's Law ($I = V/R$):
Current through $R_1$, $I_1 = V / R_1 = 12 \mathrm{~V} / 5 \Omega = 2.4 \mathrm{~A}$
Current through $R_2$, $I_2 = V / R_2 = 12 \mathrm{~V} / 10 \Omega = 1.2 \mathrm{~A}$
Current through $R_3$, $I_3 = V / R_3 = 12 \mathrm{~V} / 30 \Omega = 0.4 \mathrm{~A}$

(b) **Total current in the circuit ($I$)**:
The total current is the sum of the individual branch currents:
$I = I_1 + I_2 + I_3$
$I = (2.4 + 1.2 + 0.4) \mathrm{~A}$
$I = 4.0 \mathrm{~A}$

(c) **Total circuit resistance ($R_p$)**:
Using the formula for equivalent resistance in parallel:
$\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}$
$\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{5 \Omega} + \frac{1}{10 \Omega} + \frac{1}{30 \Omega}$
To add these fractions, find a common denominator, which is 30:
$\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{6}{30 \Omega} + \frac{3}{30 \Omega} + \frac{1}{30 \Omega}$
$\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{6+3+1}{30 \Omega} = \frac{10}{30 \Omega} = \frac{1}{3 \Omega}$
Therefore, $R_p = 3 \Omega$.

*Verification*: Using Ohm's Law for the total circuit, $R_p = V/I = 12 \mathrm{~V} / 4 \mathrm{~A} = 3 \Omega$. This matches the calculated value.

## Example 11.9

If in Figure 11.12, $R_1=10 \Omega, R_2=40 \Omega, R_3=30 \Omega, R_4=20 \Omega, R_5=60 \Omega$, and a 12 V battery is connected to the arrangement. Calculate (a) the total resistance in the circuit, and (b) the total current flowing in the circuit.



---
**Image:**
public/images/10 CBSE/Science/Electricity/6. Resistance of a System of Resistors/img_img-12.png
---



*Figure 11.12: An electric circuit showing the combination of series and parallel resistors.*

### Solution

The circuit consists of two parallel combinations connected in series.
First, calculate the equivalent resistance for the first parallel combination ($R_1$ and $R_2$). Let this be $R'$.
$R_1 = 10 \Omega$, $R_2 = 40 \Omega$
$\frac{1}{R'} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{1}{10 \Omega} + \frac{1}{40 \Omega}$
$\frac{1}{R'} = \frac{4}{40 \Omega} + \frac{1}{40 \Omega} = \frac{5}{40 \Omega} = \frac{1}{8 \Omega}$
So, $R' = 8 \Omega$.

Next, calculate the equivalent resistance for the second parallel combination ($R_3, R_4$, and $R_5$). Let this be $R''$.
$R_3 = 30 \Omega$, $R_4 = 20 \Omega$, $R_5 = 60 \Omega$
$\frac{1}{R''} = \frac{1}{R_3} + \frac{1}{R_4} + \frac{1}{R_5} = \frac{1}{30 \Omega} + \frac{1}{20 \Omega} + \frac{1}{60 \Omega}$
$\frac{1}{R''} = \frac{2}{60 \Omega} + \frac{3}{60 \Omega} + \frac{1}{60 \Omega} = \frac{2+3+1}{60 \Omega} = \frac{6}{60 \Omega} = \frac{1}{10 \Omega}$
So, $R'' = 10 \Omega$.

(a) **Total resistance in the circuit ($R_{total}$)**:
The two equivalent resistances ($R'$ and $R''$) are connected in series.
$R_{total} = R' + R''$
$R_{total} = 8 \Omega + 10 \Omega = 18 \Omega$

(b) **Total current flowing in the circuit ($I$)**:
Given battery voltage $V = 12 \mathrm{~V}$.
Using Ohm's Law, $I = V / R_{total}$:
$I = 12 \mathrm{~V} / 18 \Omega$
$I = 2/3 \mathrm{~A} \approx 0.67 \mathrm{~A}$

### Advantages and Disadvantages of Series and Parallel Circuits

We have seen that in a series circuit, the current is constant throughout the electric circuit. This makes it impractical to connect an electric bulb and an electric heater in series, because they need currents of widely different values to operate properly (as seen in Example 11.3, a bulb needs 0.18 A while a heater needs 2.2 A from the same source). If connected in series, they would both receive the same current, which would be too high for the bulb and too low for the heater to operate efficiently.

Another major disadvantage of a series circuit is that if one component fails (e.g., a bulb fuses or breaks), the circuit is broken, and none of the other components will work. This is why "fairy lights" used for decorations often cause frustration when one "dead" bulb stops the entire string from working.

On the other hand, a **parallel circuit** offers several advantages:
*   **Current Division**: It divides the current through the electrical gadgets, allowing each appliance to draw the specific current it needs for proper operation, as the voltage across each parallel branch is the same.
*   **Independent Operation**: If one component fails or is switched off, the other components in parallel continue to function normally, as their individual circuits remain complete.
*   **Lower Total Resistance**: The total resistance in a parallel circuit is always decreased (as per the formula $1/R_p = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2 + ...$). This is helpful particularly when each gadget has different resistance and requires different current to operate properly, and a low overall resistance allows the source to deliver more current to the combined load.

For these reasons, **domestic electrical circuits are almost exclusively wired in parallel**.
