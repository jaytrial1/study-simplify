A battery or a cell serves as a source of electrical energy. The chemical reactions within the cell generate a potential difference across its terminals, which sets electrons in motion, causing current to flow through a resistor or a system of resistors connected to the battery. As discussed in Section 11.2, to maintain this current, the source must continuously expend its energy.

Where does this energy go? A part of the source energy used in maintaining the current may be converted into useful work (e.g., rotating the blades of an electric fan). However, the rest of the source energy is often expended in the form of heat, which raises the temperature of the gadget. We observe this in everyday life; for example, an electric fan becomes warm if used continuously for a long time.

If an electric circuit is purely resistive (i.e., composed only of resistors connected to a battery), the source energy is continually dissipated entirely in the form of heat. This phenomenon is known as the **heating effect of electric current**. This effect is purposefully utilized in many common devices such as electric heaters, electric irons, electric ovens, and electric kettles.

Consider a current $I$ flowing through a resistor of resistance $R$. Let the potential difference across it be $V$ (Figure 11.13). Let $t$ be the time during which a charge $Q$ flows across. The work done in moving the charge $Q$ through a potential difference $V$ is $VQ$. Therefore, the source must supply energy equal to $VQ$ in time $t$.

The power input to the circuit by the source is:
$P = \frac{\text{Work done}}{\text{Time}} = \frac{VQ}{t}$
Since $I = Q/t$, we can write:
$$
P=V I
$$

Or, the energy supplied to the circuit by the source in time $t$ is $P \times t$, which equals $VIt$. What happens to this energy expended by the source? This energy gets dissipated in the resistor as heat. Thus, for a steady current $I$, the amount of heat $H$ produced in time $t$ is:
$$
H=V I t
$$

Applying Ohm's law ($V = IR$), we can substitute $V$ in the above equation:
$H = (IR) I t$
$$
H=I^{2} R t
$$

This equation is known as **Joule's Law of Heating**. The law implies that the heat produced in a resistor is:
1.  **Directly proportional to the square of the current ($I^2$)** for a given resistance.
2.  **Directly proportional to the resistance ($R$)** for a given current.
3.  **Directly proportional to the time ($t$)** for which the current flows through the resistor.

In practical situations, when an electric appliance is connected to a known voltage source, the formula $H = I^2Rt$ is often used after calculating the current through it using the relation $I = V/R$. Alternatively, by substituting $I=V/R$ into $H=VIt$, we get $H = V (V/R) t = V^2t/R$.

## Example 11.10

An electric iron consumes energy at a rate of 840 W when heating is at the maximum rate and 360 W when the heating is at the minimum. The voltage is 220 V. What are the current and the resistance in each case?

### Solution

From the power formula $P = VI$, we can find the current $I = P/V$.
Then, using Ohm's Law $R = V/I$, we can find the resistance.

(a) **When heating is at the maximum rate**:
Power $P = 840 \mathrm{~W}$
Voltage $V = 220 \mathrm{~V}$
Current $I = P/V = 840 \mathrm{~W} / 220 \mathrm{~V} \approx 3.82 \mathrm{~A}$
Resistance $R = V/I = 220 \mathrm{~V} / 3.82 \mathrm{~A} \approx 57.60 \Omega$

(b) **When heating is at the minimum rate**:
Power $P = 360 \mathrm{~W}$
Voltage $V = 220 \mathrm{~V}$
Current $I = P/V = 360 \mathrm{~W} / 220 \mathrm{~V} \approx 1.64 \mathrm{~A}$
Resistance $R = V/I = 220 \mathrm{~V} / 1.64 \mathrm{~A} \approx 134.15 \Omega$

Notice that the resistance of the electric iron changes to regulate the heating rate. A lower resistance allows more current and thus more heat, while a higher resistance allows less current and less heat.

## Example 11.11

100 J of heat is produced each second in a $4 \Omega$ resistance. Find the potential difference across the resistor.

### Solution

Given:
Heat produced $H = 100 \mathrm{~J}$
Resistance $R = 4 \Omega$
Time $t = 1 \mathrm{~s}$
We need to find the potential difference $V$.

First, find the current $I$ using Joule's Law of Heating, $H = I^2Rt$:
$I^2 = H / (R t)$
$I^2 = 100 \mathrm{~J} / (4 \Omega \times 1 \mathrm{~s})$
$I^2 = 100 / 4 \mathrm{~A}^2 = 25 \mathrm{~A}^2$
$I = \sqrt{25 \mathrm{~A}^2} = 5 \mathrm{~A}$

Now, find the potential difference $V$ using Ohm's Law, $V = IR$:
$V = 5 \mathrm{~A} \times 4 \Omega$
$V = 20 \mathrm{~V}$

The potential difference across the resistor is 20 V.

## Questions

1.  Why does the cord of an electric heater not glow while the heating element does?
2.  Compute the heat generated while transferring 96000 coulomb of charge in one hour through a potential difference of 50 V.
3.  An electric iron of resistance $20 \Omega$ takes a current of 5 A. Calculate the heat developed in 30 s.

> **Answer:**
> 1.  The cord of an electric heater is made of materials like copper or aluminum, which have very low electrical resistivity (they are good conductors). The heating element, on the other hand, is made of an alloy like nichrome, which has a very high electrical resistivity.
>     According to Joule's law of heating ($H = I^2Rt$), for the same current flowing through both the cord and the heating element, the heat produced is directly proportional to the resistance ($R$). Since the heating element has a much higher resistance than the cord, it generates significantly more heat, causing its temperature to rise to the point of glowing (incandescence), while the cord remains relatively cool.
> 2.  **Compute the heat generated:**
>     Given: Charge $Q = 96000 \mathrm{~C}$
>     Time $t = 1 \text{ hour} = 60 \text{ minutes} \times 60 \text{ seconds/minute} = 3600 \mathrm{~s}$
>     Potential difference $V = 50 \mathrm{~V}$
>
>     We know that $H = VIt$. First, we need to find the current $I$.
>     $I = Q/t = 96000 \mathrm{~C} / 3600 \mathrm{~s} = 26.67 \mathrm{~A}$ (approximately)
>
>     Now, calculate the heat generated:
>     $H = VIt = 50 \mathrm{~V} \times 26.67 \mathrm{~A} \times 3600 \mathrm{~s}$
>     $H = 4,800,600 \mathrm{~J} \approx 4.8 \times 10^6 \mathrm{~J}$
>     Alternatively, using $H = VQ$:
>     $H = 50 \mathrm{~V} \times 96000 \mathrm{~C} = 4,800,000 \mathrm{~J} = 4.8 \times 10^6 \mathrm{~J}$
>     The heat generated is $4.8 \times 10^6 \mathrm{~J}$ or 4.8 MJ.
>
> 3.  **Calculate the heat developed:**
>     Given: Resistance $R = 20 \Omega$
>     Current $I = 5 \mathrm{~A}$
>     Time $t = 30 \mathrm{~s}$
>
>     Using Joule's Law of Heating, $H = I^2Rt$:
>     $H = (5 \mathrm{~A})^2 \times 20 \Omega \times 30 \mathrm{~s}$
>     $H = 25 \mathrm{~A}^2 \times 20 \Omega \times 30 \mathrm{~s}$
>     $H = 500 \times 30 \mathrm{~J}$
>     $H = 15000 \mathrm{~J}$
>     The heat developed in 30 s is $15000 \mathrm{~J}$ or 15 kJ.

## Practical Applications of Heating Effect of Electric Current

The generation of heat in a conductor is an unavoidable consequence of electric current. In many cases, this heating is undesirable as it converts useful electrical energy into wasted heat, which can increase the temperature of components and alter their properties. However, the heating effect of electric current also has many useful applications:

*   **Electric Heating Devices**: Devices like electric laundry irons, electric toasters, electric ovens, electric kettles, and electric heaters are all based on Joule's heating. They use high-resistance heating elements to convert electrical energy efficiently into heat.
*   **Electric Bulbs (Incandescent)**: Electric heating is also used to produce light in incandescent bulbs. Here, the filament must retain as much of the generated heat as possible so that it gets extremely hot and emits light. The filament must not melt at such high temperatures. A strong metal with a high melting point, such as **tungsten** (melting point $3380^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$), is used for making bulb filaments. The filament should be thermally isolated as much as possible, using insulating supports. To prolong the life of the filament and prevent its oxidation, bulbs are usually filled with chemically inactive gases like nitrogen and argon. While most of the power consumed by the filament appears as heat, a small part of it is radiated as visible light.
*   **Electric Fuses**: A common safety application of Joule's heating is the **fuse** used in electric circuits. A fuse protects circuits and appliances by stopping the flow of any excessively high electric current. The fuse is always placed in *series* with the device it protects. It consists of a piece of wire made of a metal or an alloy (e.g., aluminum, copper, iron, lead) with an appropriate, low melting point. If a current larger than the specified safe value flows through the circuit (due to a short circuit or overload), the temperature of the fuse wire increases rapidly. This causes the fuse wire to melt and break the circuit, thus preventing damage to the appliances and wiring. Fuse wires are usually encased in a cartridge of porcelain or similar insulating material with metal ends. Fuses used for domestic purposes are rated in amperes (e.g., 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 5 A, 10 A). For example, an electric iron that consumes 1 kW (1000 W) electric power when operated at 220 V would draw a current of $I = P/V = 1000 \mathrm{~W} / 220 \mathrm{~V} \approx 4.54 \mathrm{~A}$. In this case, a 5 A fuse would be appropriate to protect the circuit.
