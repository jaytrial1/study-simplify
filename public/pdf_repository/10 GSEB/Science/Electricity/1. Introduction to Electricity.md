Electricity is a fundamental aspect of modern society, providing a controllable and convenient form of energy for various uses. This chapter will explore what electricity constitutes, how it flows in circuits, the factors that regulate current, and the heating effects of electric current and their applications.

# Electric Current and Circuit

We are familiar with phenomena like air current and water current, where flowing water constitutes water current in rivers. Similarly, when electric charge flows through a conductor, such as a metallic wire, we refer to it as an **electric current**. In a torch, cells (or a battery) provide the flow of charges, causing the torch bulb to glow when the switch is on. A switch essentially creates a conducting link between the cell and the bulb.

An **electric circuit** is defined as a continuous and closed path for an electric current. If this circuit is broken anywhere (e.g., by turning off a switch), the current stops flowing, and the connected device (like a bulb) will not work.

Electric current is quantitatively expressed as the amount of charge flowing through a particular area in unit time. In simpler terms, it is the *rate of flow of electric charges*. In metallic wire circuits, electrons are the charge carriers. Historically, when electricity was first observed, electrons were unknown, so electric current was conventionally considered the flow of positive charges. Therefore, the direction of electric current is taken as opposite to the direction of the flow of electrons, which carry negative charges.

If a net charge $Q$ flows across any cross-section of a conductor in time $t$, then the current $I$ through the cross-section is given by:

$$
I=\frac{Q}{t}
$$

The SI unit of electric charge is **coulomb (C)**. One coulomb is equivalent to the charge contained in approximately $6 \times 10^{18}$ electrons. (An electron possesses a negative charge of $1.6 \times 10^{-19} \mathrm{C}$.)

The SI unit of electric current is **ampere (A)**, named after the French scientist Andre-Marie Ampere. One ampere is defined as the flow of one coulomb of charge per second: $1 \mathrm{~A}=1 \mathrm{C} / 1 \mathrm{~s}$. Smaller quantities of current are expressed in milliampere ($1 \mathrm{~mA}=10^{-3} \mathrm{~A}$) or microampere ($1 \mu \mathrm{A}=10^{-6} \mathrm{~A}$).

An instrument called an **ammeter** is used to measure electric current in a circuit. It is *always connected in series* within the circuit so that the entire current to be measured passes through it.

## Example 11.1

A current of 0.5 A is drawn by a filament of an electric bulb for 10 minutes. Find the amount of electric charge that flows through the circuit.

### Solution

Given:
Current $I = 0.5 \mathrm{~A}$
Time $t = 10 \mathrm{~min} = 10 \times 60 \mathrm{~s} = 600 \mathrm{~s}$

From the formula $I = \frac{Q}{t}$, we can find the charge $Q$:
$Q = I \times t$
$Q = 0.5 \mathrm{~A} \times 600 \mathrm{~s}$
$Q = 300 \mathrm{~C}$

The amount of electric charge that flows through the circuit is 300 C.

## Questions

1.  What does an electric circuit mean?
2.  Define the unit of current.
3.  Calculate the number of electrons constituting one coulomb of charge.

> **Answer:**
> 1.  An **electric circuit** is a continuous and closed path along which an electric current can flow. It typically consists of a power source, conducting wires, and electrical components.
> 2.  The unit of current is the **ampere (A)**. One ampere is defined as the flow of one coulomb of charge per second ($1 \mathrm{~A} = 1 \mathrm{~C/s}$).
> 3.  We know that the charge on one electron is $1.6 \times 10^{-19} \mathrm{C}$.
>     To find the number of electrons constituting one coulomb of charge, we can use the formula:
>     Number of electrons ($n$) = Total charge ($Q$) / Charge of one electron ($e$)
>     $n = \frac{1 \mathrm{~C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19} \mathrm{~C/electron}}$
>     $n = \frac{1}{1.6} \times 10^{19} \mathrm{~electrons}$
>     $n = 0.625 \times 10^{19} \mathrm{~electrons}$
>     $n = 6.25 \times 10^{18} \mathrm{~electrons}$
>     Therefore, approximately $6.25 \times 10^{18}$ electrons constitute one coulomb of charge.
