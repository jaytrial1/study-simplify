In 1827, German physicist Georg Simon Ohm discovered the relationship between the current ($I$) flowing in a metallic wire and the potential difference ($V$) across its terminals. He stated that the potential difference ($V$) across the ends of a given metallic wire in an electric circuit is *directly proportional to the current flowing through it*, provided its temperature remains the same. This fundamental relationship is known as **Ohm's Law**.

In other words:
$V \propto I$
or $V/I = \text{constant}$
or $V = IR$

In the equation $V = IR$, $R$ is a constant for a given metallic wire at a given temperature and is called its **resistance**. Resistance is the property of a conductor that *resists the flow of charges* through it. Its SI unit is **ohm**, represented by the Greek letter $\Omega$.

According to Ohm's Law:
$$
R=V / I
$$

If the potential difference across the two ends of a conductor is 1 V and the current through it is 1 A, then the resistance $R$ of the conductor is $1 \Omega$. That is, $1 \text{ ohm} = \frac{1 \text{ volt}}{1 \text{ ampere}}$.

Also, from Ohm's Law, we can express current as:
$$
I=V / R
$$

It is clear from this equation that the current through a resistor is *inversely proportional to its resistance*. If the resistance is doubled, the current is halved. In many practical applications, it is necessary to increase or decrease the current in an electric circuit. A component used to regulate current without changing the voltage source is called a **variable resistance**. In electric circuits, a device called a **rheostat** is often used to change the resistance in the circuit.

Components with lower resistance will allow more current to flow (higher ammeter reading), while components with higher resistance will allow less current to flow (lower ammeter reading), given the same voltage source.

In this Activity, we observe that the current is different for different components. Why do they differ? Certain components offer an easy path for the flow of electric current, while others resist the flow. We know that the motion of electrons in an electric circuit constitutes an electric current. The electrons, however, are not completely free to move within a conductor. They are restrained by the attraction of the atoms among which they move. Thus, the motion of electrons through a conductor is retarded by its resistance.

A component of a given size that offers a low resistance is called a **good conductor**. A conductor having some appreciable resistance is called a **resistor**. A component of identical size that offers a higher resistance is a **poor conductor**. An **insulator** of the same size offers even higher resistance.

## Example 11.3

(a) How much current will an electric bulb draw from a 220 V source, if the resistance of the bulb filament is $1200 \Omega$?
(b) How much current will an electric heater coil draw from a 220 V source, if the resistance of the heater coil is $100 \Omega$?

### Solution

(a) For the electric bulb:
Given: Voltage $V = 220 \mathrm{~V}$; Resistance $R = 1200 \Omega$.
Using Ohm's Law, $I = V/R$:
$I = 220 \mathrm{~V} / 1200 \Omega$
$I = 0.1833 \mathrm{~A} \approx 0.18 \mathrm{~A}$

(b) For the electric heater coil:
Given: Voltage $V = 220 \mathrm{~V}$; Resistance $R = 100 \Omega$.
Using Ohm's Law, $I = V/R$:
$I = 220 \mathrm{~V} / 100 \Omega$
$I = 2.2 \mathrm{~A}$

Note the significant difference in current drawn by an electric bulb and an electric heater from the same 220 V source! This is due to their different resistances.

## Example 11.4

The potential difference between the terminals of an electric heater is 60 V when it draws a current of 4 A from the source. What current will the heater draw if the potential difference is increased to 120 V?

### Solution

First, calculate the resistance of the heater using the initial conditions.
Given: Initial potential difference $V_1 = 60 \mathrm{~V}$; Initial current $I_1 = 4 \mathrm{~A}$.
According to Ohm's Law, $R = V/I$:
$R = 60 \mathrm{~V} / 4 \mathrm{~A}$
$R = 15 \Omega$

Since the heater's resistance is constant (assuming its temperature doesn't change significantly to alter resistance, or this is an ideal case), we can use this resistance to find the new current.
Given: New potential difference $V_2 = 120 \mathrm{~V}$.
Using Ohm's Law, $I_2 = V_2/R$:
$I_2 = 120 \mathrm{~V} / 15 \Omega$
$I_2 = 8 \mathrm{~A}$

The current through the heater becomes 8 A when the potential difference is doubled.
