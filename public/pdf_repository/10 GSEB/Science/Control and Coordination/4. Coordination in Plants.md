This section explores how plants, despite lacking a nervous system and muscles, coordinate their activities and respond to stimuli. It differentiates between growth-independent and growth-dependent movements, explaining the role of plant hormones (phytohormones) in growth and development.

# Coordination in Plants

Unlike animals, plants do not possess a nervous system or muscles. Yet, they effectively respond to stimuli. Plants exhibit two primary types of movement:
1.  **Movement independent of growth:** Rapid movements, such as the folding of leaves in a *Mimosa pudica* (touch-me-not) plant when touched.
2.  **Movement due to growth:** Slower, directional movements caused by growth, like a seedling's root growing downwards and its stem growing upwards.

### Immediate Response to Stimulus

For movements independent of growth, like the sensitive plant's response to touch:
*   Movement occurs at a point different from the point of touch, indicating that information must be communicated throughout the plant.
*   Plants use **electrical-chemical means** to convey this information from cell to cell. However, they lack specialized nervous tissue for rapid conduction.
*   Plant cells change shape by altering the amount of **water** in them, leading to swelling or shrinking. This change in *turgor pressure* in specialized cells (e.g., in the pulvini at the base of leaves) causes the leaves to fold.

### Movement Due to Growth

Many plant movements are slow and directional, caused by **growth**. These are called **tropic movements** or **tropisms**.
*   **Tendrils:** In climbing plants like pea plants, tendrils are sensitive to touch. When they contact a support, the part of the tendril *away* from the object grows faster than the part in contact. This differential growth causes the tendril to coil around the support, allowing the plant to cling and climb.
*   **Directional Growth:** Plants respond to environmental triggers like light or gravity by growing in a particular direction.
    *   **Phototropism:** Growth response to light. Shoots typically exhibit *positive phototropism* (bending towards light), while roots often show *negative phototropism* (growing away from light). This helps shoots maximize light absorption for photosynthesis.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Have the old parts of the shoot and root changed direction?** No, the old parts of the shoot and root will not change direction. Their growth is already fixed in the direction they originally grew.
> *   **Are there differences in the direction of the new growth?** Yes, the new growth will show a difference in direction. The new shoot growth will bend and grow towards the new direction of light (positive phototropism), and the new root growth will continue to grow downwards due to gravity (positive geotropism), even if the flask was turned.
> *   **What can we conclude from this activity?** We can conclude that plants respond to environmental stimuli like light and gravity through directional growth movements called tropisms. Shoots exhibit positive phototropism (grow towards light) and negative geotropism (grow away from gravity), while roots exhibit negative phototropism (grow away from light) and positive geotropism (grow towards gravity). These responses are mediated by hormones and are crucial for the plant's survival and optimal positioning in its environment.

*   **Geotropism (or Gravitropism):** Growth response to gravity. Roots typically show *positive geotropism* (growing downwards towards the Earth's pull), while shoots show *negative geotropism* (growing upwards, away from gravity). This ensures roots anchor the plant and access water, while shoots reach sunlight.

If 'hydro' means water and 'chemo' refers to chemicals, what would 'hydrotropism' and 'chemotropism' mean? Can we think of examples of these kinds of directional growth movements?
> **Answer:**
> *   **Hydrotropism:** The directional growth response of a plant part towards or away from water. An example is the growth of plant roots *towards* a source of water (positive hydrotropism).
> *   **Chemotropism:** The directional growth response of a plant part towards or away from a chemical stimulus. An example is the growth of a **pollen tube** *towards* the ovules in a flower, guided by specific chemical signals released by the ovule during fertilization.

### Chemical Communication in Plants

While electrical impulses are fast, they are limited to cells connected by nervous tissue and require time to reset. Multicellular organisms, including plants, widely use **chemical communication** via **hormones**.
*   **Mechanism:** Stimulated cells release chemical compounds that diffuse to other cells. Other cells with specific receptor molecules can detect these compounds, recognize the information, and respond.
*   **Advantages:** Slower than electrical impulses but can potentially reach *all cells* of the body, regardless of nervous connections, and can act steadily and persistently.

Different **plant hormones** (also called **phytohormones**) coordinate growth, development, and environmental responses. They are synthesized in one part of the plant and diffuse to the area of action.

Examples of plant hormones:
*   **Auxin:** Synthesized at the shoot tip. It helps cells grow longer. When light comes from one side, auxin diffuses to the shady side, stimulating cells on that side to grow longer, causing the plant to bend towards light.
*   **Gibberellins:** Promote stem growth, similar to auxins.
*   **Cytokinins:** Promote cell division. They are found in high concentrations in areas of rapid cell division, like fruits and seeds.
*   **Abscisic acid:** An example of a growth-inhibiting hormone. Its effects include wilting of leaves and promoting dormancy.

### Q U E S T I O N S
1. What are plant hormones?
> **Answer:** Plant hormones, also known as phytohormones, are chemical substances produced naturally by plants in very small quantities. They act as signaling molecules that regulate and coordinate various physiological processes, including growth, development, cell division, differentiation, flowering, fruit ripening, and responses to environmental stimuli.

2. How is the movement of leaves of the sensitive plant different from the movement of a shoot towards light?
> **Answer:**
> *   **Sensitive Plant (e.g., *Mimosa pudica*):** This movement is *independent of growth* and is a rapid, non-directional response to touch (thigmonasty). It occurs due to rapid changes in turgor pressure within specialized cells (pulvini) at the base of the leaves, causing water to move out and the cells to lose rigidity, leading to the folding of leaves.
> *   **Shoot towards light:** This movement is *dependent on growth* and is a slow, directional response to light (phototropism). It occurs due to the differential growth of cells on the shaded side of the shoot, stimulated by the hormone auxin, causing the shoot to bend towards the light source.

3. Give an example of a plant hormone that promotes growth.
> **Answer:** **Auxin** (or Gibberellins, or Cytokinins) is a plant hormone that promotes growth.

4. How do auxins promote the growth of a tendril around a support?
> **Answer:** When a tendril comes in contact with a support, the side of the tendril that is *in contact* with the support experiences less auxin concentration, while the side *away* from the support accumulates more auxin. This higher concentration of auxin on the side away from the support stimulates faster cell elongation and growth on that side. As a result, the tendril grows more rapidly on the outer side, causing it to coil or circle around the support and cling to it.

5. Design an experiment to demonstrate hydrotropism.
> **Answer:**
> **Aim:** To demonstrate hydrotropism (root growth towards water).
> **Materials:** A trough or container, wet soil, dry soil, a porous pot (e.g., terracotta or clay pot), water, germinated seeds (e.g., pea or bean).
> **Procedure:**
> 1.  Fill one side of the trough with dry soil and the other side with wet soil.
> 2.  Place a porous pot filled with water into the wet soil side, ensuring the pot is partially submerged in the wet soil.
> 3.  Plant the germinated seeds in the dry soil side, a few centimeters away from the porous pot.
> 4.  Ensure that the only source of water available to the seeds is from the porous pot diffusing into the surrounding wet soil. Do not water the dry soil directly.
> 5.  Observe the direction of root growth over several days.
> **Observation:** The roots of the germinated seeds will be observed to grow preferentially towards the porous pot containing water, even if it means growing against gravity or in a non-straight path.
> **Conclusion:** This experiment demonstrates hydrotropism, as the roots exhibit positive hydrotropism by growing towards the source of water.
