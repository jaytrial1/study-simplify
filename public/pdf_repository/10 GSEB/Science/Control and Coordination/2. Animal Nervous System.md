This section delves into the nervous system in animals, explaining how it provides control and coordination, the structure and function of neurons, and the process of nerve impulse transmission.

# Animal Nervous System

In animals, *control and coordination* are primarily facilitated by the **nervous** and **muscular tissues**. When we encounter a dangerous situation, like touching a hot object, our body needs to quickly detect and respond to it.

Information from the environment is detected by specialized tips of **nerve cells** called **receptors**. These receptors are typically found in our sense organs:
*   **Gustatory receptors** detect taste (e.g., on the tongue).
*   **Olfactory receptors** detect smell (e.g., in the nose).
*   Other receptors are located in the inner ear, eyes, and skin.

Once information is acquired at the **dendritic tip** of a nerve cell, a chemical reaction is initiated, generating an **electrical impulse**. This impulse then travels:
1.  From the **dendrite** to the **cell body**.
2.  Along the **axon** to its end.

At the axon's end, the electrical impulse triggers the release of chemicals. These chemicals cross a microscopic gap called a **synapse** and initiate a similar electrical impulse in the dendrite of the *next neuron*. This mechanism allows nervous impulses to travel throughout the body. A similar synapse facilitates the transmission of impulses from neurons to other cells, such as muscle cells or glands.

Nervous tissue is composed of an organized network of **nerve cells** or **neurons**, specialized for conducting information via electrical impulses from one part of the body to another.

Look at Fig. 6.1 (a) and identify the parts of a neuron (i) where information is acquired, (ii) through which information travels as an electrical impulse, and (iii) where this impulse must be converted into a chemical signal for onward transmission.
> **Answer:**
> (i) Information is acquired at the **dendritic tip** (or dendrites).
> (ii) Information travels as an electrical impulse along the **axon**.
> (iii) The impulse must be converted into a chemical signal for onward transmission at the **axon terminal** (or nerve ending) where neurotransmitters are released into the synapse.

### Activity 6.1
*   Put some sugar in your mouth. How does it taste?
*   Block your nose by pressing it between your thumb and index finger. Now eat sugar again. Is there any difference in its taste?
*   While eating lunch, block your nose in the same way and notice if you can fully appreciate the taste of the food you are eating.

Is there a difference in how sugar and food taste if your nose is blocked? If so, why might this be happening? Read and talk about possible explanations for these kinds of differences. Do you come across a similar situation when you have a cold?
> **Answer:** Yes, there is a significant difference in how sugar and food taste if your nose is blocked. When your nose is blocked, your ability to fully appreciate the taste of food is diminished. This happens because our perception of "taste" is not solely due to the taste buds on our tongue (which detect sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami). A large part of what we perceive as taste is actually the **smell** of the food, detected by our olfactory receptors. When your nose is blocked, these olfactory receptors cannot detect the volatile chemicals from the food, thus reducing the overall sensory experience. This is why food often seems bland or tasteless when you have a cold, as your nasal passages are congested, impairing your sense of smell.
