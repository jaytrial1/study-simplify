This section introduces the classification of elements into metals and non-metals based on their properties and then delves into a detailed study of the physical characteristics of metals, such as luster, hardness, malleability, ductility, and conductivity.

# Introduction to Metals and Non-metals

In Class IX, you learned about various elements and how they can be classified as metals or non-metals based on their properties.

-   Think of some uses of metals and non-metals in your daily life.
-   What properties did you think of while categorizing elements as metals or non-metals?
-   How are these properties related to the uses of these elements?

> **Answer:**
> *   **Uses:** Metals are used for making utensils, electrical wires, machinery, jewelry, construction materials (e.g., iron for buildings, aluminum for aircraft). Non-metals are essential for life (oxygen for breathing), fuels (carbon in coal/petroleum), fertilizers (nitrogen, phosphorus), and various chemical processes (e.g., sulfur in sulfuric acid).
> *   **Properties for Categorization:** When classifying elements, properties like *luster* (shininess), *hardness*, *malleability* (ability to be hammered into sheets), *ductility* (ability to be drawn into wires), *electrical conductivity*, *thermal conductivity*, and *sonority* (ability to produce sound when struck) are considered.
> *   **Relation to Uses:**
>     *   **Metals:** Their *malleability* and *ductility* allow them to be shaped into various forms like sheets (utensils, roofing) and wires (electrical wiring). Their *high electrical and thermal conductivity* makes them suitable for electrical circuits and cooking vessels. Their *strength* and *hardness* are crucial for machinery and construction.
>     *   **Non-metals:** Oxygen's gaseous state and reactivity are vital for respiration. Carbon's ability to form various compounds makes it the backbone of organic chemistry. Sulfur's properties are useful in industries. Their generally *poor conductivity* makes them good insulators.

Let us look at some of these properties in detail.

## Physical Properties of Metals

The easiest way to start grouping substances is by comparing their physical properties. For the following activities (3.1 to 3.6), collect samples of metals such as iron, copper, aluminium, magnesium, sodium, lead, zinc, and any other easily available metal.

### Activity 3.1: Appearance and Luster

-   Take samples of iron, copper, aluminium, and magnesium. Note the appearance of each sample.
-   Clean the surface of each sample by rubbing them with sandpaper and note their appearance again.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Initial Appearance:** Metals may appear dull due to a layer of oxide or other impurities on their surface.
> *   **Appearance after Cleaning:** After rubbing with sandpaper, the true, shiny surface of the metals will be revealed.
> *   **Observation:** Metals, in their pure state, have a shining surface. This property is called **metallic luster**.

### Activity 3.2: Hardness

-   Take small pieces of iron, copper, aluminium, and magnesium. Try to cut these metals with a sharp knife and note your observations.
-   Hold a piece of sodium metal with a pair of tongs. **CAUTION:** Always handle sodium metal with care. Dry it by pressing between the folds of a filter paper.
-   Put it on a watch-glass and try to cut it with a knife.
-   What do you observe?

> **Answer:**
> *   **Observation (Iron, Copper, Aluminium, Magnesium):** These metals are generally hard and cannot be easily cut with a knife. Some might show slight indentation, but cutting through them is difficult.
> *   **Observation (Sodium):** Sodium metal is very soft and can be easily cut with a knife, almost like butter.
> *   **Conclusion:** You will find that metals are generally **hard**. The hardness varies from metal to metal.

### Activity 3.3: Malleability

-   Take pieces of iron, zinc, lead, and copper.
-   Place any one metal on a block of iron and strike it four or five times with a hammer. What do you observe?
-   Repeat with other metals.
-   Record the change in the shape of these metals.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Observation:** You will observe that iron, zinc, lead, and copper can be beaten into thin sheets without breaking. Lead and copper are particularly easy to flatten.
> *   **Conclusion:** You will find that some metals can be beaten into thin sheets. This property is called **malleability**. Gold and silver are the most malleable metals.

### Activity 3.4: Ductility

-   List the metals whose wires you have seen in daily life.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Metals for Wires:** Copper (electrical wires), Aluminium (electrical wires, overhead power lines), Iron (fencing wire, steel cables).

The ability of metals to be drawn into thin wires is called **ductility**. Gold is the most ductile metal. You will be surprised to know that a wire of about 2 km length can be drawn from one gram of gold.

It is because of their malleability and ductility that metals can be given different shapes according to our needs.

## Physical Properties of Non-metals

In the previous Class, you have learned that there are very few non-metals as compared to metals. Some examples of non-metals are carbon, sulphur, iodine, oxygen, hydrogen, etc. The non-metals are either solids or gases, except bromine which is a liquid.

Do non-metals also have physical properties similar to that of metals? Let us find out.

### Activity 3.7: Testing Non-metal Properties

-   Collect samples of carbon (coal or graphite), sulphur, and iodine. Carry out Activities 3.1 to 3.4 and 3.6 with these non-metals and record your observations.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Observations (Carbon, Sulphur, Iodine):**
>     *   **Appearance/Luster:** Carbon (coal) is dull, graphite has some luster, sulphur is dull, iodine has a metallic luster.
>     *   **Hardness:** Carbon (coal) is brittle, graphite is soft, sulphur is soft and brittle, iodine is brittle. None are generally hard like most metals.
>     *   **Malleability/Ductility:** Non-metals are generally brittle and will break or powder when hammered or stretched. They are neither malleable nor ductile.
>     *   **Electrical Conductivity:** Carbon (graphite allotrope) conducts electricity, but coal, sulfur, and iodine do not.
>     *   **Sonority:** Non-metals are not sonorous; they produce a dull sound or break when struck.

On the basis of the observations recorded in Table 3.1, discuss the general physical properties of metals and non-metals in the class. You must have concluded that we cannot group elements according to their physical properties alone, as there are many exceptions. For example:

*   All metals except *mercury* exist as solids at room temperature.
*   In Activity 3.5, you observed that metals have high melting points, but *gallium* and *caesium* have very low melting points. These two metals will melt if you keep them on your palm.
*   *Iodine* is a non-metal but it is lustrous.
*   *Carbon* is a non-metal that can exist in different forms. Each form is called an **allotrope**. *Diamond*, an allotrope of carbon, is the hardest natural substance known and has a very high melting and boiling point. *Graphite*, another allotrope of carbon, is a conductor of electricity.
*   *Alkali metals* (lithium, sodium, potassium) are so soft that they can be cut with a knife. They have low densities and low melting points.

Elements can be more clearly classified as metals and non-metals on the basis of their chemical properties.

### Key Concepts & Examples

*   **Metallic Lustre:** The characteristic shine of metals.
*   **Malleability:** The property of metals to be beaten into thin sheets. Gold and silver are highly malleable.
*   **Ductility:** The property of metals to be drawn into thin wires. Gold is the most ductile metal.
*   **Sonorous:** Metals that produce a ringing sound when struck.
*   **Thermal Conductivity:** Ability of a substance to conduct heat. Metals are generally good conductors.
*   **Electrical Conductivity:** Ability of a substance to conduct electricity. Metals are generally good conductors.
*   **Allotrope:** Different structural forms of the same element, such as diamond and graphite for carbon, which have different physical properties.
