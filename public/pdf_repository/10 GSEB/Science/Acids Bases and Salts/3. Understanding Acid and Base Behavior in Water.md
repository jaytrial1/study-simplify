This section explores the common properties shared by all acids and bases, specifically focusing on their ability to produce ions in aqueous solutions and the implications for electrical conductivity.

### What Do All Acids and All Bases Have in Common?

In Section 2.1 we have seen that all acids have similar chemical properties. What leads to this similarity in properties? We saw in Activity 2.3 that all acids generate hydrogen gas on reacting with metals, so hydrogen seems to be common to all acids. Let us perform an Activity to investigate whether all compounds containing hydrogen are acidic.

#### Activity: Electrical Conductivity of Acids and Bases

- Take solutions of glucose, alcohol, hydrochloric acid, sulphuric acid, etc.
- Fix two nails on a cork, and place the cork in a 100 mL beaker.
- Connect the nails to the two terminals of a 6 volt battery through a bulb and a switch, as shown in Fig. 2.3.
- Now pour some dilute HCl in the beaker and switch on the current.
- Repeat with dilute sulphuric acid.

- Repeat the experiment separately with glucose and alcohol solutions. What do you observe now?

> **Answer:**
> *   **Observation with dilute HCl and sulphuric acid:** The bulb will **glow**.
> *   **Observation with glucose and alcohol solutions:** The bulb will **not glow**.
> *   **Does the bulb glow in all cases?** No, the bulb does not glow in all cases. It glows only for acidic solutions (HCl, H₂SO₄) and not for glucose or alcohol solutions.

The bulb will start glowing in the case of acids, as shown in Fig. 2.3. But you will observe that glucose and alcohol solutions do not conduct electricity. Glowing of the bulb indicates that there is a flow of electric current through the solution. The electric current is carried through the acidic solution by ions.

Acids contain $\mathrm{H}^{+}$ion as cation and anion such as $\mathrm{Cl}^{-}$in $\mathrm{HCl}, \mathrm{NO}_{3}^{-}$in $\mathrm{HNO}_{3}, \mathrm{SO}_{4}^{2-}$ in $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}, \mathrm{CH}_{3} \mathrm{COO}^{-}$in $\mathrm{CH}_{3} \mathrm{COOH}$. Since the cation present in acids is $\mathrm{H}^{+}$, this suggests that acids produce hydrogen ions, $\mathrm{H}^{+}(\mathrm{aq})$, in solution, which are responsible for their acidic properties.

Repeat the same Activity using alkalis such as sodium hydroxide, calcium hydroxide, etc. What can you conclude from the results of this Activity?
> **Answer:** If the same activity is repeated with alkalis like sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) and calcium hydroxide ($\text{Ca(OH)}_2$), the bulb will **glow**.
> **Conclusion:** This indicates that alkalis (bases soluble in water) also conduct electricity in aqueous solutions. This is because they dissociate to produce hydroxide ions ($\text{OH}^-$) and metal cations, which act as charge carriers in the solution.

### What Happens to an Acid or a Base in a Water Solution?

Do acids produce ions only in aqueous solution? Let us test this.

(ii) HCl solution?

> **Answer:**
> *   **Observation:** Yes, a **gas** (hydrogen chloride, HCl) will be seen coming out of the delivery tube.
> *   **Litmus paper test:**
>     *   When tested with **dry blue litmus paper**, there will be **no change** in colour.
>     *   When tested with **wet blue litmus paper**, the litmus paper will turn **red**.
> *   **Case where litmus paper changes colour:** The litmus paper changes colour only when it is **wet** (i.e., in the presence of water).
> *   **Inference about acidic character:**
>     *   (i) **Dry HCl gas:** Dry HCl gas is **not acidic**. It does not dissociate into H⁺ ions in the absence of water, so it does not show acidic properties.
>     *   (ii) **HCl solution:** HCl solution is **acidic**. In the presence of water, HCl dissociates to produce H⁺ ions (or hydronium ions, H₃O⁺), which are responsible for its acidic character.

This experiment suggests that hydrogen ions in HCl are produced in the presence of water. The separation of $\mathrm{H}^{+}$ion from HCl molecules cannot occur in the absence of water.

$$
\mathrm{HCl}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O} \rightarrow \mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+}+\mathrm{Cl}^{-}
$$

Hydrogen ions cannot exist alone, but they exist after combining with water molecules. Thus hydrogen ions must always be shown as $\mathrm{H}^{+}(\mathrm{aq})$ or hydronium ion $\left(\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+}\right)$.
$\mathrm{H}^{+}+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O} \rightarrow \mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+}$
We have seen that acids give $\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+}$or $\mathrm{H}^{+}(\mathrm{aq})$ ion in water. Let us see what happens when a base is dissolved in water.

$$
\mathrm{NaOH}(\mathrm{~s}) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}} \mathrm{Na}^{+}(\mathrm{aq})+\mathrm{OH}^{-}(\mathrm{aq})
$$

$$
\mathrm{KOH}(\mathrm{~s}) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}} \mathrm{K}^{+}(\mathrm{aq})+\mathrm{OH}^{-}(\mathrm{aq})
$$

$$
\mathrm{Mg}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}(\mathrm{~s}) \xrightarrow{\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}} \mathrm{Mg}^{2+}(\mathrm{aq})+2 \mathrm{OH}^{-}(\mathrm{aq})
$$

Bases generate hydroxide $\left(\mathrm{OH}^{-}\right)$ions in water. Bases which are soluble in water are called *alkalis*.

All bases do not dissolve in water. An alkali is a base that dissolves in water. They are soapy to touch, bitter and corrosive. Never taste or touch them as they may cause harm. Which of the bases in the Table 2.1 are alkalis?
> **Answer:** From Table 2.1, the bases that are alkalis (i.e., soluble in water) are:
> *   Sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$)
> *   Potassium hydroxide ($\text{KOH}$)
> *   Ammonium hydroxide ($\text{NH}_4\text{OH}$)
> Calcium hydroxide ($\text{Ca(OH)}_2$) is sparingly soluble, and magnesium hydroxide ($\text{Mg(OH)}_2$) is almost insoluble, so they are generally not considered true alkalis, though they are bases. However, for simplicity at this level, some textbooks might include $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$ as an alkali due to its partial solubility.

Now as we have identified that all acids generate $\mathrm{H}^{+}(\mathrm{aq})$ and all bases generate $\mathrm{OH}^{-}(\mathrm{aq})$, we can view the neutralisation reaction as follows-

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \text{Acid} + \text{Base} \rightarrow \text{Salt} + \text{Water} \\
& \text{HX} + \text{MOH} \rightarrow \text{MX} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \\
& \text{H}^{+}(\text{aq}) + \text{OH}^{-}(\text{aq}) \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O(l)}
\end{aligned}
$$

#### Activity: Dilution of Acid/Base

Take 10 mL water in a beaker.
Add a few drops of concentrated $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{SO}_{4}$ to it and swirl the beaker slowly.
Touch the base of the beaker.
Is there a change in temperature?
Is this an exothermic or endothermic process?
Repeat the above Activity with sodium hydroxide pellets and record your observations.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Change in temperature:** Yes, there will be a significant **increase in temperature**. The beaker will feel hot.
> *   **Exothermic or endothermic:** This is a highly **exothermic** process, meaning it releases a large amount of heat.
> *   **Sodium hydroxide pellets:** When sodium hydroxide pellets are dissolved in water, the same observation will be made: the beaker will feel hot, indicating that the dissolution of a base in water is also a highly **exothermic** process.

The process of dissolving an acid or a base in water is a highly exothermic one. Care must be taken while mixing concentrated nitric acid or sulphuric acid with water. The acid must always be added slowly to water with constant stirring. If water is added to a concentrated acid, the heat generated may cause the mixture to splash out and cause burns. The glass container may also break due to excessive local heating. Look out for the warning sign (shown in Fig. 2.5) on the can of concentrated sulphuric acid and on the bottle of sodium hydroxide pellets.

Mixing an acid or base with water results in decrease in the concentration of ions $\left(\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+} / \mathrm{OH}^{-}\right)$per unit volume. Such a process is called *dilution* and the acid or the base is said to be *diluted*.

### Questions

1. Why do $\mathrm{HCl}, \mathrm{HNO}_{3}$, etc., show acidic characters in aqueous solutions while solutions of compounds like alcohol and glucose do not show acidic character?
> **Answer:** $\text{HCl}$ and $\text{HNO}_3$ are acids that dissociate in aqueous solutions to produce hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$) or hydronium ions ($\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$). It is these $\text{H}^+$ ions that are responsible for their acidic properties. Compounds like alcohol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$) and glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) also contain hydrogen, but they do not dissociate to produce $\text{H}^+$ ions when dissolved in water. Instead, they remain as neutral molecules. Therefore, they do not show acidic character.

2. Why does an aqueous solution of an acid conduct electricity?
> **Answer:** An aqueous solution of an acid conducts electricity because acids, when dissolved in water, undergo ionization or dissociation to produce charged particles called **ions**. For example, hydrochloric acid dissociates into $\text{H}^+$ (or $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$) and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions. These free-moving ions act as charge carriers, allowing the electric current to flow through the solution.

3. Why does dry HCl gas not change the colour of the dry litmus paper?
> **Answer:** Dry $\text{HCl}$ gas does not change the colour of dry litmus paper because in the absence of water, $\text{HCl}$ exists as discrete molecules and does not dissociate to produce hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$). The acidic properties (like changing litmus paper colour) are due to the presence of these $\text{H}^+$ ions. Without water, ionization does not occur, and thus, dry $\text{HCl}$ gas does not exhibit acidic behavior.

4. While diluting an acid, why is it recommended that the acid should be added to water and not water to the acid?
> **Answer:** It is recommended to add acid to water slowly with constant stirring while diluting, and not the other way around, because the process of mixing concentrated acid (especially sulphuric acid or nitric acid) with water is **highly exothermic**. If water is added to a concentrated acid, a large amount of heat is generated very rapidly. This intense heat can cause the mixture to splash out violently, leading to severe burns. Additionally, the excessive local heating can cause the glass container to break. Adding acid slowly to water distributes the heat more gradually and safely.

5. How is the concentration of hydronium ions $\left(\mathrm{H}_{3} \mathrm{O}^{+}\right)$affected when a solution of an acid is diluted?
> **Answer:** When a solution of an acid is diluted, the concentration of hydronium ions ($\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$) per unit volume **decreases**. This is because the same number of $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ ions are now spread out in a larger volume of solution.

6. How is the concentration of hydroxide ions $\left(\mathrm{OH}^{-}\right)$affected when excess base is dissolved in a solution of sodium hydroxide?
> **Answer:** When excess base is dissolved in a solution of sodium hydroxide, the concentration of hydroxide ions ($\text{OH}^-$) per unit volume **increases**. This is because additional base molecules dissociate to produce more $\text{OH}^-$ ions in the solution.
