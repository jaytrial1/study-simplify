These indicators tell us whether a substance is acidic or basic by change in colour. There are some substances whose odour changes in acidic or basic media. These are called *olfactory indicators*. Let us try out some of these indicators.

#### Activity: Olfactory Indicators

Take some finely chopped onions in a plastic bag along with some strips of clean cloth. Tie up the bag tightly and leave overnight in the fridge. The cloth strips can now be used to test for acids and bases.
Take two of these cloth strips and check their odour.
Keep them on a clean surface and put a few drops of dilute HCl solution on one strip and a few drops of dilute NaOH solution on the other.

- Rinse both cloth strips with water and again check their odour.
- Note your observations.
- Now take some dilute vanilla essence and clove oil and check their odour.
- Take some dilute HCl solution in one test tube and dilute NaOH solution in another. Add a few drops of dilute vanilla essence to both test tubes and shake well. Check the odour once again and record changes in odour, if any.
- Similarly, test the change in the odour of clove oil with dilute HCl and dilute NaOH solutions and record your observations.

Which of these - vanilla, onion and clove, can be used as olfactory indicators on the basis of your observations?
> **Answer:** Based on the observations, **vanilla, onion, and clove** can all be used as olfactory indicators.
> **Explanation:**
> *   **Onion:** Its characteristic pungent smell is destroyed (or significantly reduced) in a basic medium (like NaOH solution) but remains largely unaffected in an acidic medium (like HCl solution).
> *   **Vanilla essence:** Its characteristic pleasant smell is destroyed (or significantly reduced) in a basic medium (like NaOH solution) but remains largely unaffected in an acidic medium (like HCl solution).
> *   **Clove oil:** Its characteristic smell is destroyed (or significantly reduced) in a basic medium (like NaOH solution) but remains largely unaffected in an acidic medium (like HCl solution).
>
> Substances whose odour changes in acidic or basic media are called olfactory indicators.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Observation on zinc granules:** You will observe the formation of **gas bubbles** on the surface of the zinc granules.
> *   **Bubbles in soap solution:** Bubbles are formed in the soap solution because the gas evolved from the reaction is passed through it, getting trapped in the soap film.
> *   **Burning candle observation:** When a burning candle is brought near a gas-filled bubble, the gas burns with a characteristic **"pop" sound**. This confirms the presence of hydrogen gas.
> *   **Observations with other acids:** The observations will be **the same** for $\mathrm{HCl}$ and $\mathrm{CH}_{3} \mathrm{COOH}$ (production of hydrogen gas and a pop sound). For $\mathrm{HNO}_{3}$, the observations might be different, especially with concentrated nitric acid, as it is a strong oxidizing agent and often produces oxides of nitrogen instead of hydrogen gas. However, for dilute $\mathrm{HNO}_{3}$, hydrogen gas can sometimes be produced. For the purpose of this general reaction, the fundamental outcome (hydrogen gas) is often highlighted.

Note that the metal in the above reactions displaces hydrogen atoms from the acids as hydrogen gas and forms a compound called a salt. Thus, the reaction of a metal with an acid can be summarised as -

Acid + Metal $\rightarrow$ Salt + Hydrogen gas

> **Answer:**
> *   **With dilute sulphuric acid:**
>     $\text{Zn(s) + H}_2\text{SO}_4\text{(aq) \rightarrow ZnSO}_4\text{(aq) + H}_2\text{(g)}$
> *   **With dilute hydrochloric acid:**
>     $\text{Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow ZnCl}_2\text{(aq) + H}_2\text{(g)}$
> *   **With dilute acetic acid:**
>     $\text{Zn(s) + 2CH}_3\text{COOH(aq) \rightarrow (CH}_3\text{COO)}_2\text{Zn(aq) + H}_2\text{(g)}$

#### Activity: Reaction of Carbonates and Hydrogencarbonates with Acids

Take two test tubes, label them as A and B.

- Take about 0.5 g of sodium carbonate $\left(\mathrm{Na}_{2} \mathrm{CO}_{3}\right)$ in test tube A and about 0.5 g of sodium hydrogencarbonate $\left(\mathrm{NaHCO}_{3}\right)$ in test tube B.
- Add about 2 mL of dilute HCl to both the test tubes.
- What do you observe?

Pass the gas produced in each case through lime water (calcium hydroxide solution) as shown in Fig. 2.2 and record your observations.

> **Answer:**
> *   **Observation in test tubes A and B:** You will observe **vigorous effervescence** (rapid formation of bubbles) in both test tubes, indicating the evolution of a gas.
> *   **Observation when gas is passed through lime water:** When the gas (carbon dioxide) produced is passed through lime water (calcium hydroxide solution), the lime water turns **milky or cloudy**. This milky appearance is due to the formation of a white precipitate of calcium carbonate.

The reactions occurring in the above Activity are written as -
Test tube A: $\mathrm{Na}_{2} \mathrm{CO}_{3}(\mathrm{~s})+2 \mathrm{HCl}(\mathrm{aq}) \rightarrow 2 \mathrm{NaCl}(\mathrm{aq})+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l})+\mathrm{CO}_{2}(\mathrm{~g})$
Test tube B: $\mathrm{NaHCO}_{3}(\mathrm{~s})+\mathrm{HCl}(\mathrm{aq}) \rightarrow \mathrm{NaCl}(\mathrm{aq})+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l})+\mathrm{CO}_{2}(\mathrm{~g})$
On passing the carbon dioxide gas evolved through lime water,

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathrm{Ca}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}(\mathrm{aq})+\mathrm{CO}_{2}(\mathrm{~g}) \rightarrow \mathrm{CaCO}_{3}(\mathrm{~s})+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l}) \\
& \text { (Lime water) (White precipitate) }
\end{aligned}
$$

On passing excess carbon dioxide the following reaction takes place:

$$
\begin{aligned}
& \mathrm{CaCO}_{3}(\mathrm{~s})+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l})+\mathrm{CO}_{2}(\mathrm{~g}) \rightarrow \mathrm{Ca}\left(\mathrm{HCO}_{3}\right)_{2}(\mathrm{aq}) \\
& \text { (Soluble in water)
\end{aligned}
$$

Limestone, chalk and marble are different forms of calcium carbonate. All metal carbonates and hydrogencarbonates react with acids to give a corresponding salt, carbon dioxide and water.

Thus, the reaction can be summarised as -
Metal carbonate/Metal hydrogencarbonate + Acid $\rightarrow$ Salt + Carbon dioxide + Water

### How do Acids and Bases React with each other?

#### Activity: Neutralization Reaction

- Take about 2 mL of dilute NaOH solution in a test tube and add two drops of phenolphthalein solution.
- What is the colour of the solution?
- Add dilute HCl solution to the above solution drop by drop.
- Is there any colour change for the reaction mixture?
- Why did the colour of phenolphthalein change after the addition of an acid?
- Now add a few drops of NaOH to the above mixture.
- Does the pink colour of phenolphthalein reappear?
- Why do you think this has happened?

> **Answer:**
> *   **Initial colour:** The solution will be **pink**. Phenolphthalein is pink in basic solutions.
> *   **Colour change after adding HCl:** Yes, the pink colour will **disappear**, and the solution will become colourless.
> *   **Why colour changed:** The colour of phenolphthalein changed because the added dilute HCl (an acid) reacted with and neutralized the dilute NaOH (a base). As the solution became neutral or slightly acidic, phenolphthalein turned colourless.
> *   **Pink colour reappears:** Yes, the pink colour of phenolphthalein will **reappear**.
> *   **Why it happened:** The pink colour reappeared because adding a few drops of NaOH (a base) again made the solution basic. The excess base restored the basic conditions, causing phenolphthalein to turn pink again.

In the above Activity, we have observed that the effect of a base is nullified by an acid and vice-versa. The reaction taking place is written as -
$\mathrm{NaOH}(\mathrm{aq})+\mathrm{HCl}(\mathrm{aq}) \rightarrow \mathrm{NaCl}(\mathrm{aq})+\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}(\mathrm{l})$
The reaction between an acid and a base to give a salt and water is known as a *neutralisation reaction*. In general, a neutralisation reaction can be written as -

Base + Acid $\rightarrow$ Salt + Water

### Reaction of Metallic Oxides with Acids

#### Activity: Reaction of Copper Oxide with Acid

- Take a small amount of copper oxide in a beaker and add dilute hydrochloric acid slowly while stirring.
- Note the colour of the solution. What has happened to the copper oxide?

> **Answer:**
> *   **Colour of the solution:** The solution will become **blue-green**.
> *   **What happened to copper oxide:** The copper oxide will **dissolve**.

You will notice that the colour of the solution becomes blue-green and the copper oxide dissolves. The blue-green colour of the solution is due to the formation of copper(II) chloride in the reaction. The general reaction between a metal oxide and an acid can be written as -

Metal oxide + Acid $\rightarrow$ Salt + Water

Now write and balance the equation for the above reaction.
> **Answer:**
> $\text{CuO(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow CuCl}_2\text{(aq) + H}_2\text{O(l)}$

Since metallic oxides react with acids to give salts and water, similar to the reaction of a base with an acid, metallic oxides are said to be *basic oxides*.

### Reaction of a Non-metallic Oxide with Base

You saw the reaction between carbon dioxide and calcium hydroxide (lime water) in Activity 2.5. Calcium hydroxide, which is a base, reacts with carbon dioxide to produce a salt and water. Since this is similar to the reaction between a base and an acid, we can conclude that nonmetallic oxides are *acidic in nature*.

### Questions

1. Why should curd and sour substances not be kept in brass and copper vessels?
> **Answer:** Curd and sour substances are acidic in nature. Brass and copper are metals. When acidic substances are kept in brass or copper vessels, the acids react with the metals to produce hydrogen gas and harmful metallic salts. These metallic salts can make the food poisonous and unfit for consumption, as well as corrode the vessels.

2. Which gas is usually liberated when an acid reacts with a metal? Illustrate with an example. How will you test for the presence of this gas?
> **Answer:** The gas usually liberated when an acid reacts with a metal is **hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$)**.
> **Example:** When dilute hydrochloric acid reacts with zinc metal:
> $\text{Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow ZnCl}_2\text{(aq) + H}_2\text{(g)}$
> **Test for hydrogen gas:** To test for the presence of hydrogen gas, bring a **burning candle or splinter** near the mouth of the test tube or the gas-filled bubble. Hydrogen gas burns with a characteristic **"pop" sound**.

3. Metal compound A reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to produce effervescence. The gas evolved extinguishes a burning candle. Write a balanced chemical equation for the reaction if one of the compounds formed is calcium chloride.
> **Answer:** The gas that extinguishes a burning candle and causes effervescence with an acid is **carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$)**. This indicates that metal compound A is a **metal carbonate**. Since one of the compounds formed is calcium chloride ($\text{CaCl}_2$), the metal compound A must be **calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$)**.
> **Balanced chemical equation:**
> $\text{CaCO}_3\text{(s) + 2HCl(aq) \rightarrow CaCl}_2\text{(aq) + H}_2\text{O(l) + CO}_2\text{(g)}$
