This section provides a concise summary of the key concepts covered in the chapter, reinforcing the understanding of carbon's unique properties, bonding, and the practical applications of its compounds, followed by a set of review exercises.

# What you have learnt

*   Carbon is a versatile element that forms the basis for all living organisms and many of the things we use.
*   This large variety of compounds is formed by carbon because of its tetravalency and the property of catenation that it exhibits.
*   Covalent bonds are formed by the sharing of electrons between two atoms so that both can achieve a completely filled outermost shell.
*   Carbon forms covalent bonds with itself and other elements such as hydrogen, oxygen, sulphur, nitrogen and chlorine.
*   Carbon also forms compounds containing double and triple bonds between carbon atoms. These carbon chains may be in the form of straight chains, branched chains or rings.
*   The ability of carbon to form chains gives rise to a homologous series of compounds in which the same functional group is attached to carbon chains of different lengths.
*   The functional groups such as alcohols, aldehydes, ketones and carboxylic acids bestow characteristic properties to the carbon compounds that contain them.
*   Carbon and its compounds are some of our major sources of fuels.
*   Ethanol and ethanoic acid are carbon compounds of importance in our daily lives.
*   The action of soaps and detergents is based on the presence of both hydrophobic and hydrophilic groups in the molecule and this helps to emulsify the oily dirt and hence its removal.

> **Answer:**

4.  Explain the nature of the covalent bond using the bond formation in $\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$.

> **Answer:**
> 4.  **Nature of covalent bond in $\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$ (Chloromethane):**
>     A covalent bond is formed by the **sharing of electron pairs** between two atoms, allowing both atoms to achieve a stable, completely filled outermost electron shell (noble gas configuration).
>     In $\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$:
>     *   Carbon has 4 valence electrons and needs 4 more to complete its octet.
>     *   Hydrogen has 1 valence electron and needs 1 more to complete its duplet (like Helium).
>     *   Chlorine has 7 valence electrons and needs 1 more to complete its octet.
>     Carbon forms three single covalent bonds with three hydrogen atoms by sharing one electron pair with each hydrogen. Carbon also forms one single covalent bond with the chlorine atom by sharing one electron pair. In doing so, carbon achieves 8 electrons in its outermost shell, each hydrogen achieves 2 electrons, and chlorine achieves 8 electrons (6 from lone pairs + 2 from shared pair). All atoms achieve a stable electron configuration without the transfer of electrons, resulting in strong intramolecular bonds.

    (b) $\text{H}_2\text{S}$.
    (c) propanone.

>

>         ```
>             H . . S . . H
>                 . .
>         ```
>
>     (c) **Propanone ($\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3$):**
>         Carbon (central) forms a double bond with oxygen and single bonds with two $\text{CH}_3$ groups.
>         ```
>             H   H   O   H
>             . . . . //  . .
>           H : C : C : C : H
>             . . . . \ . .
>             H   H   H

>         (The oxygen has two lone pairs)
>
>     (d) **$\text{F}_2$ (Fluorine molecule):**
>         Fluorine (Group 17) has 7 valence electrons. Two fluorine atoms share one pair of electrons to form a single covalent bond, each achieving an octet.

6.  What is an homologous series? Explain with an example.

> **Answer:**
> 6.  **Homologous Series:** A homologous series is a series of organic compounds in which all members have the same functional group and similar chemical properties, but differ from the adjacent member by a $-\text{CH}_2-$ group in their molecular formula.
>     **Characteristics of a homologous series:**
>     *   All members can be represented by a general formula.
>     *   Successive members differ by a $-\text{CH}_2-$ unit (and 14 u in molecular mass).
>     *   They have similar chemical properties due to the same functional group.
>     *   There is a gradation in physical properties (like melting point, boiling point, density) as molecular mass increases.
>
>     **Example: Alkane homologous series**
>     *   General formula: $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2}$
>     *   Members:
>         *   Methane ($\text{CH}_4$)
>         *   Ethane ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$)
>         *   Propane ($\text{C}_3\text{H}_8$)
>         *   Butane ($\text{C}_4\text{H}_{10}$)
>     Each successive member differs by a $-\text{CH}_2-$ unit.

7.  How can ethanol and ethanoic acid be differentiated on the basis of their physical and chemical properties?

> **Answer:**
> 7.  **Differentiation between Ethanol and Ethanoic Acid:**
>     *   **Physical Properties:**
>         *   **Smell:** Ethanol has a characteristic alcoholic smell. Ethanoic acid has a pungent, vinegar-like smell.
>         *   **Freezing Point:** Pure ethanoic acid (glacial acetic acid) has a relatively high melting/freezing point (290 K or 16.6 °C) and often freezes in cold climates. Ethanol has a much lower freezing point (156 K or -114 °C) and does not freeze easily in normal winter conditions.
>     *   **Chemical Properties:**
>         *   **Litmus Test:** Ethanoic acid is an acid, so it will turn blue litmus paper red. Ethanol is neutral and will have no effect on litmus paper.
>         *   **Reaction with Carbonates/Bicarbonates:** Ethanoic acid reacts with sodium carbonate ($\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$) or sodium hydrogencarbonate ($\text{NaHCO}_3$) to produce brisk effervescence (due to $\text{CO}_2$ gas evolution). Ethanol does not react with carbonates or bicarbonates.
>             *   $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{NaHCO}_3 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2(\text{g})$
>         *   **Esterification:** Both react with each other in the presence of concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ to form a sweet-smelling ester, but this is a reaction *between* them, not a distinguishing test for one over the other.

8.  Why does micelle formation take place when soap is added to water? Will a micelle be formed in other solvents such as ethanol also?

> **Answer:**
> 8.  **Why micelle formation takes place when soap is added to water:**
>     Soap molecules have a unique structure with two distinct parts:
>     *   A long **hydrophobic** (water-repelling) hydrocarbon chain (the 'tail').
>     *   A short **hydrophilic** (water-attracting) ionic part (the 'head').
>     When soap is added to water, the hydrophobic tails are insoluble in water and try to avoid contact with it. To minimize this contact, they cluster together in the interior. Simultaneously, the hydrophilic ionic heads are soluble in water and orient themselves outwards, facing the water. This spherical arrangement, with hydrophobic tails inward and hydrophilic heads outward, is called a **micelle**. This structure allows the soap to encapsulate oily dirt in its hydrophobic core, keeping the dirt suspended in water and enabling washing.
>
>     **Will a micelle be formed in other solvents such as ethanol also?**
>     No, a typical micelle (as formed in water) will **not** be formed in solvents like ethanol. Ethanol is a polar solvent, but it also has a significant non-polar character due to its ethyl group. The hydrocarbon tail of the soap molecule would be soluble in ethanol to some extent, and the ionic head would also interact with the polar ethanol molecules. Without the strong immiscibility between the solvent and the hydrophobic tail, the driving force for micelle formation (to hide the hydrophobic part from the solvent) is absent or greatly reduced. Instead of micelles, soap molecules would likely dissolve individually or form different types of aggregates in ethanol.

9.  Why are carbon and its compounds used as fuels for most applications?

> **Answer:**
> 9.  **Reasons why carbon and its compounds are used as fuels:**
>     *   **High Calorific Value:** They release a large amount of heat energy upon combustion, making them efficient sources of energy.
>     *   **Readily Available:** Many carbon compounds (like coal, petroleum, natural gas, wood) are naturally abundant and easily accessible.
>     *   **Controllable Combustion:** Their burning can be controlled (e.g., by adjusting air supply) to produce a steady flame and heat.
>     *   **Relatively Clean Burning (for complete combustion):** When burned completely in sufficient oxygen, they primarily produce carbon dioxide and water, which are generally less harmful than other combustion products (though $\text{CO}_2$ is a greenhouse gas).
>     *   **Ease of Storage and Transport:** Many carbon-based fuels (like petrol, diesel, LPG) are liquids or easily liquefied gases, making them convenient to store and transport.

10. Explain the formation of scum when hard water is treated with soap.

> **Answer:**
> 10. **Formation of scum when hard water is treated with soap:**
>     Hard water contains dissolved salts of calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) and magnesium ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$) ions. Soap molecules are typically sodium or potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids (e.g., sodium stearate).
>     When soap is added to hard water, the calcium and magnesium ions react with the soap molecules. The $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ions displace the sodium or potassium ions from the soap, forming **insoluble calcium or magnesium salts of fatty acids**. These insoluble salts appear as a white, sticky, curdy precipitate called **scum**.
>     This scum does not lather and adheres to clothes or surfaces, making the cleaning process ineffective and leaving behind residues. This is why more soap is needed in hard water, as a portion of the soap is wasted in forming scum before it can effectively clean.

12. What is hydrogenation? What is its industrial application?

> **Answer:**
> 12. **Hydrogenation:**
>     Hydrogenation is a chemical reaction that involves the **addition of hydrogen** ($\text{H}_2$) to an unsaturated compound (containing carbon-carbon double or triple bonds) to form a saturated compound (containing only carbon-carbon single bonds). This reaction typically occurs in the presence of a catalyst such as nickel (Ni), palladium (Pd), or platinum (Pt).
>
>     **Industrial Application:**
>     The most significant industrial application of hydrogenation is the **conversion of vegetable oils into vegetable ghee (vanaspati ghee or margarine)**. Vegetable oils contain long unsaturated carbon chains (unsaturated fatty acids), which are generally liquid at room temperature. Through hydrogenation, these unsaturated fatty acids are converted into saturated fatty acids, making the product solid or semi-solid at room temperature. This process is used to produce solid fats for cooking and baking.

13. Which of the following hydrocarbons undergo addition reactions: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6, \text{C}_3\text{H}_8, \text{C}_2\text{H}_4, \text{C}_2\text{H}_2$ and $\text{CH}_4$.

> **Answer:**
> 13. **Hydrocarbons that undergo addition reactions:**
>     Addition reactions are characteristic of **unsaturated hydrocarbons**, meaning those that contain carbon-carbon double bonds (alkenes) or triple bonds (alkynes). Saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes) typically undergo substitution reactions.
>     *   $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ (Ethane): Saturated alkane.
>     *   $\text{C}_3\text{H}_8$ (Propane): Saturated alkane.
>     *   $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4$ (Ethene): Unsaturated alkene (contains a double bond). **Undergoes addition reactions.**
>     *   $\text{C}_2\text{H}_2$ (Ethyne): Unsaturated alkyne (contains a triple bond). **Undergoes addition reactions.**
>     *   $\text{CH}_4$ (Methane): Saturated alkane.
>
>     Therefore, $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4$ (ethene) and $\text{C}_2\text{H}_2$ (ethyne) undergo addition reactions.

14. Give a test that can be used to differentiate between saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons.

> **Answer:**
> 14. **Test to differentiate between saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons:**
>     The **Bromine Water Test** can be used to differentiate between saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons.
>     *   **Procedure:** Add a few drops of orange-brown bromine water ($\text{Br}_2(\text{aq})$) to separate samples of the hydrocarbons and shake well.
>     *   **Observation:**
>         *   **Unsaturated hydrocarbons** (alkenes and alkynes) will rapidly decolorize the orange-brown bromine water, turning it colorless. This is because bromine adds across the double or triple bond in an addition reaction.
>         *   **Saturated hydrocarbons** (alkanes) will not react with bromine water under normal conditions (in the absence of UV light) and thus, the orange-brown color of the bromine water will persist.

15. Explain the mechanism of the cleaning action of soaps.

> **Answer:**
> 15. **Mechanism of the cleaning action of soaps:**
>     The cleaning action of soaps is based on their unique molecular structure, which has both a hydrophilic (water-loving) and a hydrophobic (water-hating) part.
>     1.  **Dual Nature of Soap Molecule:** A soap molecule is a long-chain fatty acid salt. It consists of:
>         *   A long non-polar hydrocarbon chain (hydrophobic 'tail') that is soluble in oil and grease.
>         *   A short ionic (polar) part (hydrophilic 'head') that is soluble in water.
>     2.  **Interaction with Dirt:** When soap is added to water containing oily or greasy dirt, the hydrophobic tails of the soap molecules orient themselves towards the oil/grease droplets, dissolving into them. The hydrophilic heads remain outside, facing the water.
>     3.  **Micelle Formation:** As more soap molecules surround the oil/grease droplet, they form a spherical structure called a **micelle**. In a micelle, the oily dirt is trapped in the center (hydrophobic core), while the hydrophilic ionic heads form the outer surface, facing the surrounding water.
>     4.  **Emulsification and Suspension:** The formation of micelles effectively emulsifies the oil/grease, breaking it down into tiny droplets. These micelles, with their charged, hydrophilic outer surfaces, are now soluble in water. The electrostatic repulsion between the charged surfaces of different micelles prevents them from coalescing and allows them to remain suspended as a stable colloidal solution in the water.
>     5.  **Rinsing Away:** Due to their suspension in water, the dirt-laden micelles can be easily rinsed away with water, leaving the surface or fabric clean.
