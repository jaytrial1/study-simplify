# Chapter Summary and Exercises

### What You Have Learnt

*   The ability of the eye to focus on both near and distant objects by adjusting its focal length is called the **accommodation of the eye**.
*   The smallest distance at which the eye can see objects clearly without strain is called the **near point of the eye** or the **least distance of distinct vision**. For a young adult with normal vision, this is about 25 cm.
*   The common refractive defects of vision include **myopia**, **hypermetropia**, and **presbyopia**.
    *   **Myopia** (short-sightedness), where the image of distant objects is focused before the retina, is corrected by using a **concave lens** of suitable power.
    *   **Hypermetropia** (far-sightedness), where the image of nearby objects is focused beyond the retina, is corrected by using a **convex lens** of suitable power.
    *   **Presbyopia** is an age-related condition where the eye loses its power of accommodation.
*   The splitting of white light into its component colors is called **dispersion**.
*   **Scattering of light** causes phenomena like the blue color of the sky and the reddening of the Sun at sunrise and sunset.

### Questions (from original text)

1.  What is meant by power of accommodation of the eye?
    > **Answer:** The *power of accommodation* of the eye is its ability to adjust the focal length of its crystalline lens to focus on objects located at different distances. This adjustment is achieved by the ciliary muscles changing the curvature (and thus the thickness) of the eye lens.

2.  A person with a myopic eye cannot see objects beyond 1.2 m distinctly. What should be the type of the corrective lens used to restore proper vision?
    > **Answer:** A person with myopia (nearsightedness) needs a **concave lens** (diverging lens) to correct their vision. The concave lens will diverge the light rays from distant objects slightly before they enter the eye, causing them to focus correctly on the retina.

3.  What is the far point and near point of the human eye with normal vision?
    > **Answer:**
    *   The **far point** of a human eye with normal vision is **infinity**. This means a normal eye can see very distant objects clearly.
    *   The **near point** of a human eye with normal vision is about **25 cm**. This is the closest distance at which an object can be seen distinctly without strain.

4.  A student has difficulty reading the blackboard while sitting in the last row. What could be the defect the child is suffering from? How can it be corrected?
    > **Answer:** The student is likely suffering from **myopia** (nearsightedness). This defect makes distant objects (like the blackboard from the last row) appear blurred while nearby objects are seen clearly. It can be corrected by using a **concave lens** of suitable power.

5.  A person needs a lens of power -5.5 dioptres for correcting his distant vision. For correcting his near vision he needs a lens of power +1.5 dioptre. What is the focal length of the lens required for correcting (i) distant vision, and (ii) near vision?
    > **Answer:**
    > The power of a lens (P) is given by the formula P = 1/f, where f is the focal length in meters.
    > (i) **For distant vision:**
    > Power (P1) = -5.5 D
    > Focal length (f1) = 1/P1 = 1/(-5.5) m = -0.1818 m (approx) = -18.18 cm (approx)
    > (ii) **For near vision:**
    > Power (P2) = +1.5 D
    > Focal length (f2) = 1/P2 = 1/(+1.5) m = +0.6667 m (approx) = +66.67 cm (approx)

6.  The far point of a myopic person is 80 cm in front of the eye. What is the nature and power of the lens required to correct the problem?
    > **Answer:**
    > For a myopic person, the far point is not at infinity. To correct this, a concave lens is needed so that it forms a virtual image of a distant object (at infinity) at the person's far point (80 cm).
    > Object distance, u = -∞ (for distant objects)
    > Image distance, v = -80 cm = -0.80 m (virtual image formed at the far point)
    > Using the lens formula: 1/f = 1/v - 1/u
    > 1/f = 1/(-0.80) - 1/(-∞)
    > 1/f = -1/0.80 - 0
    > 1/f = -1.25 D
    > Therefore, the **nature of the lens is concave** (since power is negative) and its **power is -1.25 Dioptres**.

8.  Why is a normal eye not able to see clearly the objects placed closer than 25 cm?
    > **Answer:** A normal eye cannot see objects clearly when they are placed closer than 25 cm because the ciliary muscles cannot contract enough to increase the curvature of the eye lens beyond a certain limit. This means the focal length of the eye lens cannot be decreased sufficiently to converge the light rays from such close objects precisely onto the retina. As a result, the image formed is blurred, and the eye experiences strain. This minimum distance of 25 cm is known as the least distance of distinct vision or the near point.

9.  What happens to the image distance in the eye when we increase the distance of an object from the eye?
    > **Answer:** The image distance in the eye (the distance between the eye lens and the retina) remains essentially *fixed*. When we increase the distance of an object from the eye, the ciliary muscles relax, causing the eye lens to become thinner and its focal length to increase. This adjustment ensures that the image is always formed precisely on the retina, maintaining a clear image despite the change in object distance. The eye's ability to do this is called accommodation.

10. Why do stars twinkle?
    > **Answer:** Stars twinkle due to **atmospheric refraction**. Starlight travels through various layers of Earth's atmosphere, which have continuously changing refractive indices due to variations in air density, temperature, and air currents. As light passes through these turbulent layers, it undergoes continuous bending. Since stars are very distant and act as point sources of light, these continuous changes in refraction cause the path of light from the star to vary slightly. This leads to fluctuations in the amount of light entering our eyes, making the star appear to flicker or twinkle (sometimes brighter, sometimes fainter).

11. Explain why the planets do not twinkle.
    > **Answer:** Planets do not twinkle because they are much closer to Earth than stars and appear as *extended sources* of light, rather than point sources. A planet can be considered a collection of many point-sized light sources. While light from individual points on the planet may undergo fluctuations due to atmospheric refraction, the total amount of light entering our eye from all these points averages out. The increase in brightness from some points is compensated by the decrease in brightness from others at any given instant, effectively nullifying the twinkling effect.

12. Why does the sky appear dark instead of blue to an astronaut?
    > **Answer:** The sky appears dark (black) to an astronaut in space because there is **no atmosphere** to scatter sunlight. The blue color of the sky is a result of the scattering of shorter wavelength blue light by the fine particles and molecules in Earth's atmosphere (Tyndall effect). In the vacuum of space, or at very high altitudes where the atmosphere is extremely thin, there are virtually no particles to scatter sunlight. Therefore, light travels directly from the Sun without being dispersed, and the background appears dark.
