This section introduces atmospheric refraction and explains various natural phenomena caused by it, such as the twinkling of stars and the non-twinkling of planets.

# Atmospheric Refraction Phenomena

You might have noticed the apparent random wavering or flickering of objects when seen through a turbulent stream of hot air rising above a fire or a radiator. This occurs because the air directly above the fire becomes hotter and less dense than the cooler air above it. Hotter air has a slightly lower refractive index than cooler air. Since the physical conditions (density and refractive index) of the air are not stationary, the apparent position of objects viewed through this hot air fluctuates. This wavering is a small-scale example of **atmospheric refraction**—the refraction of light by the Earth's atmosphere.

### Twinkling of Stars

The twinkling of stars is a similar phenomenon, but on a much larger scale. It is caused by the atmospheric refraction of starlight.

*   When starlight enters the Earth's atmosphere, it undergoes continuous refraction before reaching the Earth's surface.
*   Atmospheric refraction occurs in a medium (the atmosphere) that has a gradually changing refractive index. The density of air, and thus its refractive index, varies with altitude and local conditions.
*   Because the atmosphere bends starlight *towards the normal*, the apparent position of a star is slightly different from its actual position. A star appears slightly higher (above) its actual position when viewed near the horizon.
*   Furthermore, this apparent position of the star is not stationary. Since the physical conditions of the Earth's atmosphere (temperature, density, air currents) are constantly changing, the path of light rays coming from the star varies slightly.
*   Stars are extremely distant, approximating point-sized sources of light. As the path of light rays from a star fluctuates, the amount of starlight entering our eye flickers—the star sometimes appears brighter and at other times fainter, creating the twinkling effect.

### Why Planets Do Not Twinkle

Unlike stars, planets do not twinkle. This is because planets are much closer to the Earth and are seen as *extended sources* of light, not point-sized sources. If we consider a planet as a collection of a large number of individual point-sized sources of light, the total variation in the amount of light entering our eye from all these individual sources will, on average, cancel each other out. Some point sources might appear brighter while others appear fainter at any given moment, but the overall effect averages to zero, thus nullifying the twinkling effect.

### Advance Sunrise and Delayed Sunset

Atmospheric refraction also causes the Sun to be visible to us about 2 minutes *before* the actual sunrise and about 2 minutes *after* the actual sunset.

*   **Actual sunrise** refers to the moment the Sun geometrically crosses the horizon.
*   Due to atmospheric refraction, light rays from the Sun (which is actually below the horizon) bend as they pass through the denser layers of the atmosphere, making the Sun appear to be above the horizon earlier than it actually is.
*   Similarly, at sunset, the Sun appears above the horizon for about 2 minutes even after it has geometrically set.
*   This phenomenon also explains the apparent flattening of the Sun's disc at sunrise and sunset.
