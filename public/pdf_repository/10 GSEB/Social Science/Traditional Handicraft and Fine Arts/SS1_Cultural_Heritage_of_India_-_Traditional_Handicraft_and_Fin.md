1_Understanding Indian Cultural Heritage
This section introduces the concept of Indian cultural heritage, distinguishing it from material and genetic inheritance, and highlights its significance and ancient nature.

# Understanding Indian Cultural Heritage

India is a country with a rich cultural heritage. Heritage includes both materialistic and genetic aspects. While *genetic inheritance* refers to physical and mental characteristics passed from parents, *material heritage* constitutes properties like house, land, and estates inherited from ancestors.

*Cultural heritage*, however, is what a person obtains or creates using their intellect and skill. It also includes traditions, customs, rituals, and specific lifestyles adopted by ancestors. Furthermore, elements like education, farming, trade, daily life rules, festivals, entertainment, art, handicrafts, beliefs, and skills are part of cultural heritage.

India, as an ancient country, possesses rich traditions, social values, customs, and a strong family system. Preserving this heritage is our duty. Ancient literature mentions 64 arts, including handicrafts, workmanship, art, drawing, music, dramatic art, and dance. The prosperity of India, partly due to valuable ancient currencies, attracted visitors. Yoga education, now accepted globally, is also considered part of cultural heritage, leading to the celebration of June 21st as 'World Yoga Day'.

This chapter will explore the diverse art and craft forms of India.

2_Art of Indian Artisans and Clay Work
This section focuses on the traditional skills of Indian artisans and the ancient art of working with clay.

# Art of Indian Artisans

The beauty of Indian cultural heritage is evident in the traditional art of its artisans. Through their remarkable skills in art and handicraft, they have shaped the varied lifestyles of people. Handicrafts like:
*   Weaving and embroidery
*   Wooden art
*   Clay work
*   Drawing and painting
*   Leather industry
*   Enamel work
*   Sculpture
*   Architecture
*   Akik work
*   Diamond-related artistic workmanship
*   Knitting work

All these possess a unique identity in India.

## Clay Work

Humans and clay have been closely linked since ancient times, with clay items used extensively before the invention of metal. Pottery was central to life, from toys and pots to storage vessels for grains, milk, ghee, curd, and buttermilk. Kitchenware was predominantly clay-based. House walls and huts were plastered with a mixture of cow dung and clay.

Archaeological sites of the Harappa, Mohenjo-Daro, and Lothal cultures have yielded numerous red-coloured clay utensils. The *potter's wheel* is considered an excellent ancient Indian invention for clay work. Even today, the *garba* (a holed pitcher holding an earthen lamp) used during Navratri showcases this tradition.

India was renowned for producing both baked (*terracotta*) and unbaked clay utensils since ancient times, as evidenced by findings at Nagarjunakonda in South India and Laghnaj in Mehsana district.

3_Textile Arts: Weaving and Embroidery
This section delves into the textile traditions of India, specifically the arts of weaving and embroidery.

# Textile Arts: Weaving and Embroidery

## Art of Weaving

The process of making long threads from small pieces of cotton and joining them is called *spinning*. Mahatma Gandhi emphasized spinning as a cottage industry, linking it to the Swadeshi movement for self-reliance during the freedom struggle, which made history.

### Hand Weaving

India has been known for its textile expertise since ancient times. A famous example is the fine muslin of Dhaka, where an entire piece of cloth of a specific size could pass through a ring or fit into a matchbox.

Hand-woven items like carpets (*Galicha*) from various regions, knitted work on clothing, and other artistry have held prominent places. *Patola* of Patan, Kanjivaram and Banarasi sarees, and *Bandhani* of Rajasthan are uniquely Indian identities.

Gujarat flourished in weaving during the Solanki era. Many artisans settled in Patan during the reign of Siddharaj Jaysingh, making Patolas world-famous due to their skill. This art is over 850 years old, and only a few artisans possess the necessary complex skills. Silk fabric (*Bevad Ikt*) produced in Patan is called Patola. *Ikt* refers to the weaving technique. Patola sarees are designed to be worn on either side, are durable, and their colours remain intact. ## Art of Embroidery

Evidence of embroidery has been found on clothing of idols and statues from the Harappa and Mohenjo-Daro excavations. The art of weaving designs onto clothing for men, women, and children has been famous since ancient times. During the Indus civilization, embroidery was seen on clothes in Sindh, Saurashtra, and Kutch regions. Kashmiri (*kasab*) work from Kashmir is also well-known.

Besides embroidery, *Bandhani* from Jamnagar, Jetpur, Bhuj, and Mandvi in Gujarat is very popular. Traditional Bandhani, with its luster and beautiful designs like elephants, puppets, dice, and pitchers, remains popular. Women in the Kutch and Saurashtra regions have significantly contributed to this cottage industry. Traditional embroidery art is seen on items like:
*   Sakh torans
*   Chakda
*   Pillow covers
*   Bed sheets
*   Todaliya
*   Clothing like kediya

Embroidery is also done on quilts and shawls. The embroidery work of the 'Jat' community in the Banni area of Kutch is particularly unique.

4_Handicrafts: Leather, Jewelry, and Metal Work
This section explores traditional Indian crafts involving leather, precious stones (diamonds, beads, akik), enamel, jari, and metals.

# Handicrafts: Leather, Jewelry, and Metal Work

## Leather Industry

The skin of dead animals has been used for various purposes in ancient India. After processing the skin traditionally, leather was used to make:
*   *Masak* (leather bag for water)
*   *Kos* (another water lifting device)
*   Musical instruments like drum, dholak, tabla
*   Bellows for blacksmiths
*   Footwear
*   Leather belts for animals
*   Shields used during wars

The leather industry held a significant place in ancient India. Well-knitted *Mojadi* from Rajasthan, shoes, leather purses, belts, and gear for horses and camels like *saaj*, *palan*, *lagaam*, and whips were widely used.

## Diamond, Bead Work, and Enamel Work

India's long coastline (7517 km) provided access to diamonds and beads in abundance. Gems and diamonds have been used extensively since ancient times, with trade carried out through sea routes. Diamond-studded ornaments have been highly demanded abroad. World-famous diamonds like 'Kohinoor' and 'Great Mughal' were found in India. Indians' fondness for diamond and beaded ornaments has led to great variety. Historically, rich and royal people wore diamond jewelry alongside gold.

Diamonds, rubies, beads, emeralds, and topaz were used to adorn thrones, crowns, and royal jewelry like bracelets and garlands. Bead work is also prominent in Gujarat, seen in items like:
*   Beautiful bead *Torans*
*   *Pachhit*
*   Pots
*   *Bari* (door grills/frames)
*   *Chakda* (traditional wall piece)
*   Decorative coconuts for ceremonies (*Lagna na nariyel*)
*   *Indhoni*
*   *Modiya* (net covering bullock's mouth/horns)
*   Handfans
*   Animal back covers (*Jhool*)

These items are knitted artistically.

India has been a leading country in jewelry since ancient times. It is unique in its expertise in *enamel work* alongside gold and silver work. Enamel work is the artistic skill of inlaying bright colours (red, green, blue) into gold or silver ornaments like rings, bangles, earrings, keychains, and chains. Experts in enamel work are found particularly in Jaipur, Delhi, Lucknow, Varanasi, and Hyderabad.

## Jari Work

*Jari work* has existed in India since ancient times. It involves weaving golden and silver threads into clothes and doing embroidery with them for decoration. Surat is a prominent center for jari work. The knitting and embroidery of *Jardoshi work* are particularly beautiful. Artisans in Surat can create dresses like sarees, panetar, and gharchola with intricate jari borders.

## Metal Work (Engraving)

Metal work is a very old art form in India. The Metal Age followed the Stone Age. Artisans at Lothal created weapons like sickles, drills (curved saws), and needles from copper and bronze. They also made utensils, idols, and pots. Arms and ammunition for wars were made of metal. Gold and silver were used for ornaments, copper, brass, and bronze for utensils and idols, while iron was used for instruments and weapons.

5_Handicrafts: Wood, Inlay, and Akik
This section covers traditional Indian crafts using wood, the art of inlaying precious stones, and working with Akik stones.

# Handicrafts: Wood, Inlay, and Akik

## Wooden Art

Humans, trees, and forests are closely connected. Initially, wood was used as firewood. Later, it was used for making weapons, constructing buildings, and other construction work. Gradually, wood found different uses:
*   Making idols
*   Toys for children
*   Wooden pillars
*   Windows and doors
*   Small huts on terraces
*   Thrones
*   Chairs
*   Grills

This led to the development of *wood carving*. Wooden furniture like swings and toys from Idar and Sankheda in Gujarat are well-known.

## Inlay Work

*Inlay work* is considered a very ancient art of India. Indian kings, emperors, rulers, and royal people wore ornaments like necklaces, bracelets, crowns, rings, nose rings, and *Damni* (Tiko) studded with precious gems such as diamonds, pearls, and rubies. Artisans were skilled in inlay art. The Bikaner area of Rajasthan is especially popular for inlay ornaments.

## Akik Work

*Akik* is a type of precious stone, often found in river valleys. It is mainly chalcedony mixed with silica, appearing as blue or white stone. *Carnelian*, a semi-transparent stone with a beautiful red colour, is a main type. In Gujarat, various sizes of precious stones are available at Ranpur and Surat. These stones are sent to Khambhat to be shaped into beautiful ornaments and designs. Beads and garlands are prepared from these stones.

6_Painting as an Art Form
This section discusses painting as a significant art form in India, highlighting its ancient origins and examples.

# Painting as an Art Form

Among all arts, painting holds a foremost position. It is an art expressed through lines and colours, aiming to express emotions by imitating animate and inanimate objects from nature. Evidence of Indian painting dates back 5000 years to the Harappan culture. Archaeological excavations have uncovered many examples.

Cave paintings by primitive humans from the Stone Age at Bhimbetka (Madhya Pradesh) show pictures of animals and birds. People also drew geometrical designs and sketches. Notable examples include elephants, rhinoceroses, and deer. The paintings at Ajanta and Ellora are considered incomparable specimens. Drawing *swastik*, *Pitcher*, and Ganesha sketches during festivals and celebrations is an old tradition in India. *Rangoli* making is also a very old tradition in some regions.

7_Indian Fine Arts: Music
This section explores the fine arts of India, focusing specifically on the rich tradition of Indian music.

# Indian Fine Arts: Music

Singing, playing instruments, dancing, and performing characters are unique features of Indian culture. Dramatic arts based on epics like Ramayana and Mahabharata have historically provided both entertainment and moral education.

## Music

Indian music is unique globally due to its tune, rhythm, and harmony, distinguishing it from music in other countries. *Samveda*, one of the four Vedas, is associated with music; its *Richas* (hymns) are sung with tune and rhythm. Music encompasses both singing and playing instruments. The seven basic notes (*swaras*) of music are SA, RE, GA, MA, PA, DHA, NI.

Indian music is primarily divided into two parts: *classical music* and *folk music*. Classical music is believed to have six main *ragas*:
1.  Shree
2.  Deepak
3.  Hindol
4.  Megh
5.  Bhairavi
6.  Malkauns

Many texts on music were written in ancient India. Three important ones are:

*   **Sangeet Makarand:** Written by the music scholar Narad around 900 BC. It describes 19 types of *veenas* and 101 rhythms, which are still relevant.
*   **Sangeet Ratnakar:** Written by Pandit Sarangdev, who lived in Daulatabad (Devgiri) and was familiar with both South and North Indian music. Pandit Vishnu Narayan Bhatkhande considers it the most authentic book on music. It is considered unique for understanding all aspects of music.
*   **Sangeet Parijat:** An important book on music written by Pandit Ahobale in 1665, focusing on Northern Hindustani music. He noted that each raga has its own individual identity and characteristics, describing 29 types of tunes.

# Indian Fine Arts: Dance

The word '*Nritya*' comes from the Sanskrit word '*Nrit*'. Dance is a medium for expressing beauty and achieving aesthetic joy through rhythm (*Taal*) and harmony (*laya*). Shiva is considered the originator and lord of dance, known as *Nataraj*. He is believed to have brought dance from heaven to earth to teach it to people.

### Bharatnatyam

The origin of Bharatnatyam is believed to be the Tanjore district in Tamil Nadu. The primary sources for this dance form are Bharatmuni's '*Natyasashtra*' and Nandikeshwar's '*Abhinav Darpan*'. Prominent figures who have preserved this heritage include Gopi Krishna, Mrinalini Sarabhai, Vaijanti Mala, and Hema Malini.

### Kuchipudi

This dance style was created in the 15th century and is largely based on depicting women's beauty. It incorporates various poses and gestures performed by both male and female dancers. Kuchipudi is famous in Andhra Pradesh. Well-known dancers who have preserved this tradition include Guru Prahlad Sharma, Raja Reddy, and Shobha Naidu.

## Kathakali

This dance style is very famous in Kerala. The word *Kathakali* is derived from dramas based on ancient epics (Ramayana, Mahabharata) and mixed dramas in Sanskrit and Malayalam. The characters wear beautiful, elaborate costumes (*plaited attires*) and artistic crowns. Recognising the character relies on reading their facial expressions. Dancers use facial expressions and hand poses to introduce characters from the 'Trilok'. They appear in front of a curtain illuminated only by an earthen lamp. Notable figures who have gained fame nationally and internationally include Poet Vallathod (founder of Kalamandal Kathakali), Kalamandalam Krishnaprasad, and Shivaraman.

## Kathak Dance

The saying 'Kathan kare so Kathak kahave' relates to Kathak dance, meaning "one who tells a story is called a Kathak". This dance narrates the life events and incidences of Lord Krishna. It is thought to have developed with the '*Shrungar Bhakti*' (devotion expressing love) of the Vaishnav community. In this style, the dancer turns around on one leg and incorporates various references through movement. Women typically wear narrow trousers and a frilled dress. This dance style has been kept alive by artists like Pandit Birju Maharaj, Sitara Devi, and Kumudini Lakhia.

# Manipuri Dance

People of Manipur dance on every occasion and festival. This style is primarily based on the *Rasalilas* and *Bal Lila* (childhood stories) of Shri Krishna. Manipuri dance has two main types: *Lasya* (gentle, graceful) and *Tandav* (vigorous, energetic). Dancers wear a plaited green coloured skirt called '*Kumin*', along with a silk blouse and a belt around the waist. Artists like Guru Amobisingh, Atombosingh, Guru Bipin Sinha, Nayna Zaveri, and Nirmal Mehta have gained worldwide fame.

9_Indian Fine Arts: Dramatic Art
This section focuses on the tradition of dramatic art in India, including theatre and performance styles like Bhavai.

# Indian Fine Arts: Dramatic Art

Presenting moral values (*sanskar*) alongside entertainment is a unique feature of Indian dramatic art. Anchors and clowns often entertain the audience. Bharatmuni's '*Natyashastra*' is a well-known text in this field. Dramatic art, which combines writing, performance, and audiovisual elements, can entertain people of all ages. Bharatmuni described dramatic art as an amalgamation of all arts, stating, "There is no book, there is no sculpture, there is no knowledge, there is no deed which is lacking in dramatic art". Bharatmuni's first play was 'Devasur Sangram'.

Sanskrit literature boasts many renowned dramatists. Bhas is considered a poet among poets, leaving behind dramas based on the Mahabharata like 'Karnabhar', 'Urubhanga', and 'Dutvakyam'. Kalidas, a foremost poet, gifted plays like 'Abhigyan Shakuntalam', 'Vikramorvashiyam', and 'Malvikagnimitram'. Many other dramatists also enriched Sanskrit dramatic art.

In Gujarati dramatic art, the name of 'Jayshankar Sundari' is foremost. Other remarkable playwrights include Amrut Nayak, Bapulal Nayak, Dina Pathak, Jashwant Thakar, Upendra Trivedi, and Pravin Joshi, and Deepak Gheewala. Regional and other drama institutions have also contributed significantly.

## Bhavai

Scriptwriters describe *Bhavai* as an emotion-oriented drama. This special dramatic art was started about 700 years ago by Asait Thakar. It provided public education along with economical entertainment and flourished during the Solanki Era. It was typically performed without curtains, incorporating light humor and music played on a trumpet. Main features include characters disguised as Ramdev, Jhanda Jhulan, Kajoda, etc.

The themes of Bhavai often include protest against social vices. Characters like *Rangla-Rangli* are used to promote social programs like girls' education and 'save the girl-child'. Performers play the trumpet and pray to the Goddess by reciting hymns.

10_Folk Dances of Gujarat
This section highlights the diverse folk dance forms specific to the state of Gujarat.

# Folk Dances of Gujarat

Gujarat has varied dance forms belonging to different communities, performed according to their traditions and customs. These include tribal and folk dances, Garba, Rasa, and many others. Such dances are often seen during festivals, marriages, and fairs.

## Tribal Dances

Tribal dances in Gujarat are performed during festivals, marriages, fairs, and to please deities. Most dances are performed by forming a circle, accompanied by traditional musical instruments like drum, manjira, thali, and dholak, with songs in regional languages.

In the dance form called *Chado*, dancers imitate birds like peacocks, squirrels, and sparrows. Dances like 'Mali No Chaalo' and 'Thakryo Chaalo' are seen in the Dang region. The '*Tippani Dance*' is performed by laborers (*Shramhari*) from Bhil and Koli communities. Dancers perform in a group using thick sticks joined with small sticks struck to the ground.

## Garba

The word *Garba* is derived from 'Garbh deep' (a lamp in a pot). An earthen lamp is placed in a holed earthen pot, which dancers balance on their heads while moving in a circle. Garbas are performed during the Navratri festival (Hindu month of Aso Sud Ekam to Navmi, sometimes up to Dasam or Sharad Poonam) throughout Gujarat. Gujarati people perform and sing Garba to worship and pray to Adhya Shakti (Goddess of power), Mother Jagdamba. It is generally performed in an open ground, encircling a '*Mandvi*' (a small structure) kept in the center, accompanied by handclaps and drum beats.

Gujarati poetry known as *Garba* and *Garbi* are chiefly associated with Krishna Bhakti (devotion to Lord Krishna). Gujarati poet Dayaram wrote poems that inspired ladies to sing colourful and emotional *garbi* expressing love for Lord Krishna.

## Rasa

*Rasa* is a dance performed in a circle along with singing. It is believed that Lord Krishna showed 'Rasa Lila' to the devotee Narsinh Mehta. Rasa is mostly performed in Gujarat during Navratri and Janmashtami festivals. *Dandiya Rasa*, using sticks, is a common type. Some people perform Rasa by placing an earthen pot on their heads. Due to the influence of the Vaishnav sect, Rasa has become more popular. Women wear traditional embroidered chaniya-choli, and men wear embroidered kediya-dhoti while performing Rasa.

11_Other Folk Dances and Conclusion
This section covers additional specific folk dances of Gujarat and concludes the chapter by summarizing the richness and assimilative nature of Indian cultural heritage.

# Other Folk Dances and Conclusion

## Other Dances of Gujarat

*   **Gof Gunthan Dance:** Performed to drumbeats and the rhythm (*taal* and *laya*) of music. Dancers tie a rope to a *mandapa*, pillar, or tree, holding the other end. They dance in a group, holding the rope in one hand and a *dandiya* (stick) in the other. They move in and out of the circle, creating and opening a plait-like formation with the ropes.
*   **Dhamaal Dance of Siddis:** Performed by the Siddi community, originally from Africa, now residing in Jambur village in Gir. They dance rhythmically striking a *Mashira* (coconut shell with lamp inside, covered with cloth), wearing peacock feathers and holding small drums, forming a circle. They dance with high and low pitch sounds of 'Ho-Ho' that echo in the forest, imitating animal and bird voices.
*   **Merayo Dance, Padhar Dance, Koli, and Mer Dances:**
    *   *Merayo Dance* is seen near the stepwell of the Banaskantha region. A *Toran* is woven from tall grass (*reed* and *Jhunjhadi*). This dance is performed to drumbeats and involves sword tricks.
    *   In the Surendranagar region, people of the *Padhaar* community dance with the rhythm of *dandiya* and *manjira*. Their movements create scenes resembling sea waves or a ship moving on waves.
    *   *Kolis* of Saurashtra perform *Koli dance*, wearing a *madhrasio* (colourful cloth for the head), an embroidered and mirrored turban, and a green embroidered belt. They wear a '*Bhet*' on their waist.
    *   Similarly, *Mer dance* of Saurashtra and other dances of the *Bharwad* tribe are well-known.

As discussed throughout the chapter, Indian culture is universal and assimilative. The arrival of various tribes throughout history has led to significant growth and development in handicrafts, home artistry, painting, dancing, and other fine arts. This demonstrates the rich and varied heritage of India, leaving a permanent impression on the world.
