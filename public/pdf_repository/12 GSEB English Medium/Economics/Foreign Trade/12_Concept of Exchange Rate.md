12_Concept of Exchange Rate

# Introduction

When an Indian tourist visits a foreign country, they may not be able to purchase goods there using Indian Rupees. They need the currency of that country. Likewise, when an Indian importer imports goods from a foreign country, they will have to make payment in that particular country's currency or in an internationally acceptable currency. These are examples of India's demand for foreign currency.

Similarly, foreigners may demand India's currency.

Therefore, such tourists or traders approach banks or officially registered currency traders to convert their domestic currency into a foreign or internationally acceptable currency. Such conversion of currency is done at a specific rate at a specific time which is known as the exchange rate. The exchange rate is the price of a foreign currency in terms of domestic currency. In other words, it is the units of home currency required to buy one unit of a foreign currency.

# Definition of Exchange Rate

## Key Definitions

- The rate at which the currency of one country can be converted into the currency of another country is called the exchange rate.
- The exchange rate is the price of a foreign currency in terms of domestic currency.

## Explanation

For a particular country, the exchange rate is the price of one unit of a foreign currency in units of home currency; in other words, it is the units of home currency required to buy one unit of a foreign currency.

For example, an exchange rate of US $\$ 1 = ₹60$ implies that in order to buy $\$1$, a price of ₹60 must be paid.

# Impact of Exchange Rate Fluctuations

## Rise in Exchange Rate

A rise in the exchange rate for India means that the value of Indian currency has declined in the international market. More Indian ₹ will have to be paid to buy one unit of a foreign currency. It implies that the foreign currency becomes expensive and hence the value of Indian ₹ falls.

Initially, if US $\$ 1 = ₹60$ and the exchange rate rises for India, then US $\$ 1 = ₹65$.

## Fall in Exchange Rate

On the other hand, when the exchange rate falls for India, the value of the Indian ₹ rises.
(However, in the actual analysis of a rise or fall in the value of a currency, the time gap between the rise or fall in value of the currency, prices of goods in the various countries, etc., are taken into consideration.)

## Influencing Factors

Sometimes exchange rates change owing to open market forces, and sometimes the government of a country may alter the exchange rate to impact imports and exports.

# Impact on Imports and Exports

## Effect of Rising Exchange Rate

If the exchange rate rises for India and the value of ₹ falls, the demand for imports by India tends to decline, and India's exports tend to rise.

When the price of one US \$ rises from ₹60 to ₹65, an Indian importer has to pay ₹65 now instead of the earlier ₹60 to import a commodity worth US \$1. Hence, imports tend to fall.

While earlier by spending US \$1, a foreign trader could purchase goods worth ₹60, now they can buy goods worth ₹65. Hence, exports from India tend to rise.

## Effect of Falling Exchange Rate

The reverse happens when the exchange rate for India falls.
