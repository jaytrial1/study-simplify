4_Causes of Low Productivity of Agriculture

India is an agriculture oriented nation, but it also has many agriculture related problems. One of the major problems is low agricultural productivity. In reality, agricultural productivity, whether measured in terms of productivity per hectare or income per hectare, is very low compared to the rest of the world. The responsible factors for this are as follows:

# Institutional Factors

The influence of farmers working within institutions, felt in physical, social, economic, and legal aspects, are considered institutional factors. In India, these factors are often negative, leading to slow or minimal agricultural development and, consequently, low agricultural productivity.

Institutional factors include land revenue collection systems like the Zamindari, Mahalwari, and Ryotwari systems (implemented by the British), as well as agricultural finance, agricultural marketing systems, and land ownership systems. These institutional factors often remain resistant or negative, contributing to low agricultural productivity.

## Land Revenue Collection Systems

Three revenue collection systems - Zamindari, Mahalwari, and Ryotwari - were prevalent during India's independence. Tenants and landless laborers cultivated the land under these systems. Landlords compulsorily took a large share of the crop (leaving farmers with only subsistence levels) as land rent. This discouraged farmers from increasing productivity, as they lacked the incentive to increase production or implement innovations, resulting in stagnant agricultural production. Consequently, agricultural production did not increase according to the nation's or the cultivators' requirements.

After independence, the "Tillers Land" act was passed to protect tillers and control land rent. However, incomplete implementation meant that farmers were not entirely free from exploitation, and the productivity of the agricultural sector became a serious issue.

## Agriculture Finance

The majority of farmers in India face poverty and require agricultural finance. They need funds to buy fertilizers, seeds, pesticides, etc., for cultivation. Since independence, private money lenders have played a significant role. In 1951, they managed 71.6% of agricultural finance, providing it at very high interest rates and manipulating accounts to exploit poor farmers.

After independence, the government nationalized banks, reducing the importance of money lenders. The government expanded institutional agricultural finance by establishing Regional Rural Banks in 1975 and the National Bank for Agriculture and Rural Development (NABARD) in 1982. These efforts reduced the role of money lenders to 27%, with institutions managing the remaining agricultural credit. However, farmers still face high credit costs, leading to low revenue (profitability). With low profits, farmers are less motivated to improve productivity or engage in agriculture-oriented work, resulting in low agricultural productivity.

## Agriculture Marketing

Weak infrastructure in remote Indian villages results in inadequate road and transportation facilities to connect them to agricultural markets. Agricultural markets also exhibit varying produce rates at the beginning and end of the season. In most cases, high price benefits accrue to traders and hoarders. Farmers, often indebted, are forced to sell their crops to local lenders and brokers before they are ready. They lack market knowledge, knowledge of market rates, and understanding of selling procedures, preventing them from getting good returns on their crops and causing them to become pessimistic.

## Rural Social Structure

Indian farmers are often fatalistic and possess limited information. Rural society is bound by old traditions and structures. Being fatalistic, they accept problems from nature and situations of scarcity. They cultivate only for subsistence and lack the motivation to achieve economic development, cultivate more efficiently, or increase their income. This is why agriculture, the central point of rural areas, experiences low productivity.

# Technological Factors

The Indian agricultural sector utilizes old, traditional technologies, equipment, ideologies, and techniques, weakening it. Even today, farmers use plows and bullocks instead of tractors. They use traditional seeds instead of hybrid seeds, resulting in low productivity. They use cow dung as manure instead of chemical-based fertilizers, further impacting productivity. Indian farmers also use limited amounts of pesticides and newer technologies to protect their crops. Consequently, the Indian agricultural sector progresses very slowly.

# Other Factors

## Pressure of Population

One of the main reasons for the low productivity of the agricultural sector is population pressure. This pressure can be understood by examining the dependency on agriculture for employment. At the time of independence, 72% of the population was engaged in agriculture. This reduced to 58% in 2001-02, and further to 49% in 2013-14. Thus, the burden on agriculture has reduced, but it is still higher than other sectors and compared to economies abroad. Here, the total produce of agriculture is distributed among a large population based on production or income, indicating low labor productivity.

## Lack of Economic Planning

The Government of India dedicated the first five-year plan exclusively to the agricultural sector. However, from the second five-year plan (1956) onward, India's economic planning focused on industries. The government has not allocated as much effort, time, and money to the agricultural sector compared to the industrial sector. Therefore, it can be said that as the agricultural sector experiences irregular and slow development, the government has not provided adequate support, resulting in the worrisome condition of the Indian agricultural sector.
